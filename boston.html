Boston

Sixteen schools, which is more than any metro on this board except Greenville, and twelve of them are Division 3. The usual problem is inverted here. The running works at the top — three target-tier programs, more than any new metro in this pass — but those three are MIT, Harvard and Tufts, which admit 5%, 4% and 11% of applicants. Below them the depth falls away fast: he would arrive as the #1 man at ten of the sixteen, and five never finished seven runners in a championship at all. In Boston this is an admissions decision wearing a running decision's clothes.

MIT is the strongest single row in this pass: 35.2% of its bachelor's degrees are in computer science — the highest share on the whole board — and across the NEWMAC championship, the Division 3 East regional and Division 3 nationals he lands as their 6th man, 22 seconds inside their seven. Admit rate 5%, average net price $20,111.

Harvard measures almost identically to its seventh man — six seconds inside it across the Ivy League championship, the Division 1 Northeast regional and the national championship — at a 4% admit rate and $19,066, the lowest net price in range. Tufts is the third: four championships on file including Division 3 nationals, 15 seconds inside their seventh, 11% admit, and $39,998, which is nearly the most expensive row here. Need-based aid is why the two hardest admissions in the metro are also its two cheapest rows.

The two deep rows are the near misses, and both are Division 1: Northeastern is 8 seconds outside its seventh man across the IC4A, the CAA championship and the Division 1 regional, and Boston College is 31 outside in the ACC. Neither is out of reach in two years; both are on the wrong side of the line today.

What caution means in this metro

Eleven of the sixteen rows are caution, and that word is doing real work here: at ten of them he would be the fastest man on the roster on arrival. Five programs never finished seven runners in a 2025 championship — Lesley, UMass Boston, Lasell, Curry and Regis — so there is no seventh man to measure against and the ladder falls back to their last finisher. Lesley is the extreme: he would arrive 184 seconds ahead of their #1.

Wentworth is the row to understand before writing the metro off. It is 23.9% computer science, 2.7 miles from downtown, and admits 91% — the accessible version of Boston CS — but he would arrive as their #2 man, two seconds behind their #1 and 167 seconds inside their seventh. That is a place to be the team rather than join one, and at $34,170 it is not cheap.

The ones worth real effort

  1. MIT — 2.2 miles out, D3 NEWMAC, and the best CS row on the board at 35.2% of degrees. He is their 6th man, 22 seconds inside a seven that spans 73 seconds, measured across three championships including D3 nationals. Net price $20,111 against a 5% admit rate. Division 3, so there is no athletic money at all — the whole conversation is admissions, and a coach's support in that process is the only lever that exists.
  2. Harvard — 5.5 miles out, Ivy League, 10.3% CS, and the cheapest row in the metro at $19,066 on need-based aid. He lands six seconds inside their seventh man averaged over the Ivy championship, the Division 1 Northeast regional and the national championship, which is as close to exactly-the-right-place as this board measures. The Ivy League offers no athletic scholarships either; the admit rate is 4%.
  3. Tufts — 6 miles, NESCAC, 12% CS, four championships on file and 15 seconds inside their seventh. The most plausible of the three on admissions at 11%, and the most expensive at $39,998.

Two more belong here with an asterisk. Northeastern (D1 CAA, 2.5 mi, 16% CS, $30,915) and Boston College (D1 ACC, 7 mi, $41,704) are both deep rather than thin — 8 and 31 seconds outside their sevenths — which means a real training environment where he would not score early. Northeastern's co-op program is a CS-specific reason to take it seriously. Bentley is the metro's only Division 2 program, so it is the only place partial athletic money exists inside 20 miles — and he would arrive 58 seconds ahead of their #1, which is the trade this metro keeps offering.

Thirteen of these sixteen schools cannot offer him a dollar of athletic money. Twelve are Division 3, where athletic aid does not exist by rule, and Harvard's Ivy League does not award it either; the only partial-aid door inside 20 miles is Bentley in Division 2. That makes the metro's $31,033 median net price — the highest of any metro on this board — mostly a question about need-based and merit aid. The two cheapest rows are Harvard at $19,066 and MIT at $20,111, both driven by need-based aid at schools that admit under 6%; the cheapest accessible rows are Emmanuel at $26,706, Regis at $27,477 and Lasell at $27,511, and all three are caution tier. UMass Boston is the only public in range, so it is the only row where the residency question that dominates Chicago and Greenville arises at all — its $39,924 already carries the non-resident premium this board adds by hand.
