New York City

The ring here is 20 driving miles from Midtown, plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance — a shape, not a circle, because Long Island is a commutable corridor and Westchester and central New Jersey are a different decision. Tightening from 50 miles cost this metro ten schools, including one of its three target-tier fits. A Division-1-only rule was then tried on top of that and reversed: it cut 31 schools from this page — every CUNY college, every Division 3 private, the whole cheap end — and the reversal put all of them back, measurements and pages intact. What is on the board is 28 programs: ten Division 1, seven Division 2 and eleven Division 3, every one with a real 2025 cross country result behind its tier. Four are target tier and all four are Division 1; the next-best fit is NYU, at Deep, on his doorstep. The reason to read all 28 rather than the ten is the reason the rule was reversed — at 18 of them he would arrive as their #1, which is a fact about this metro that a divisional filter hides rather than fixes.

The tighter ring costs Fairfield and Monmouth, and both were real

Fairfield (50 mi, Connecticut) was one of three target-tier schools in this metro: he slotted in as their 6th man at the MAAC championship on a tight 59-second seven. Its NCAA regional 10K disagreed — the same comparison made him their #1 — so it was flagged as the metro's highest-value verification. That question is now moot at 20 miles. Monmouth (45 mi, New Jersey) was Deep tier and unambiguous: about 14 seconds outside their seven in the CAA.

Also out: Army West Point (50 mi — free tuition, excellent CS, real distance program, and a congressional nomination plus a five-year service obligation), Sacred Heart (52), and six Westchester and New Jersey schools — Mercy (25), Pace (30), Manhattanville (30), SUNY Purchase (30), Ramapo (25) and Drew (30). Princeton and Rutgers leave the cut list. None of the eight was tiered above Verify, so the honest accounting is: the radius change cost one Target, one Deep, and eight unknowns.

A Division-1-only rule briefly made most of that accounting moot — only Army West Point and Sacred Heart are Division 1 among the eight, since Mercy and Pace are D2 and Manhattanville, SUNY Purchase, Ramapo and Drew are D3 — and with the rule reversed the accounting stands as written. All eight are still out, on the radius rather than on division, and those six D2 and D3 names are now exactly the kind of school this board carries: on the evidence of the 28 inside the ring, most of them would arrive at caution. Fairfield and Monmouth are the two the radius genuinely cost, and if it ever loosens they come straight back.

The CUNY finding: the cheapest computer science degrees in this project, and no training group

The single largest piece of work this metro produced was filling in the CUNY system, and all of it is back on the board now that the divisional rule is gone. Ten CUNY programs came into range: Hunter, Brooklyn, City College, Lehman, John Jay, York, Medgar Evers, Baruch, Queens College and, at Division 2, the College of Staten Island. Three failed the both-sports rule first (Baruch, Brooklyn and John Jay field cross country and no men's outdoor track). The remaining seven each sponsor both sports, each grant a bachelor's in computer science, and each report a federal average net price under $6,000 — the cheapest combination of those three things anywhere in this project.

And all seven were measured, and the running answer is brutal. At every one of them he would be the team's #1 by minutes, not seconds. At Hunter's CUNYAC championship his projection is nine minutes inside their 7th man; at Lehman it is thirty-one minutes. Their 1–7 spreads — 384 seconds at Hunter, 1,150 at Lehman — say so more clearly than any tier could. The 1500 says it a second time: at Hunter, Lehman, Medgar Evers, City College, Queens College and Staten Island he would be their fastest 1500 man too, and York College put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all year. So the cheap-CUNY finding holds on money and inverts on running: $3,000 a year buys a computer science degree and no training group. That is a real option for someone who would train alone by choice. It is the opposite of what this board is looking for.

Which is the point. The Division-1-only rule removed all seven, and it removed nothing the framing rule had not already removed — they were caution tier on measurement, not on division. That is the case for the rule and against it in one sentence: on this page it deleted the right schools for the wrong reason. Now that they are back, read this paragraph before reading the price, because the price was never the problem. Two caveats belong with those net prices anyway. They are averages over a student body that is overwhelmingly in-state and living at home; a South Carolina applicant pays about $15,300 at CUNY, and most of these campuses have no dormitories at all, so housing means renting in New York City and that cost appears nowhere in the federal file (how residency enters the net cost).

Four schools were on this board in error and have no men's cross country

The other three — NYIT (D2), Montclair State (D3) and William Paterson (D3) — are the same error and are listed in the no men's cross country table below, where their rows say "no men's cross country team" rather than a tier. All three were carrying a Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looks like a program awaiting measurement rather than a program that does not exist.

St. John's is the more embarrassing one, and it is the one that is still Division 1 — it was carried as a cut, which asserted a men's team existed and was too strong. It does not exist; the program it had been measured against was the women's one. It is kept on the map and in the table below rather than deleted so the correction is visible.

The ones worth real effort

  1. NJIT — the best D1-plus-CS combination in the metro, and now confirmed on depth data: at the America East championship he would have been their 4th man, 91 seconds inside their scoring seven. A genuine tech-school CS program at 33% of its degrees, 65% admit. The value case is weaker than it looked: $16,504 is New Jersey's resident net price, and on his residency it is about $34,194 — roughly $10,000 under Fordham rather than less than half of it. The one caveat is the same shape as Davidson's: he would be filling the middle of their seven rather than joining a pack, and they took only four runners to the Mid-Atlantic regional.
  2. Seton Hall — the open question this round closed. It was target tier on a 14:58 track mark alone, and the Big East result agrees: their 5th man, 22 seconds inside their seven on an 85-second spread, and their 4th at the Mid-Atlantic regional. Big East distance running at 18 miles from Midtown, with a $31,446 net price that a strong student's merit aid moves.
  3. Fordham — still the best overall fit in the metro. At the Atlantic 10 championship he would have been their 6th man, 19 seconds inside their scoring seven, with a healthy 95-second 1–7 spread. Add a real CS department and Van Cortlandt Park — the most historic cross country course in America — directly next to campus. It answers the "where do you actually run in New York" problem better than anything else on the list. The catch is money: a $44,338 average net price, the second highest in this metro.
  4. Columbia — back on the board after being cut on a 13:43 team best. At Ivy Heps he would have been their #5, 73 seconds inside their seven; at the Northeast regional he slips to 8th, so read the tier as the softer edge of target rather than its middle. World-class CS, 5 miles from campus to Midtown, and a $21,590 average net price that is far below its sticker. The gate is admissions, and a coach's support is the thing that moves it.
  5. NYU — Deep, Division 3, and at zero miles. NYU won the UAA championship with seven runners inside 42 seconds and his projection lands about 28 seconds off the back of that seven: a real program he would train hard with and not travel for as a freshman, attached to as strong a computer science department as anything on this page. Two gates, and neither is running. The admit rate is 9%, and Division 3 offers no athletic money at all, so a coach's support is the only lever on either. Its $37,050 average net price is the sixth highest of the 28.
  6. Stony Brook — Deep rather than target: 3 seconds outside their seven on average, their 8th man at the CAA meet and their 7th at the regional. That is a real program he would train with and travel for by sophomore year, the strongest CS department on Long Island, and the worst residency penalty in this metro: about $40,594 net on his residency, against $18,784 for a New Yorker. It is 60 miles out and only on this board because of the Long Island rule, so treat the commute honestly.

That is six of the 28. The remaining 22 are all at caution, and 18 of them are caution for the same reason: he would arrive at or in front of their #1. Among the Division 1 five, Saint Peter's is the clearest case and the cheapest private on this board at $12,199, which is exactly the trap this project is built to catch: dead level with their fastest man on a 212-second spread across seven. LIU, Manhattan, Wagner and Fairleigh Dickinson are the others. The seventeen D2 and D3 caution rows are worse, not better — at Lehman he is thirty-one minutes inside the 7th man — and they are on the board because a page that hides them cannot explain why the cheap options are cheap.

Two names have come off this list, and neither for a divisional reason. Hunter College was "the new entry to email first" on the strength of being cheap, close and unmeasured; its CUNYAC result puts him nine minutes inside their 7th man, so the odds that he is inside their seven turned out to be 100% and worthless. Stevens Institute was here for its CS department, and at the MAC championship he would have arrived as their #1, 51 seconds ahead of their fastest man; their NCAA regional run puts him at their #3, so the board carries the average, #2. Both schools are still here, at caution, with their pages intact — measurement is what moved them, not division.

Hofstra is cut, and it was one of this page's two headline recommendations

An outdoor 5000 of 14:32 suggested a clean 40-second gap, and this page called Hofstra "clean on both axes." They then won the CAA championship with a top seven of 23:02 to 24:45 — he would have finished roughly 75 seconds outside their scoring seven. That is walk-on territory by the same rule that cut Furman and Iona. The 14:32 was a floor, not a measure of the squad, and the reversal is stated here rather than quietly edited out. Hofstra is in Hempstead, so it stays on the board under the Long Island rule — as a cut.

Wagner sits at caution for a softer reason: only six runners finished the NEC championship, their front two are respectable, and their 4th man ran 28:59 — a 359-second spread across six, with no 7th man to compare against at all. Something is wrong with either the squad or the result. Worth one email, not five. It used to carry Verify, which was wrong twice over: the race had been found, and the finding was bad news rather than missing news.

NYU: too good, not too thin — and the school the Division 1 rule cost this metro

NYU won the UAA championship with seven runners inside 42 seconds; his projection lands about 28 seconds off the back of that. That is Deep tier: a real program where he would train hard and not travel as a freshman, on his doorstep, with as strong a computer science department as anything on this page. The 9% admit rate is the gate, and since Division 3 offers no athletic money at all, a coach's support is the only thing that moves it. It is the fifth name on the list above, and the only one of the six that is not Division 1.

It is Division 3, which is why it spent a version of this board archived. Of the 31 schools the divisional rule removed from this metro, NYU was the only one better than caution tier — so the honest cost of the rule in New York was one school, and what it cost there was academic and geographic rather than a scholarship, since D3 has no scholarship to offer. That was the whole case against the rule here, and the rule is now gone: it is written up in the methodology with what it cost everywhere else.

Ten schools here sponsor cross country but not men's track — and every one of them is Division 3

An earlier version of this page listed five of these as a caution to raise with a coach. It is a removal rule now, and the full count in this metro is ten: they field men's cross country with no men's outdoor track and field, so they cannot give him both seasons. That is more than a third of what this metro used to hold, and it is the pattern behind the number — small private and CUNY colleges in New York routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop there. Each was checked twice: the sport list the school publishes itself, and TFRRS for any 2026 men's outdoor mark at any distance. SUNY Maritime carries a second constraint besides — a regiment and summer sea terms, which is a different life, not a detail — and the Merchant Marine Academy, which does sponsor both sports and is on the board, carries the same one. The rule, written out.

All ten are Division 3, which is itself the finding: no Division 1 or Division 2 school in this metro sponsors cross country without men's track. All ten were measured before they were removed, and the measurements say the same thing the tier column does — he would be the #1 runner at every one of them. They keep their pages and their pins, because this rule is one line of data away from reversal if a school adds the sport.

Eight programs here never finished seven runners, and one of them never finished five

The depth number this board is built on does not exist at 8 of the 28 schools. LIU, Manhattan and Wagner in Division 1, plus CCNY and Molloy, each got six men to the finish of their conference championship and no more; Caldwell and Medgar Evers managed five; York College managed four, which is below the five a team needs to score at all. On those eight pages there is no 7th man to compare him against, so the comparison falls back to the last finisher and the vs their 7th column is blank. All eight are already at caution on other evidence, so nothing here changes a tier — but the three Division 1 names are the ones to notice, because a missing 7th man in the NEC or the MAAC is a different problem than a missing 7th man in the CUNYAC.

Count the ten schools that sponsor cross country without men's track and it is thirteen of 38. Mount Saint Vincent also finished four and John Jay finished three; both are off the board on the both-sports rule before the depth number even matters. York College is the one that stays, and it also put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all season. No price makes a roster like that work, and York's $4,456 federal net price — about $12,406 at the rate he would actually pay — is the cheapest illustration on the site of why the cheap end of this metro was never the answer.

The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Across all 28 schools it says the same thing the cross country data says, in a different event: he is competitive at nearly every one of them, and too good at two thirds.

He would have made 27 of the 28 conference finals, and finished top eight in 21. The single exception is NJIT, the metro's best D1-plus-CS value and a target on cross country: America East took twelve men into its final and the slowest of them ran 3:55.8, so he would have missed it by five seconds. Nothing about that contradicts the cross country read; it is the same program measured in a different event, and it is the most useful thing on NJIT's page to raise with the coach. The 21 top-eight finishes need reading down, though, because in five of the fifteen conferences on this page his 4:01 would have won the final outright — the CUNYAC (won in 4:28.4), the ECC (4:02.6), the CACC (4:04.7) and Skyline (4:13.1), which is 13 schools and a depth problem rather than a result, plus the Atlantic 10, where the final was genuinely fast and still won in 4:03.6 off a slow first 800. A tactical final is a placing, not a time, and every school page says so where it happens.

The thinness is the finding, and it is the same finding as the cross country table. He would be the fastest 1500 man on the roster at 18 of the 27 programs with a mark on file, and the median team best across those 27 is 4:07.8 — nearly seven seconds slower than his projection. York College put nobody in a 1500 all season, the only school here with no mark at all, and four more entered nobody at their conference meet: Seton Hall, Fairleigh Dickinson, Caldwell and St. Thomas Aquinas. Seton Hall is the one worth a question: it is target tier on depth and entered nobody in the 1500 at the Big East championship, and its season best of 4:01.3 is three-tenths off his projection — a Big East program whose distance men race the 800 and the 5000. The healthy answers are all deep rosters, and there are only four: Columbia, where he is #9 of the 12 men who ran one; NYU, #8 of 14; Stony Brook, #8 of 13; and Fordham, #5 of 15. Those are packs to sit in rather than squads to lead. NJIT is #5 of only five and LIU #5 of seven, which is the same slot on a roster a third the size. Six programs took a 1500 runner past the conference meet: Fordham, Wagner, LIU and Saint Peter's to the IC4A/ECAC championships, Kean and Rutgers–Newark to the ECAC Division 3 meet. Three things specific to New York

Cost of living is the dominant variable, and putting all thirteen publics on his residency is what makes the cheap end legible. At the rate he would actually pay, net price on this board runs from $6,174 at the Merchant Marine Academy — a federal service academy that charges $895 to everyone and grants no computer science degree, so it is the cheapest row and not an option — to $44,338 at Fordham, with a median of about $22,969. The genuinely cheap end is CUNY: Hunter about $10,934, Lehman $11,098, CCNY $11,726, Queens $12,145, York $12,406, every one of them a squad he would lead from his first race. Saint Peter's at $12,199 is the cheapest private and the clearest trap on the board. Then a gap to Columbia at $21,590, which is the one genuinely surprising number in the metro: a 4% admit rate attached to a net price under the median, because its aid is need-based and generous. At the top, in order: Fordham $44,338, Stevens Institute $41,346, Stony Brook about $40,594 (against $18,784 for a New Yorker — the worst residency penalty here), Felician $40,045, Rutgers–Newark about $39,215, NYU $37,050 and NJIT about $34,194. Rank the ten Division 1 programs alone and the median is $29,844, which is the cleanest statement of the metro's shape: the cheap half of this page is cheap because it is thin. The gap between the ends is six figures over four years and no partial athletic scholarship in men's distance running comes close to closing it. Two caveats: every figure is an average across all students after grants, not a quote for one applicant, and every ≈ premium is exactly what a non-resident waiver would erase, so ask. Model total cost of attendance, not tuition.

Training terrain is a genuine question to raise with coaches. Ask specifically where the team does long runs and workouts. Good answers: Van Cortlandt Park, Central Park, Prospect Park, the Rockefeller carriage trails, Bear Mountain for hills, Sunken Meadow and Bethpage on Long Island. A bad answer is a hesitation, or "we do a lot on the track." At a commuter school, ask a harder version of the same question: where does the team actually meet, and how many of them get there.

Thirteen publics, two states of in-state tuition, neither of them his. Just under half this board is public and every one of those thirteen will charge out-of-state rates to a South Carolina applicant: seven CUNY colleges (CCNY, College of Staten Island, Hunter, Lehman, Medgar Evers, Queens and York), two SUNY (Stony Brook and Farmingdale State), three in New Jersey (NJIT, Kean and Rutgers–Newark) and the Merchant Marine Academy, which is federal and charges the same $895 either way. The spreads are large: CUNY is about $7,400 in-state against $15,300 out, which is what turns Hunter's $2,984 federal net price into about $10,934; Stony Brook is $10,931 against $32,741, which is what turns $18,784 into about $40,594; NJIT is $19,974 against $37,664 and Rutgers–Newark $17,250 against $36,762. Every one of those figures on this page has the gap added back, marked with a ≈ because it is arithmetic on a federal average rather than a reported figure (method). Ask each of them whether a non-resident waiver exists before ranking any of them on price — on a CUNY campus the answer is worth about $8,000 a year, which is most of the price difference this page is built on.
