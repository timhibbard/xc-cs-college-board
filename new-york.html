Metro 2 of 3 · 20 mile radius + Long Island

New York City

The ring here is 20 driving miles from Midtown, plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance — a shape, not a circle, because Long Island is a commutable corridor and Westchester and central New Jersey are a different decision. Tightening from 50 miles cost this metro ten schools, including one of its three target-tier fits. Filling in the CUNY system and Long Island added sixteen. Every one of the 28 schools here has a 2025 cross country result, which resolved the metro's two open questions in opposite directions: Seton Hall is confirmed as a target on real depth data, and the cheap CUNY colleges are athletically empty. This metro then paid the heaviest price of the three for the both-sports rule: ten of its schools sponsor men's cross country and no men's outdoor track, and all ten are off the board, taking the list from 38 to 28.

The tighter ring costs Fairfield and Monmouth, and both were real

Fairfield (50 mi, Connecticut) was one of three target-tier schools in this metro: he slotted in as their 6th man at the MAAC championship on a tight 59-second seven. Its NCAA regional 10K disagreed — the same comparison made him their #1 — so it was flagged as the metro's highest-value verification. That question is now moot at 20 miles. Monmouth (45 mi, New Jersey) was Deep tier and unambiguous: the same shape as NYU in the CAA, about 21 seconds outside their seven.

Also out: Army West Point (50 mi — free tuition, excellent CS, real distance program, and a congressional nomination plus a five-year service obligation), Sacred Heart (52), and six Westchester and New Jersey schools — Mercy (25), Pace (30), Manhattanville (30), SUNY Purchase (30), Ramapo (25) and Drew (30). Princeton and Rutgers leave the cut list. None of the eight was tiered above Verify, so the honest accounting is: the radius change cost one Target, one Deep, and eight unknowns.

The finding that changes this metro: seven CUNY colleges

Filling in the CUNY system added eight programs to this board — Hunter, Brooklyn, City College, Lehman, John Jay, York, Medgar Evers and, at Division 2, the College of Staten Island. With Baruch and Queens College, which were already here, that was ten. Three of the ten have since come off under the both-sports rule — Baruch, Brooklyn and John Jay sponsor cross country and no men's outdoor track — which leaves seven CUNY colleges in range, every one of them sponsoring both sports, every one granting a bachelor's in computer science, and every one under $6,000 in federal average net price. Read that last figure carefully, though: the federal net price at a public is computed from in-state students only, and CUNY charges a non-resident about $7,950 more in tuition. The cheapest, Hunter, is $2,984 for a New Yorker and about $10,934 for him — still the cheapest school on this board he could realistically attend, and still under $14,000 across all seven, but not in a class of its own (how residency enters the net cost). Hunter awards about 10% of its bachelor's degrees in computer science, a higher share than Fordham, NYU or any D1 on this page except NJIT.

All of them have been measured, and the running answer is brutal. At every one of them he would be the team's #1 by minutes, not seconds. At Hunter's CUNYAC championship his projection is nine minutes inside their 7th man; at Lehman it is thirty-one minutes. The CUNYAC 8K fields are not a distance league in the sense this board uses the word, and their 1–7 spreads — 384 seconds at Hunter, 1,150 at Lehman — say so more clearly than any tier could. The 1500 says it a second time: at Hunter, Lehman, Medgar Evers, City College, Queens College and Staten Island he would be their fastest 1500 man too, and York College put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all year. So the cheap-CUNY finding survives intact on money and inverts on running: $3,000 a year buys a computer science degree and no training group. That is a real option for someone who would train alone by choice. It is the opposite of what this board is looking for.

Read that net price with care, though. It is an average over a student body that is overwhelmingly in-state and living at home. A South Carolina applicant pays the out-of-state rate — about $15,300 at CUNY, still low — and most of these campuses have no dormitories at all, so housing means renting in New York City and that cost appears nowhere in the federal file. The right way to use this: CUNY is the cheapest tuition on the page by a wide margin and the most expensive housing problem on it.

Four schools were on this board in error and have no men's cross country

Three of the four were carrying a Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looks like a program awaiting measurement rather than a program that does not exist. St. John's is the more embarrassing one — it was carried as a cut, which asserted a men's team existed and was too strong. It does not exist. They are kept on the map rather than deleted so the correction is visible.

The ones worth real effort

  1. NJIT — the best D1-plus-CS combination in the metro, and now confirmed on depth data: at the America East championship he would have been their 4th man, 84 seconds inside their scoring seven. A genuine tech-school CS program at 33% of its degrees, 65% admit. The value case is weaker than it looked: $16,504 is New Jersey's resident net price, and on his residency it is about $34,194 — roughly $10,000 under Fordham rather than less than half of it. The one caveat is the same shape as Davidson's: he would be filling the middle of their seven rather than joining a pack, and they took only four runners to the Mid-Atlantic regional.
  2. Seton Hall — the open question this round closed. It was target tier on a 14:58 track mark alone, and the Big East result agrees: their 5th man, 15 seconds inside their seven on an 85-second spread, and 5th again at the Mid-Atlantic regional. Big East distance running at 18 miles from Midtown, with a $31,446 net price that a strong student's merit aid moves.
  3. Fordham — still the best overall fit in the metro. At the Atlantic 10 championship he would have been their 6th man, 12 seconds inside their scoring seven, with a healthy 95-second 1–7 spread. Add a real CS department and Van Cortlandt Park — the most historic cross country course in America — directly next to campus. It answers the "where do you actually run in New York" problem better than anything else on the list. The catch is money: a $44,338 average net price, the second highest in this metro.
  4. Columbia — back on the board after being cut on a 13:43 team best. At Ivy Heps he would have been their #5, 66 seconds inside their seven; at the Northeast regional he slips to 8th, so read the tier as the softer edge of target rather than its middle. World-class CS, 5 miles from campus to Midtown, and a $21,590 average net price that is far below its sticker. The gate is admissions, and a coach's support is the thing that moves it.
  5. Stony Brook — Deep rather than target: 10 seconds outside their seven on average, their 8th man at the CAA meet and their 7th at the regional. That is a real program he would train with and travel for by sophomore year, the strongest CS department on Long Island, and the worst residency penalty in this metro: about $40,594 net on his residency, against $18,784 for a New Yorker. It is 60 miles out and only on this board because of the Long Island rule, so treat the commute honestly.
  6. NYU — also Deep, and the metro's best answer if D3 and admissions leverage appeal. See the callout below.

Two names have come off this list, both because they were measured. Hunter College was "the new entry to email first" on the strength of being cheap, close and unmeasured; its CUNYAC result puts him nine minutes inside their 7th man, so the odds that he is inside their seven turned out to be 100% and worthless. Stevens Institute was here for its CS department, and at the MAC championship he would have arrived as their #1 (their 3rd man at the Metro regional). Stevens remains the right answer if computer science alone decides it — the running argument is gone.

Hofstra is cut, and it was one of this page's two headline recommendations

An outdoor 5000 of 14:32 suggested a clean 40-second gap, and this page called Hofstra "clean on both axes." They then won the CAA championship with a top seven of 23:02 to 24:45 — he would have finished roughly 82 seconds outside their scoring seven. That is walk-on territory by the same rule that cut Furman and Iona. The 14:32 was a floor, not a measure of the squad, and the reversal is stated here rather than quietly edited out. Hofstra is in Hempstead, so it stays on the board under the Long Island rule — as a cut.

Wagner sits at caution for a softer reason: only six runners finished the NEC championship, their front two are respectable, and their 4th man ran 28:59 — a 359-second spread across six, with no 7th man to compare against at all. Something is wrong with either the squad or the result. Worth one email, not five. It used to carry Verify, which was wrong twice over: the race had been found, and the finding was bad news rather than missing news.

NYU: too good, not too thin

NYU won the UAA championship with seven runners inside 42 seconds; his projection lands about 35 seconds off the back of that. It is now Deep rather than a borderline keep, and with Monmouth out of range it is the metro's only Deep-tier school: a real program where he would train hard and not travel as a freshman. D3 means there is no scholarship at stake in aiming high — but the 9% admit rate is the real gate, and a coach's support is what moves it.

Ten schools here sponsor cross country but not men's track, and all ten are off the board

An earlier version of this page listed five of these as a caution to raise with a coach. It is a removal rule now, and the full count is ten: they field men's cross country with no men's outdoor track and field, so they cannot give him both seasons. That is more than a third of what this metro used to hold, and it is the pattern behind the number — small private and CUNY colleges in New York routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop there. Each was checked twice: the sport list the school publishes itself, and TFRRS for any 2026 men's outdoor mark at any distance. Their cross country numbers are kept intact on their own pages, because the numbers are true and the rule is what removed them. SUNY Maritime and the Merchant Marine Academy carry a second constraint besides: a regiment and summer sea terms, which is a different life, not a detail. The rule, written out.

Eight programs here never finished seven runners, and one could not finish five

The depth number this board is built on does not exist at 8 of the 28 schools: they never got seven men to the finish of a championship race in 2025. CCNY, Molloy, LIU, Manhattan, Wagner, Caldwell and Medgar Evers all finished five or six. Worse, York College finished four — below the five a team needs to score at all — and it also put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all season. That is a roster in trouble rather than a team to join, and no price makes it work — $4,456 a year in-state, about $12,406 at his rate. The list used to run to thirteen schools including John Jay at three finishers and Mount Saint Vincent at four; those two left the board under the both-sports rule instead, which is a different reason for the same answer.

The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. New York reads the opposite way from Greenville on this event, in both directions at once.

He would have made every conference final but one. 27 of the 28 championships here took a field his 4:01 would have qualified for, and he would have scored — top eight — at 21 of them. The single exception is NJIT, the metro's best D1-plus-CS value and a target on cross country: America East took twelve men into its final and the slowest of them ran 3:55.8, so he would have missed it by five seconds. Nothing about that contradicts the cross country read; it is the same program measured in a different event, and it is the most useful thing on NJIT's page to raise with the coach.

And the thinness is worse here than in the 8K. At 18 of the 27 programs that ran a 1500 at all he would be their fastest man in the event. That includes Seton Hall, which is target tier on depth and entered nobody in the 1500 at the Big East championship — a Big East program whose distance men race the 800 and the 5000. Fordham is the healthiest 1500 answer in the metro — their #5 on the season depth chart, which is a real pack to sit in rather than a squad to lead. Six programs took a 1500 runner past the conference meet into a postseason round: Fordham, Kean, Rutgers–Newark, Wagner, LIU and Saint Peter's. How the 1500 comparison is built.

Three things specific to New York

Cost of living is the dominant variable, and putting every public on his residency compresses the spread. Net price on the rate he would pay runs from about $10,934 at Hunter to $44,338 at Fordham — the Merchant Marine Academy is lower still at $6,174, but it comes with a service obligation and no CS degree — with Stevens $41,346, Stony Brook about $40,594, Felician $40,045, Rutgers–Newark about $39,215 and NYU $37,050 at the top, and NJIT no longer in the middle at about $34,194. The seven CUNY campuses sit between roughly $10,900 and $13,700 and are the only genuinely cheap options here. The median in this metro moves from $19,244 to $22,969 once the non-resident premium is added, the largest shift of the three metros, because almost everything cheap here is public. (Yeshiva was the most expensive school here at $49,965 and is off the board: no men's track.) The remaining gap is still six figures over four years and no partial athletic scholarship in men's distance running comes close to closing it. Three caveats, pulling in different directions: every figure is an average across all students after grants, not a quote for one applicant; the estimated premium on the publics is exactly what a non-resident waiver would erase, so ask; and the cheapest figures belong to commuter campuses with no dormitories, where his real cost is tuition plus New York rent. Model total cost of attendance, not tuition.

Training terrain is a genuine question to raise with coaches. Ask specifically where the team does long runs and workouts. Good answers: Van Cortlandt Park, Central Park, Prospect Park, the Rockefeller carriage trails, Bear Mountain for hills, Sunken Meadow and Bethpage on Long Island. A bad answer is a hesitation, or "we do a lot on the track." At a commuter school, ask a harder version of the same question: where does the team actually meet, and how many of them get there.

Two states, two sets of in-state tuition, neither of them his. The New Jersey publics (Kean, NJIT, Rutgers–Newark) and the New York publics (the seven CUNYs, Farmingdale, Stony Brook) will all charge out-of-state rates to a South Carolina applicant. The gap is small at CUNY — roughly $7,400 in-state against $15,300 out — and large at Stony Brook, where it is $10,931 against $32,741. Every net price on this page now has that gap added back for the twelve out-of-state publics in this metro, marked with a ≈ because it is arithmetic on a federal average rather than a reported figure (method). Ask each one whether a non-resident waiver exists before ranking any of them on price.
