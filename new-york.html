Metro 2 of 3 · 20 mile radius + Long Island · Division 1

New York City

The ring here is 20 driving miles from Midtown, plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance — a shape, not a circle, because Long Island is a commutable corridor and Westchester and central New Jersey are a different decision. Tightening from 50 miles cost this metro ten schools, including one of its three target-tier fits. Then the board was narrowed to Division 1, and that took 31 more — every CUNY college, every Division 3 private, the whole cheap end of the page. What is left is ten Division 1 programs, four of them at target tier, and every one has a real 2025 cross country result. The narrowing is not a loss of information: the 31 archived schools keep their measurements, their coaches and their own pages, tabled at the bottom of this page, and reading them is the fastest way to understand why this metro looked cheaper and deeper than it is. Of those 31, exactly one was better than caution tier — NYU, at Deep.

The tighter ring costs Fairfield and Monmouth, and both were real

Fairfield (50 mi, Connecticut) was one of three target-tier schools in this metro: he slotted in as their 6th man at the MAAC championship on a tight 59-second seven. Its NCAA regional 10K disagreed — the same comparison made him their #1 — so it was flagged as the metro's highest-value verification. That question is now moot at 20 miles. Monmouth (45 mi, New Jersey) was Deep tier and unambiguous: about 14 seconds outside their seven in the CAA.

Also out: Army West Point (50 mi — free tuition, excellent CS, real distance program, and a congressional nomination plus a five-year service obligation), Sacred Heart (52), and six Westchester and New Jersey schools — Mercy (25), Pace (30), Manhattanville (30), SUNY Purchase (30), Ramapo (25) and Drew (30). Princeton and Rutgers leave the cut list. None of the eight was tiered above Verify, so the honest accounting is: the radius change cost one Target, one Deep, and eight unknowns.

The Division 1 rule has since made most of that accounting moot. Of the eight unknowns, only Army West Point and Sacred Heart are Division 1 — Mercy and Pace are D2, and Manhattanville, SUNY Purchase, Ramapo and Drew are D3, so all six would have been archived at 20 miles or 50. Fairfield and Monmouth are the two that the radius genuinely cost, and both are Division 1, so if the radius ever loosens they come straight back.

The CUNY finding, and why the Division 1 rule costs nothing here

The single largest piece of work this metro produced was filling in the CUNY system, and it is worth reading even though every CUNY college is now archived — none of them is Division 1. Ten CUNY programs came into range: Hunter, Brooklyn, City College, Lehman, John Jay, York, Medgar Evers, Baruch, Queens College and, at Division 2, the College of Staten Island. Three failed the both-sports rule first (Baruch, Brooklyn and John Jay field cross country and no men's outdoor track). The remaining seven each sponsor both sports, each grant a bachelor's in computer science, and each report a federal average net price under $6,000 — the cheapest combination of those three things anywhere in this project.

And all seven were measured, and the running answer is brutal. At every one of them he would be the team's #1 by minutes, not seconds. At Hunter's CUNYAC championship his projection is nine minutes inside their 7th man; at Lehman it is thirty-one minutes. Their 1–7 spreads — 384 seconds at Hunter, 1,150 at Lehman — say so more clearly than any tier could. The 1500 says it a second time: at Hunter, Lehman, Medgar Evers, City College, Queens College and Staten Island he would be their fastest 1500 man too, and York College put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all year. So the cheap-CUNY finding holds on money and inverts on running: $3,000 a year buys a computer science degree and no training group. That is a real option for someone who would train alone by choice. It is the opposite of what this board is looking for.

Which is the point. The Division 1 rule removed all seven, and it removed nothing the framing rule had not already removed — they were caution tier on measurement, not on division. If the divisional rule ever comes off, come back to this paragraph before coming back to the price: the price was never the problem. Two caveats belong with those net prices anyway. They are averages over a student body that is overwhelmingly in-state and living at home; a South Carolina applicant pays about $15,300 at CUNY, and most of these campuses have no dormitories at all, so housing means renting in New York City and that cost appears nowhere in the federal file (how residency enters the net cost).

Four schools were on this board in error and have no men's cross country

The other three — NYIT (D2), Montclair State (D3) and William Paterson (D3) — are the same error and are listed in the Division 1 archive below, where their rows say "no men's cross country team" rather than a tier. All three were carrying a Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looks like a program awaiting measurement rather than a program that does not exist.

St. John's is the more embarrassing one, and it is the one that is still Division 1 — it was carried as a cut, which asserted a men's team existed and was too strong. It does not exist; the program it had been measured against was the women's one. It is kept on the map and in the table below rather than deleted so the correction is visible.

The ones worth real effort

  1. NJIT — the best D1-plus-CS combination in the metro, and now confirmed on depth data: at the America East championship he would have been their 4th man, 91 seconds inside their scoring seven. A genuine tech-school CS program at 33% of its degrees, 65% admit. The value case is weaker than it looked: $16,504 is New Jersey's resident net price, and on his residency it is about $34,194 — roughly $10,000 under Fordham rather than less than half of it. The one caveat is the same shape as Davidson's: he would be filling the middle of their seven rather than joining a pack, and they took only four runners to the Mid-Atlantic regional.
  2. Seton Hall — the open question this round closed. It was target tier on a 14:58 track mark alone, and the Big East result agrees: their 5th man, 22 seconds inside their seven on an 85-second spread, and their 4th at the Mid-Atlantic regional. Big East distance running at 18 miles from Midtown, with a $31,446 net price that a strong student's merit aid moves.
  3. Fordham — still the best overall fit in the metro. At the Atlantic 10 championship he would have been their 6th man, 19 seconds inside their scoring seven, with a healthy 95-second 1–7 spread. Add a real CS department and Van Cortlandt Park — the most historic cross country course in America — directly next to campus. It answers the "where do you actually run in New York" problem better than anything else on the list. The catch is money: a $44,338 average net price, the second highest in this metro.
  4. Columbia — back on the board after being cut on a 13:43 team best. At Ivy Heps he would have been their #5, 73 seconds inside their seven; at the Northeast regional he slips to 8th, so read the tier as the softer edge of target rather than its middle. World-class CS, 5 miles from campus to Midtown, and a $21,590 average net price that is far below its sticker. The gate is admissions, and a coach's support is the thing that moves it.
  5. Stony Brook — Deep rather than target: 3 seconds outside their seven on average, their 8th man at the CAA meet and their 7th at the regional. That is a real program he would train with and travel for by sophomore year, the strongest CS department on Long Island, and the worst residency penalty in this metro: about $40,594 net on his residency, against $18,784 for a New Yorker. It is 60 miles out and only on this board because of the Long Island rule, so treat the commute honestly.

That is five of the ten. The other five — LIU, Saint Peter's, Manhattan, Wagner and Fairleigh Dickinson — are all at caution, and they are all caution for the same reason: he would arrive at or in front of their #1. Saint Peter's is the clearest case and the cheapest school on this board at $12,199, which is exactly the trap this project is built to catch: dead level with their fastest man on a 212-second spread across seven.

Three names have come off this list, and only one of them for a divisional reason. Hunter College was "the new entry to email first" on the strength of being cheap, close and unmeasured; its CUNYAC result puts him nine minutes inside their 7th man, so the odds that he is inside their seven turned out to be 100% and worthless. Stevens Institute was here for its CS department, and at the MAC championship he would have arrived as their #1. Both would be archived as non-D1 anyway, but both were already gone on measurement. NYU is the one that the Division 1 rule actually took — see the callout below.

Hofstra is cut, and it was one of this page's two headline recommendations

An outdoor 5000 of 14:32 suggested a clean 40-second gap, and this page called Hofstra "clean on both axes." They then won the CAA championship with a top seven of 23:02 to 24:45 — he would have finished roughly 75 seconds outside their scoring seven. That is walk-on territory by the same rule that cut Furman and Iona. The 14:32 was a floor, not a measure of the squad, and the reversal is stated here rather than quietly edited out. Hofstra is in Hempstead, so it stays on the board under the Long Island rule — as a cut.

Wagner sits at caution for a softer reason: only six runners finished the NEC championship, their front two are respectable, and their 4th man ran 28:59 — a 359-second spread across six, with no 7th man to compare against at all. Something is wrong with either the squad or the result. Worth one email, not five. It used to carry Verify, which was wrong twice over: the race had been found, and the finding was bad news rather than missing news.

NYU: too good, not too thin — and the one school the Division 1 rule cost this metro

NYU won the UAA championship with seven runners inside 42 seconds; his projection lands about 28 seconds off the back of that. That is Deep tier: a real program where he would train hard and not travel as a freshman, on his doorstep, with as strong a computer science department as anything on this page. The 9% admit rate is the gate, and since Division 3 offers no athletic money at all, a coach's support is the only thing that moves it.

It is Division 3, so it is archived. Of the 31 schools the divisional rule removed from this metro, NYU is the only one that was better than caution tier — which means the honest cost of the rule in New York is one school, and what was lost there was academic and geographic rather than a scholarship. Its measurement, its coach and its page are all still here; the archive is below.

Ten schools here sponsor cross country but not men's track — and every one of them is also not Division 1

An earlier version of this page listed five of these as a caution to raise with a coach. It is a removal rule now, and the full count in this metro is ten: they field men's cross country with no men's outdoor track and field, so they cannot give him both seasons. That is more than a third of what this metro used to hold, and it is the pattern behind the number — small private and CUNY colleges in New York routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop there. Each was checked twice: the sport list the school publishes itself, and TFRRS for any 2026 men's outdoor mark at any distance. SUNY Maritime and the Merchant Marine Academy carry a second constraint besides: a regiment and summer sea terms, which is a different life, not a detail. The rule, written out.

All ten are Division 2 or Division 3, so the board's Division 1 rule reaches them too. They are tabled in the Division 1 archive below rather than here, each marked with the reason that got there first — the table this callout used to hold is now empty, which is itself the finding: no Division 1 school in this metro sponsors cross country without men's track.

Three programs here never finished seven runners — and the Division 1 rule is why it is not thirteen

The depth number this board is built on does not exist at 3 of the 10 Division 1 schools: LIU, Manhattan and Wagner each got only six men to the finish of their conference championship, so there is no 7th man to compare him against and their pages compare him to the last finisher instead. All three are already at caution for a different reason, so nothing here changes on the missing number. None of the ten finished fewer than five, which means every program on this board can at least score.

Across the full all-divisions sweep the count was thirteen of 38. That is the version worth keeping in mind, because it is what the archive is full of: CCNY, Molloy, Caldwell and Medgar Evers at five or six, York College at four and Mount Saint Vincent at four — below the five a team needs to score at all — and John Jay at three. York also put nobody in a 1500 outdoors all season. No price makes a roster like that work, and York's $4,456 in-state net price is the cheapest illustration on the site of why the cheap end of this metro was never the answer.

The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Under the Division 1 rule this reads much closer to Greenville than the all-divisions version did, and the two useful numbers move in opposite directions.

He would have made nine of the ten conference finals, and scored in four. The single exception is NJIT, the metro's best D1-plus-CS value and a target on cross country: America East took twelve men into its final and the slowest of them ran 3:55.8, so he would have missed it by five seconds. Nothing about that contradicts the cross country read; it is the same program measured in a different event, and it is the most useful thing on NJIT's page to raise with the coach. Read the four scoring finishes carefully as well — three are the NEC, where the twelfth man ran 4:13.1, and the fourth is the Atlantic 10, where the final was won in 4:03.6 off a slow first 800. A tactical final is a placing, not a time, and every school page says so where it happens.

The Division 1 rule fixes the thinness that made this section alarming. Across the all-divisions board he would have been the fastest 1500 man at 18 of 28 programs. On this board it is four of ten — Seton Hall, Manhattan, Saint Peter's and Fairleigh Dickinson — and the median team best is 3:49.6 against his 4:01. Seton Hall is the one worth a question: it is target tier on depth and entered nobody in the 1500 at the Big East championship, and its season best of 4:01.3 is three-tenths off his projection — a Big East program whose distance men race the 800 and the 5000. Fordham and NJIT are the healthiest answers, both his #5 on their season depth chart, which is a real pack to sit in rather than a squad to lead; Columbia is deepest, where he is #9 of 12. Five programs took a 1500 runner past the conference meet into a postseason round: Fordham, Columbia, Stony Brook, Wagner and LIU. How the 1500 comparison is built.

Three things specific to New York

Cost of living is the dominant variable, and putting every public on his residency compresses the spread, and the Division 1 rule removed the cheap end outright. On this board, net price at the rate he would pay runs from $12,199 at Saint Peter's to $44,338 at Fordham, and the median is about $29,844 — more than the all-divisions median of $22,969, because everything under $6,000 on the old page was CUNY or Division 3 and is now archived. Below Saint Peter's there is Fairleigh Dickinson at $15,404 and then a gap to Columbia at $21,590, which is the one genuinely surprising number in the metro: a 4% admit rate attached to a net price under the median, because its aid is need-based and generous. At the top: Fordham $44,338, Stony Brook about $40,594 (against $18,784 for a New Yorker — the worst residency penalty here) and NJIT about $34,194, which is roughly $10,000 under Fordham rather than less than half of it. Only two schools on this board are public, so the residency correction moves almost nothing now; the archive is where it mattered. The remaining gap is still six figures over four years and no partial athletic scholarship in men's distance running comes close to closing it. Two caveats: every figure is an average across all students after grants, not a quote for one applicant, and the estimated premium on NJIT and Stony Brook is exactly what a non-resident waiver would erase, so ask. Model total cost of attendance, not tuition.

Training terrain is a genuine question to raise with coaches. Ask specifically where the team does long runs and workouts. Good answers: Van Cortlandt Park, Central Park, Prospect Park, the Rockefeller carriage trails, Bear Mountain for hills, Sunken Meadow and Bethpage on Long Island. A bad answer is a hesitation, or "we do a lot on the track." At a commuter school, ask a harder version of the same question: where does the team actually meet, and how many of them get there.

Two states, two sets of in-state tuition, neither of them his. The Division 1 board here has exactly two publics: NJIT in New Jersey and Stony Brook in New York, and both will charge out-of-state rates to a South Carolina applicant. NJIT is $19,974 in-state against $37,664 out; Stony Brook is $10,931 against $32,741, which is why an $18,784 federal net price becomes about $40,594 for him. Both figures on this page have that gap added back, marked with a ≈ because it is arithmetic on a federal average rather than a reported figure (method). Ask both whether a non-resident waiver exists before ranking either on price. The wider archive is where this correction really bit — twelve out-of-state publics, and it is what turned CUNY's $3,000 into $15,300.
