Greenville, South Carolina

The deepest and best list of the three metros, and the one the cross country data improved: 22 schools here have him finishing inside their scoring seven, more than four times the other two metros combined, and the closest of them is 25 miles from home. That number was nine until every school on the page got measured. It also lost the most to the walk-on cut, because the closest school with the best CS department in the region is a nationally elite distance program. Widening the ring from 200 to 250 miles more than tripled this list — from 27 schools to 81 — but almost none of that growth came from the extra fifty miles. It came from admitting that the original list was incomplete. The list then came back down to 77, because four of those schools sponsor cross country without men's outdoor track and he wants to run both.

The real correction here is not the extra fifty miles — it is that this list was incomplete

Checking the ring properly, conference by conference, turned up roughly 40 schools that were inside the original 200 miles all along and had simply never been found: most of Conference Carolinas, most of the SIAC and the CIAA, the Peach Belt, the USA South, half the South Atlantic Conference. Southern Wesleyan is 25 driving miles away and was not on this page. Adding a school at 230 miles while omitting one at 25 is not defensible, so the fix was to add all of them: every NCAA Division 1, 2 and 3 school inside 250 driving miles that sponsors men's cross country, plus one non-NCAA program close to home (Montreat, NAIA, at 70 miles).

That takes the list from 27 schools to 81 — and then back down to 77, once the both-sports rule below removed four cross-country-only programs. The README's earlier claim that this board listed every school in range was false when it was written. It is closer to true now. Two known gaps remain, and both are named in the methodology: the NAIA and NCCAA programs in this footprint are not systematically covered, and sponsorship was read off conference tables, so a school whose conference table is stale can still be wrong in either direction.

And it changed the ranking, once the additions were measured. They entered at Verify — full federal cost, admissions and CS data, no running data at all — which was a placeholder rather than a judgement. Every one of them now has a 2025 championship result, and the target tier went from nine schools to 23 — 22 after the both-sports rule took Mercer off the board. The single best illustration is the one that was always closest: Southern Wesleyan, 25 driving miles from home, where he would have been their 4th man at Conference Carolinas, 105 seconds inside their seven. It was not on this page at all two versions ago.

Georgia State was on this board in error — there is no men's program

It sat at Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looked like a program awaiting measurement rather than one that does not exist. It is kept on the map, greyed, rather than deleted, so the correction stays visible.

The six that matter

Every ranking below is anchored to a 2025 cross country result rather than to one outdoor 5000 mark, so each one names where he would actually have finished in that team's seven. This list did change when the 55 additions were measured: the school 25 miles from the house went from unmeasured to the closest target on the board, and the cheapest target now costs $7,892 a year. Each of the six also carries its 2026 conference 1500 field on its own page; where that field contradicts the cross country read, it is flagged below.

  1. Southern Wesleyan — 25 driving miles, D2 Conference Carolinas, a verified CS degree, a 100% admit rate and $15,464 net. He would have been their 4th man at the conference championship, 105 seconds inside their scoring seven on a tight 116-second spread. Two cautions, both worth an email rather than a veto: 747 undergraduates is a small training group, and they took only four runners to the D2 regional two weeks later.
  2. UNC Asheville — 62 miles, Big South, mountain terrain, a $12,250 average net price and a 92% admit rate. He slots in as their 6th man at the Big South, 50 seconds inside their scoring seven, and as their 5th at the regional, where only six of them finished. Still the best combination of proximity, athletic fit, admissions ease and price among the D1 options.
  3. Davidson — their 4th man, and coach support is real admissions leverage at a 13% admit rate. The highest-value application on the list: running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not. The caveat is their own shape — a big gap between their #1 and their 4th through 7th, so he would be filling a hole rather than joining a pack.
  4. UNC Charlotte — the boundary case the data resolved, and it resolved upward. He lands essentially level with their 7th man (11 seconds inside) on a soft 167-second 1–7 spread. 100 miles, D1 American, 80% admit, $15,435 net, and one of the larger CS departments on the board.
  5. Emory — the biggest single correction in this metro. Its 15:06 outdoor 5000 was a sparse-table artifact; on the UAA result he is their 5th man, not their #1. Elite CS, D3, no athletic money but strong need-based aid, and an 11% admit rate that a coach's support genuinely moves.
  6. Fayetteville State — the cheapest target-tier school anywhere on this board at $7,892 net, D2 CIAA, verified CS, 82% admit. They won the CIAA championship and he would have been their 3rd man there and their 6th at the Atlantic regional. Read the 250-second spread and the six-man regional squad as what they are: a front-loaded team with a thin back half, which is exactly where a 5th-to-7th man is useful. It is also 235 miles away, and its staff directory publishes no email — call the office.

Just behind them: UNC Greensboro ($10,965 net, 4th-to-5th man, the cheapest D1 target), Berry College (3rd-to-4th man at both the SAA meet and the D3 South regional), Catawba and UNC Pembroke for the D2 scholarship argument, and Campbell and Coastal Carolina at the far edge of the ring. Read ETSU's 171-second spread as a warning though: a team that strung out that badly is not a pack. Mercer used to be named here and is gone — it sponsors men's cross country and no men's outdoor track, which is the single most expensive line in this whole project.

One target has to be flagged: Queens (Charlotte) has no computer science degree. It earns the tier on running — their 5th man, 15 seconds inside their seven, on the tightest spread in the metro at 56 seconds — and it fails the other half of the search. It is the only school in the target band anywhere on the board without a CS program.

The two boundary cases are settled, and they split

UNC Charlotte moves up. Their seven at the American championship ran within 11 seconds of his projection at the back, so he is in the travel squad as a freshman. That is a target.

Appalachian State moves down to Deep. Their 7th man is about 35 seconds faster than his projection — a strong Sun Belt program he would train with but not travel for in year one. Not a cut, and not a target either: worth an email that asks specifically what their incoming distance class looks like.

Anderson (SC) also drops to Deep, and it is the most carefully checked school on the board. At 30 miles it was the closest target-tier school here — Southern Wesleyan has since taken that title at 25 — so it got the most scrutiny: their South Atlantic Conference course ran about 96 seconds slow at 8K (Anderson, Catawba and Wingate each confirm that against the D2 regional), and once corrected he sits within two seconds of their 7th man — the closest call anywhere in this project. Two seconds is not a distinction; treat Anderson as a real option and ask the coach.

Lenoir-Rhyne is withdrawn from the top picks

An earlier version of this page grouped Lenoir-Rhyne with Catawba and Anderson as a healthy D2 fit. Their cross country result does not support it: their scoring six spanned 326 seconds at the D2 Southeast regional, and he would arrive as their #1. That is the thin-squad pattern this whole board is built to avoid. Catawba survives the same test — 5th man, 95 seconds inside their seven — so the D2 scholarship argument is still live, just at Catawba and Anderson rather than at Lenoir-Rhyne.

The sharpest trade-off on the board: Wofford

Strong academics, a verified CS degree, 32 miles from home — and a team best 5000 of 15:27 that he would beat the day he arrived. The cross country result now says the same thing more bluntly: at the Southern Conference championship Wofford finished six runners, and his projection would have been their #1 by 56 seconds across a squad that spanned 222 seconds. Being a team's #1 is workable if the coach is building a recruiting class around him. Ask directly who else they are signing, and how many men will be on the roster in the fall. It is bad if he trains alone for four years.

Wofford is not the exception — 23 of the 76 measured programs here never finished seven

Measuring every school turned up a pattern the old page could not see. At 23 of these 76 programs there is no 7th man to compare against: they never got seven runners to the finish of a championship race in 2025. Carson-Newman, Gardner-Webb, Lenoir-Rhyne, Winthrop, Wofford, King University, Newberry, Montreat, Coker, Methodist, Piedmont and Oglethorpe are all in that group, and Benedict finished three. Those pages compare him to the team's last finisher and say so plainly. It is not a data gap; it is the single most useful thing this round of work produced, because every one of those schools would otherwise have looked like a place he could contribute. He can, and there would be nobody to run with.

Cut as walk-on

Worth naming the cost plainly: this removes Furman at 0 miles, the closest school with one of the strongest CS departments in the region. If a walk-on or redshirt path ever becomes acceptable, it returns at the top of the list, not the bottom.

Clemson, at 32 miles, is no longer on this list. It was cut on a 14:36 team-best 5000 alone, and the ACC championship result does not support that: he would have been their 8th man there and their 7th at the Southeast regional, 15 seconds outside their seven on average. That is deep — a program he would train with and not travel for as a freshman — and it is back on the board above.

Four rows on this table used to read "out of range," which was simply wrong — Columbia SC is 105 driving miles from Greenville, Athens 140, Knoxville 145 and Winston-Salem 175, all inside even the old 200-mile ring. South Carolina, Georgia, Tennessee and Wake Forest now carry the real reason, which is level rather than distance. UNC Chapel Hill and Duke join them, having genuinely come into range at 250 miles. Those cuts used to rest on reputation; five of the six have now been measured against their 2025 championship results and the cut held every time — at Tennessee, the softest of them, he would have been their 7th man and still 52 seconds outside it. That is what a confirmed cut looks like, and it is why Clemson's reinstatement is credible rather than wishful. South Carolina is the one row on this page still resting on reputation — no 2025 result has been transcribed for it, and its row says so.

The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Two readings come out of it.

The event is within reach almost everywhere. His 4:01 would have made the conference final at 66 of these 77 championships and scored — top eight — at 35. The eleven where he would have missed the final are the level marker worth knowing by name: ETSU, UNC Greensboro, Appalachian State, Chattanooga, Coastal Carolina, Queens (Charlotte), The Citadel, West Georgia, Western Carolina, Wofford and Clemson. Six of those eleven are target tier on cross country, which is the useful surprise: the 8K and the 1500 are not the same question, and a program can be the right depth fit in the fall and out of reach in May.

And the thinness shows up again. At 33 of the 72 programs here that ran a 1500 at all he would be their fastest man in the event — a warning by the rule this board is built on, not a win. Five programs (Allen University, Bluefield State, Fayetteville State, Johnson C. Smith and Guilford) sponsor men's track and entered nobody in a 1500 outdoors all season, and they are part of a larger group: 23 of the 77 entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference meet. Fayetteville State is the awkward one: cheapest target-tier school on the entire board at $7,892 net, and no 1500 runner to compare him to. That is a question for the coach, not a cut.

Among the six at the top of this page the 1500 reads well: Southern Wesleyan (their #2), UNC Asheville (#6), Davidson (#7), UNC Charlotte (#17), Emory (#8) — all inside a final, and Charlotte's #17 on the season depth list is the healthiest number here, because it means a genuine pack to train in. How big the school is, and how big the town is

Two new columns in the table above, and they only appear on this page: Students (undergraduate enrollment, federal) and Town (the population of the place the campus actually sits in, U.S. Census ACS 2024 5-year). New York and Chicago do not need them. Around Greenville they matter, because the 250-mile ring reaches from Charlotte to towns of six hundred people, and nothing else in the table tells those two apart.

The median school here has 1,819 undergraduates in a town of 27,636, but the median hides the shape of it: 25 of the 77 sit in a place under 10,000 people and 21 sit in one over 100,000. There is not much in between.

Five campuses have more undergraduates than their town has residents — Kennesaw State (41,254 students, Kennesaw's 34,605), Clemson (23,300 / 18,072), Western Carolina (10,024 / 7,973), UNC Pembroke (5,330 / 2,822) and Montreat (762 / 630). App State is near-parity at 19,444 in a Boone of 20,032. In all of them the school is the town, which is a real answer to "what is there to do" and not automatically a bad one for someone whose afternoons are spoken for.

At the other end are the genuinely small places: Montreat (630), Misenheimer NC (659 — and Pfeiffer enrolls 655, so school and town are the same size), Franklin Springs GA (1,108), Due West SC (1,177) and Young Harris GA (1,252). Ask those coaches exactly one question: where does the team run. A 12-mile long run out of a town of six hundred is either trails and gravel, which is ideal, or a state highway with no shoulder, which is not.

Size cuts both ways on the two things that actually decide this. A small college means a small training group — fewer people to chase, which is the whole point of the framing rule — and it means the CS lab-conflict problem below is at its worst. A big state school means depth to chase and multiple lab sections, and it means he is one of forty distance runners competing for a coach's attention rather than one of eight. The target tier spans the entire range: Catawba, 1,235 students in Salisbury, and UNC Charlotte, 24,453 in a city of 903,844, are the same tier and completely different lives.

Neither column moves any tier, and both carry caveats worth knowing before quoting them — Augusta, Macon and Athens are consolidated city-county governments so their numbers are county-wide, four of these places are census-designated rather than incorporated, and a small figure inside Atlanta (Morrow GA, 6,364) describes the address rather than the setting. Read the town column next to the Mi column, never alone. The sources and all four caveats are in §6.

Money and admissions

Recruitment is an admissions lever at the selective schools. Coaches at Davidson and Emory get supported slots and will run a pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach informally whether he would be admitted. Initiate this in the spring of junior year. The federal numbers make the case sharper than the earlier estimates did: Davidson admits 13% and Emory 11%, and he would be inside the scoring seven at both.

Where academics are an asset rather than a constraint: UNC Asheville (92%), Appalachian State (90%), UNC Greensboro (89%), ETSU (86%), and nearly the whole D2 list — all figures now from federal data rather than estimates. Admission is close to automatic, which shifts all the leverage to money. Two estimates on this page were badly wrong before that check: Wofford is 52%, not ~70%, and Lander is 81%, not ~55%.

Academic aid often beats athletic aid. D1 cross country and track is a 12.6-scholarship equivalency spread across a 35–45 man roster — quarter and half rides, not full ones. A strong student stacking merit aid at UNC Asheville, ETSU, High Point, Berry, or Catawba can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer somewhere else.

The wider ring added nine in-state options, which is the cheapest lever available. A South Carolina resident pays in-state tuition at SC State ($11,060), Francis Marion ($11,160), Coastal Carolina ($11,640), The Citadel ($12,570) and the College of Charleston ($12,978), alongside Lander, USC Upstate and Winthrop, which were already here. USC Aiken was on that list and has come off it: no men's outdoor track. Those are sticker tuition figures, not net, and they are lower than the net price of most of the private colleges on this page. It also added 35 new Division 2 programs — three of which have since left under the both-sports rule — which is where partial athletic aid actually lives: D2 allows 12.6 scholarship equivalencies in men's cross country and track, the same as D1, on much smaller rosters.

CS labs collide with practice. Large schools like Charlotte and App State run multiple lab sections; small ones — Wofford, Presbyterian, Berry, Piedmont, North Greenville, Catawba, and nearly every one of the small colleges just added — may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. Ask every coach how many of their athletes major in CS or engineering and how they handle lab conflicts. This question eliminates programs faster than times do — and every school page now carries the coach's name, title, office phone and email, so asking it is a list of messages to send rather than a research project.
