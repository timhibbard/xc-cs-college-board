Greenville, South Carolina

Still the deepest and best list of the three metros, and the one that has changed the most. Widening the ring from 250 to 300 driving miles reached eight Division 1 programs new to the board — three of them join it, three are cut on level and two fail the both-sports rule. A Division-1-only rule was then tried and reversed: it had removed 55 schools from this ring alone, thirteen of them at target tier, which is most of what the rule cost anywhere, and all 55 are back with their measurements intact. The board here is 81 programs — 31 Division 1, 38 Division 2, 11 Division 3 and one NAIA, and 29 of them are target tier: he finishes inside their scoring seven with a real runner still in front of him. That is more than the other two metros hold in total, and every one of the 81 is measured on a 2025 championship result rather than a single track mark. The closest target is Anderson (SC) at 29 driving miles. This metro also still loses the most to the walk-on cut, because the closest school with the best CS department in the region is a nationally elite distance program.

Two rules pulled in opposite directions, and the one that subtracted has been reversed

The search was built the hard way before it was narrowed, and that order matters. Checking the ring conference by conference turned up roughly 40 schools that were inside the original 200 miles all along and had simply never been found: most of Conference Carolinas, most of the SIAC and the CIAA, the Peach Belt, the USA South, half the South Atlantic Conference. Southern Wesleyan is 27 driving miles away and was not on this page. Adding a school at 230 miles while omitting one at 27 is not defensible, so all of them were added — every NCAA D1, D2 and D3 school in range that sponsors men's cross country, plus a handful of NAIA programs. Pushing the ring out to 300 driving miles then reached Auburn, Raleigh, Blacksburg, Birmingham, Cookeville and Richmond, Kentucky, and the same conference-by-conference pass caught two more that had been inside 250 all along: Radford at 250 miles and Jacksonville State at 248.

All eight are now measured, and they split three ways. Three joined the board: Auburn (253 mi) and Samford (296 mi) at target, and Radford (250 mi) at caution, because at the Southeast regional he would have arrived ahead of Radford's #1 — the thin-program pattern, not a fit. Three are cut on level: NC State, Virginia Tech and Eastern Kentucky, the last of which won the ASUN with seven men inside 30 seconds and is the widest gap on the cut list after Wake Forest. And two came off under the both-sports rule: Tennessee Tech, which measured target at their 5th-to-6th man, and Jacksonville State. Two of the eight are fits, one is a caution, and the pass cost about as much as it bought.

Then the board was narrowed to Division 1, and this ring paid for almost all of it — which is why the rule did not survive. Fifty-five schools came off here, against 31 in New York and 10 in Chicago, and all thirteen of the target-tier schools the rule cost anywhere on the board were in this ring. Nothing about the running justified that: the thirteen were target tier on measured championship results, not on division. All 55 are back on the board above, and the rule is written up in the methodology as a decision tried and undone rather than quietly dropped.

Two rows say most clearly what the reversal bought back. Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, where he lands six seconds inside their 7th man on a 77-second spread — the closest call anywhere in this project, and Division 2. And Fayetteville State, the cheapest target-tier school on the entire board at about $11,892 on his residency, also Division 2. Under the divisional rule the nearest target was UNC Asheville at 64 miles and the cheapest was Coastal Carolina at $13,966; both are still here, one place further down each list. That is the trade in four numbers.

Two known gaps remain in the underlying search, and both are named in the methodology: the NAIA and NCCAA programs in this footprint are not systematically covered, which matters again now that every division is on the board — Montreat is the only NAIA row here and there are certainly others — and sponsorship was read off conference tables, so a school whose conference table is stale can still be wrong in either direction.

Georgia State was on this board in error — there is no men's program

It sat at Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looked like a program awaiting measurement rather than one that does not exist. It is kept on the map, greyed, rather than deleted, so the correction stays visible.

Six is a shortlist out of 29 target-tier programs, so the criterion is stated rather than implied: a slot in the middle of the seven, and a #1 who is still comfortably ahead of him, and then proximity, price and the CS degree as tie-breakers. Three of the six are Division 1, two are Division 2 and one is Division 3, which is the shape of this ring rather than a quota. The nearest target is Anderson (SC) at 29 miles, the cheapest is Fayetteville State at about $11,892 on his residency, and the dearest is Auburn at about $46,355. The Division 2 scholarship argument, and why it is back on the board

This callout is about picking the right D2 school, and after a version in which the question was shelved it is live again. Lenoir-Rhyne was withdrawn from an earlier version's top picks on its own result: their scoring six spanned 326 seconds at the D2 Southeast regional and he would arrive as their #1, which is the thin-squad pattern this whole board is built to avoid. Catawba and Anderson survived the same test — 5th-to-6th man and 7th man respectively — which is why both are in the six at the top of this page.

Division 2 is where the money for a distance runner is easiest to find, and this ring is where it is: 38 of the 81 schools on this page are D2, ten of them at target tier, and D2 allows the same 12.6 scholarship equivalencies as D1 across much smaller rosters. That arithmetic is the whole argument — a quarter ride at Catawba or Fayetteville State is a larger share of a smaller squad's money than a quarter ride in the Big South. Ask each of the ten how many equivalencies the men's distance group actually gets, because the allowance is a ceiling and most D2 programs are nowhere near it.

The sharpest trade-off on the board: Wofford

Strong academics, a verified CS degree, 32 miles from home — and a team best 5000 of 15:27 that he would beat the day he arrived. The cross country result now says the same thing more bluntly: at the Southern Conference championship Wofford finished six runners, and his projection would have been their #1 by 56 seconds across a squad that spanned 222 seconds. Being a team's #1 is workable if the coach is building a recruiting class around him. Ask directly who else they are signing, and how many men will be on the roster in the fall. It is bad if he trains alone for four years.

Wofford is not the exception — 25 of the 81 programs here never finished seven

Measuring every school turned up a pattern the old page could not see. 25 of the 81 programs on this board have no 7th man to compare against: they never got seven runners to the finish of a championship race in 2025. Counting the rows that are off the board for other reasons, it is 27 of the 96 measured programs in this ring. Nine finished five or fewer — Winthrop, Allen University, Coker, Johnson C. Smith, LaGrange, Methodist, Savannah State and King University at five, and Benedict at three — and Shorter never fielded a measurable championship squad at all, which is why it is the one verify row left on the board.

Five of the 25 are Division 1 — Winthrop at five finishers, and Gardner-Webb, Wofford, NC Central and Auburn at six — so this is not a small-college problem with a divisional fix, which is one of the reasons the Division 1 rule did not survive. A further 14 programs came up short in one of their two races and fielded a full seven in the other, which is usually a regional entry list rather than a roster problem. All 25 pages compare him to the team's last finisher instead and say so plainly. It is not a data gap; it is the single most useful thing this round of work produced, because every one of those schools would otherwise have looked like a place he could contribute. He can, and there would be nobody to run with.

The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Three readings come out of it, and the first one is a warning about the denominator.

Nearly a third of this ring does not race the metric mile at its own conference meet. 23 of the 81 programs entered nobody in the 1500 at their conference championship, and five entered nobody in it all season — Allen University, Bluefield State, Fayetteville State, Johnson C. Smith and Guilford. Seven of the 23 are Division 1: Elon, Campbell, Chattanooga, College of Charleston, The Citadel, Presbyterian and Wofford. College of Charleston and Fayetteville State are the awkward ones, because both are target tier on cross country and neither gives the 1500 anything to compare against. That is a question for the coach rather than a cut: some distance groups here race the 800 and the 5000 and skip the middle entirely. Every number below is therefore quoted against the 58 programs that actually entered somebody.

He would have made 47 of those 58 conference finals and finished top eight in 26. The eleven misses are the level marker worth knowing by name, and they are mostly the good programs: ETSU, UNC Greensboro, Western Carolina, Appalachian State, Coastal Carolina, Queens (Charlotte), West Georgia, Samford, Auburn, Clemson and Georgia Tech — seven of the eleven are target tier on cross country, which is the useful surprise. The 8K and the 1500 are not the same question, and a program can be the right depth fit in the fall and out of reach in May. Read the 26 scoring finishes down as well: in four conferences — the Atlantic 10, the SIAC, the CIAA and the Collegiate Conference of the South — his 4:01 would have won the final outright, which is ten schools and a depth reading rather than a result.

And the thinness is still the dominant reading, because the median program here has a 1500 man at 3:59.9 and he would be the fastest 1500 runner on the roster at 33 of the 76 programs with a mark on file. Cross country lands on the same count from a completely separate measurement — 33 schools where he would arrive as their #1 — which is the point: two events, two sets of results, one answer. Among the six at the top of this page the 1500 splits cleanly. How big the school is, and how big the town is

Two new columns in the table above, and they only appear on this page: Students (undergraduate enrollment, federal) and Town (the population of the place the campus actually sits in, U.S. Census ACS 2024 5-year). New York and Chicago do not need them. Around Greenville they matter, because the 300-mile ring reaches from Charlotte to towns of six hundred people, and nothing else in the table tells those two apart.

No column moved more when the Division 1 rule was reversed. The median school here has 1,867 undergraduates in a town of 27,829 — against 6,081 in a town of 38,910 on the Division 1 board, because the small-town private colleges are almost all D2 and D3. 25 of the 81 sit in a place under 10,000 people and 22 sit in one over 100,000, which is close to a straight split between two completely different versions of freshman year rather than a distribution with a middle.

Five campuses on the board have more undergraduates than their town has residents — Kennesaw State (41,254 students, Kennesaw's 34,605), Clemson (23,300 / 18,072), Western Carolina (10,024 / 7,973), UNC Pembroke (5,330 / 2,822) and Montreat (762 / 630). App State is near-parity at 19,444 in a Boone of 20,032, and so are Pfeiffer (655 / 659) and North Greenville (1,819 / 1,880) at a thirtieth of the scale. In all of them the school is the town, which is a real answer to "what is there to do" and not automatically a bad one for someone whose afternoons are spoken for.

At the other end, the smallest places on the board are Montreat NC (630) and Misenheimer NC (659, where Pfeiffer is target tier), then Franklin Springs GA (1,108), Due West SC (1,177), Young Harris GA (1,252) and Emory VA (1,419). Ask those coaches exactly one question: where does the team run. A 12-mile long run out of a town of six hundred is either trails and gravel, which is ideal, or a state highway with no shoulder, which is not.

Size cuts both ways on the two things that actually decide this. A small college means a small training group — fewer people to chase, which is the whole point of the framing rule — and it means the CS lab-conflict problem below is at its worst. A big state school means depth to chase and multiple lab sections, and it means he is one of forty distance runners competing for a coach's attention rather than one of eight. The target tier spans the whole range: Pfeiffer, 655 students, and Kennesaw State, 41,254, are the same tier and completely different lives — a factor of 63 between them.

Neither column moves any tier, and both carry caveats worth knowing before quoting them — Augusta, Macon and Athens are consolidated city-county governments so their numbers are county-wide, four of these places are census-designated rather than incorporated, and a small figure inside Atlanta (Morrow GA, 6,364) describes the address rather than the setting. Read the town column next to the Mi column, never alone. The sources and all four caveats are in §6.

Money and admissions

Recruitment is an admissions lever at the selective schools. Coaches at Davidson get supported slots and will run a pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach informally whether he would be admitted. Initiate this in the spring of junior year. The federal numbers make the case sharper than the earlier estimates did: Davidson admits 13% and he would be their 4th man, which is the single highest-value application on this page. Georgia Tech is the harder version of the same lever — a 14% overall admit rate, and a CS admit that is much harder than that, against a deep rather than target running fit. Emory belongs in this paragraph too: an 11% admit rate, a target-tier running fit and Division 3, which means no athletic money to trade but a coach's support that still moves an application.

Where academics are an asset rather than a constraint: 34 of the 81 schools here admit 80% or more — every one of the 81 now has a federal admit rate, and the median across the ring is 75%. Among the fits that means UNC Asheville (92%), UNC Pembroke (93%), Pfeiffer (96%), Appalachian State (90%), UNC Greensboro (89%), Campbell (87%), ETSU (86%), Augusta University (86%), Emory & Henry (84%) and Chattanooga (81%) — all figures from federal data rather than estimates. Admission is close to automatic at those, which shifts all the leverage to money, and it is the D2 and D3 layer that supplies most of them. One estimate on this page was badly wrong before the federal check: Wofford is 52%, not ~70%.

Academic aid often beats athletic aid. D1 cross country and track is a 12.6-scholarship equivalency spread across a 35–45 man roster — quarter and half rides, not full ones, and D2 has the same allowance on a smaller squad. A strong student stacking merit aid at UNC Asheville, ETSU, Catawba, High Point or Elon can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer somewhere else.

In-state tuition is the cheapest lever available, and there are nine of them. A South Carolina resident pays in-state tuition at SC State ($11,060), Francis Marion ($11,160), USC Upstate ($11,588), Coastal Carolina ($11,640), Lander ($11,700), The Citadel ($12,570), the College of Charleston ($12,978), Winthrop ($14,678) and Clemson ($15,554). Those are sticker tuition figures, not net, and they are lower than the net price of most of the private colleges on this page. Only two of the nine — Coastal Carolina and the College of Charleston — are target tier, which is the useful overlap and also the limit of it: the other seven are caution or deep, so in-state tuition on its own has never picked a school here. There are 43 Division 2 programs in this ring counting the ones off the board, 38 of them on it, and D2 allows the same 12.6 scholarship equivalencies as D1 on much smaller rosters. That is where partial athletic aid actually lives.

CS labs collide with practice. Large schools like Charlotte, Kennesaw State and App State run multiple lab sections; small ones — Pfeiffer (655 undergraduates), Tusculum (785), Presbyterian (852), Mars Hill (1,044), Catawba (1,235) and Davidson (1,867) — may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. This is now the sharpest question on the page, because the median school in this ring has 1,867 undergraduates. Ask every coach how many of their athletes major in CS or engineering and how they handle lab conflicts. This question eliminates programs faster than times do — and every school page now carries the coach's name, title, office phone and email, so asking it is a list of messages to send rather than a research project.
