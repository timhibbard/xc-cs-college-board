Metro 1 of 3 · 300 mile radius · Division 1

Greenville, South Carolina

Still the deepest and best list of the three metros, and the one that both gained and lost the most in the last two changes. Widening the ring from 250 to 300 driving miles reached eight Division 1 programs new to the board — three of them join it, three are cut on level and two fail the both-sports rule; narrowing the board to Division 1 removed 55 schools from this ring alone, thirteen of them at target tier. What is left is 16 schools where he finishes inside their scoring seven — more than three times the other two metros combined — every one of them measured on a 2025 championship result rather than a single track mark. The closest is now UNC Asheville at 64 miles; before the Division 1 rule it was Anderson (SC) at 29, and that row is archived rather than deleted, which is the honest way to carry a decision like this. This metro also still loses the most to the walk-on cut, because the closest school with the best CS department in the region is a nationally elite distance program.

Two rules, pulling in opposite directions: 300 miles added schools, Division 1 took away more

The search was built the hard way before it was narrowed, and that order matters. Checking the ring conference by conference turned up roughly 40 schools that were inside the original 200 miles all along and had simply never been found: most of Conference Carolinas, most of the SIAC and the CIAA, the Peach Belt, the USA South, half the South Atlantic Conference. Southern Wesleyan is 27 driving miles away and was not on this page. Adding a school at 230 miles while omitting one at 27 is not defensible, so all of them were added — every NCAA D1, D2 and D3 school in range that sponsors men's cross country, plus a handful of NAIA programs. Pushing the ring out to 300 driving miles then reached Auburn, Raleigh, Blacksburg, Birmingham, Cookeville and Richmond, Kentucky, and the same conference-by-conference pass caught two more that had been inside 250 all along: Radford at 250 miles and Jacksonville State at 248.

All eight are now measured, and they split three ways. Three joined the board: Auburn (253 mi) and Samford (296 mi) at target, and Radford (250 mi) at caution, because at the Southeast regional he would have arrived ahead of Radford's #1 — the thin-program pattern, not a fit. Three are cut on level: NC State, Virginia Tech and Eastern Kentucky, the last of which won the ASUN with seven men inside 30 seconds and is the widest gap on the cut list after Wake Forest. And two came off under the both-sports rule: Tennessee Tech, which measured target at their 5th-to-6th man, and Jacksonville State. Two of the eight are fits, one is a caution, and the pass cost about as much as it bought.

Then the board was narrowed to Division 1, and this ring paid for almost all of it. Fifty-five schools came off here, against 31 in New York and 10 in Chicago, and all thirteen of the target-tier schools the rule cost anywhere on the board were in this ring. None of it is thrown away: every one keeps its measurements, its coach and its own page in the archive at the bottom of this page. Read that table as the price of the rule, not as a list of rejections.

The single most expensive row is the nearest one. Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, where he lands six seconds inside their 7th man on a 66 second spread — the closest call anywhere in this project, and it is Division 2. Fayetteville State was the cheapest target-tier school on the entire board at about $11,892 on his residency, and it is Division 2 as well. On the Division 1 board the nearest target is UNC Asheville at 64 miles and the cheapest is Coastal Carolina at about $13,966, 238 miles out. That is the trade in two numbers.

Two known gaps remain in the underlying search, and both are named in the methodology: the NAIA and NCCAA programs in this footprint are not systematically covered — which no longer affects the board, only the archive — and sponsorship was read off conference tables, so a school whose conference table is stale can still be wrong in either direction.

Georgia State was on this board in error — there is no men's program

It sat at Verify tier, which is the failure mode of a tier that means "no data yet": it looked like a program awaiting measurement rather than one that does not exist. It is kept on the map, greyed, rather than deleted, so the correction stays visible.

The six that matter

Every ranking below is anchored to a 2025 cross country result rather than to one outdoor 5000 mark, so each one names where he would actually have finished in that team's seven. This list has been rewritten twice in quick succession: once when the 55 additions were measured, and again when the board was narrowed to Division 1, which removed four of the previous six outright. The nearest target is now UNC Asheville at 64 miles and the cheapest is Coastal Carolina at about $14,000 on his residency, 238 miles away; the dearest is Auburn at about $46,355, which the 300-mile ring added. Each of the six also carries its 2026 conference 1500 field, its coach and the program's Instagram on its own page; where the 1500 contradicts the cross country read, it is flagged below.

  1. UNC Asheville — 64 driving miles, Big South, mountain terrain, a 92% admit rate, and the cleanest fit on the page by the rule this board is built on: their #1 is 39 seconds faster than he projects, which is the middle of the healthy band, and he lands as their 4th-to-5th man, 57 seconds inside their scoring seven on an 83-second spread. The one entry here the residency correction really hurts: its $12,250 federal net price is what a North Carolinian pays, and on his residency it is about $29,598. Best combination of proximity, athletic fit and admissions ease on the board — but no longer the best on price, which is now a waiver question for coach Adam Puett.
  2. Davidson — 115 miles, Atlantic 10, their 4th man and 48 seconds inside their seven, and coach support is real admissions leverage at a 13% admit rate. The highest-value application on the list: running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not, and at $17,379 it is also the second-cheapest target here. Two caveats, both about shape rather than level — their #1 is 90 seconds ahead of him, and their 1–7 spans 139 seconds, so he would be filling a hole between their front runner and their back half rather than joining a pack. His 4:01 would have won the Atlantic 10 1500 final, which is a tactical-race artifact rather than a level read, and their own 1500 depth chart is faster than that suggests.
  3. Coastal Carolina — the cheapest target on the Division 1 board at $13,966, Sun Belt, 75% admit, verified CS. He slots in as their 3rd-to-4th man, 29 seconds inside their seven on a tight 89-second spread. The thing to ask about is the front of that squad, not the back: their #1 is only 19 seconds faster than he projects, which is the edge of the thin-program pattern this board exists to catch. 238 miles is also the second-longest drive on this list. Coach Michael Bianchina.
  4. College of Charleston — 213 miles, CAA, 60% admit, $18,960, and the most balanced numbers in the target band: their 5th man, 27 seconds inside the seven, their #1 70 seconds ahead of him, and a 97-second 1–7 spread across two races rather than one. Nothing here is an artifact of a single result. Coach Chris Bailey.
  5. ETSU — 122 miles, Southern Conference, 86% admit, about $20,033 on his residency. Their 4th man, 35 seconds inside the seven, and a genuinely strong front — their #1 is 136 seconds faster than he projects. The warning is the same one the old page carried: a 171-second 1–7 spread means the back half of that squad is soft, so the 4th-man slot is real but the pack around it may not be. He would also have missed the SoCon 1500 final on his projection, which is worth knowing before the email.
  6. UNC Charlotte — the boundary case the data resolved, and it resolved upward. He lands essentially level with their 7th man (19 seconds inside) on a soft 167-second spread. 108 miles, American Conference, 80% admit, about $30,688 on his residency ($15,435 in-state), and the largest CS department on the board at 15.2% of all degrees granted — which for a computer science major is the strongest academic argument in this ring outside Davidson and Georgia Tech.

Just behind them: UNC Greensboro (3rd-to-4th man and 133 seconds inside their seven — the deepest slot in the metro, though $27,316 on his residency is the argument it loses), Kennesaw State (4th man, but a 275-second spread, which is a strung-out squad rather than a pack), Campbell and Chattanooga at the far edge of the old 250-mile ring, and High Point and Elon if the price stops mattering. The two the wider ring added sit here rather than in the six above, and for opposite reasons: Auburn is an SEC target on the arithmetic but finished only six men at the conference meet and costs about $46,355, and Samford is the cleaner of the two on running — their 3rd-to-4th man, 109 seconds inside their seven — at 296 miles and $32,622, so distance and price are what hold it back rather than the fit. Mercer used to be named here and is gone — it sponsors men's cross country and no men's outdoor track, and it is one of three Division 1 schools the both-sports rule cost, alongside Tennessee Tech, which the wider ring reached at target tier and which the rule removed in the same pass, and Jacksonville State. Anderson (SC), Catawba, Emory, Fayetteville State and nine other target-tier schools are gone for a different reason — the Division 1 rule — and they are tabled at the bottom of this page rather than deleted.

One target has to be flagged: Queens (Charlotte) has no computer science degree. It earns the tier on running — their 5th man, 22 seconds inside their seven, on the tightest spread in the metro at 56 seconds — and it fails the other half of the search. It is the only school in the target band anywhere on the board without a CS program.

The boundary cases are settled, and they split

UNC Charlotte moves up. Their seven at the D1 Southeast regional ran 30:18 to 33:05 over 10K, putting their 7th man 19 seconds behind his projection, so he is in the travel squad as a freshman. That is a target.

Appalachian State moves down to Deep. Their 7th man is about 27 seconds faster than his projection — a strong Sun Belt program he would train with but not travel for in year one. Not a cut, and not a target either: worth an email that asks specifically what their incoming distance class looks like.

Anderson (SC) is settled too, and then archived, which is worth stating in the same breath. At 30 miles it was the closest target-tier school here and got the most scrutiny of any row on the board: their South Atlantic Conference course ran about 96 seconds slow at 8K (Anderson, Catawba and Wingate each confirm that against the D2 regional), and once corrected he sits six seconds inside their 7th man — the closest call anywhere in this project. It is Division 2, so the Division 1 rule removed it. The measurement stands and the page stands; only the board changed.

The Division 2 scholarship argument, and why it is now an archive argument

This callout used to be about picking the right D2 school. That question is settled and then shelved, and both halves are worth keeping. Lenoir-Rhyne was withdrawn from an earlier version's top picks on its own result: their scoring six spanned 326 seconds at the D2 Southeast regional and he would arrive as their #1, which is the thin-squad pattern this whole board is built to avoid. Catawba and Anderson survived the same test — 5th-to-6th man and 7th man respectively — so the D2 argument was live, and specific.

It is now an argument about the archive rather than the board. Division 2 is where the money for a distance runner is easiest to find: 52 of the 96 archived schools are D2, and in this ring they include most of the thirteen target-tier rows the rule cost. If a partial D2 offer ever becomes more attractive than a walk-on-adjacent spot in a D1 seven, the reversal is one line of data and nothing has to be re-measured.

The sharpest trade-off on the board: Wofford

Strong academics, a verified CS degree, 32 miles from home — and a team best 5000 of 15:27 that he would beat the day he arrived. The cross country result now says the same thing more bluntly: at the Southern Conference championship Wofford finished six runners, and his projection would have been their #1 by 56 seconds across a squad that spanned 222 seconds. Being a team's #1 is workable if the coach is building a recruiting class around him. Ask directly who else they are signing, and how many men will be on the roster in the fall. It is bad if he trains alone for four years.

Wofford is not the exception, and the Division 1 rule is why it is no longer the rule

Measuring every school turned up a pattern the old page could not see. Across the full all-divisions sweep of this ring, 27 of 96 measured programs had no 7th man to compare against: they never got seven runners to the finish of a championship race in 2025. Carson-Newman, Lenoir-Rhyne, King University, Newberry, Montreat, Coker, Methodist, Piedmont and Oglethorpe were all in that group, and Benedict finished three.

On the Division 1 board that count is five of 31 — Winthrop at five finishers, and Gardner-Webb, Wofford, NC Central and now Auburn at six — with nine more that came up short in one of their two races and fielded a full seven in the other, which is usually a regional entry list rather than a roster problem. Those pages compare him to the team's last finisher and say so plainly. It is not a data gap; it is the single most useful thing this round of work produced, because every one of those schools would otherwise have looked like a place he could contribute. He can, and there would be nobody to run with.

Cut as walk-on

Worth naming the cost plainly: this removes Furman at 7 miles, the closest school with one of the strongest CS departments in the region. If a walk-on or redshirt path ever becomes acceptable, it returns at the top of the list, not the bottom.

Clemson, at 30 miles, is no longer on this list. It was cut on a 14:36 team-best 5000 alone, and the ACC championship result does not support that: he would have been their 8th man there and their 7th at the Southeast regional, 8 seconds outside their seven on average. That is deep — a program he would train with and not travel for as a freshman — and it is back on the board above.

Georgia Tech is no longer on this list either, and it came off by the board's own rule rather than by judgement. It was carried as a cut on reputation; the transcribed times put it 41.8 seconds outside their seventh man across the ACC championship and the D1 South regional, and the published cut line is 45. Keeping it cut would have meant contradicting the ladder, so it is back on the board at deep, 143 miles out, with the strongest CS department in this ring — and with the one caveat that reverses the usual argument here: at 30.4% of all degrees granted in computer science and a 14% overall admit rate, the binding constraint at Georgia Tech is the application, not the running.

Four rows on this table used to read "out of range," which was simply wrong — Athens is 101 driving miles from Greenville, Knoxville 169 and Winston-Salem 177, all inside even the old 200-mile ring. Georgia, Tennessee and Wake Forest now carry the real reason, which is level rather than distance, and UNC Chapel Hill and Duke came genuinely into range as the ring widened. Every cut on this page has now been measured against its 2025 championship results and the cut held every time — at Tennessee, the softest of them, he would have been 45 seconds outside the seven at the SEC meet, where they ran a full squad; at Wake Forest, the most decisive, 137 seconds outside in both races. That is what a confirmed cut looks like, and it is why the Clemson and Georgia Tech reinstatements are credible rather than wishful. South Carolina used to be the one row here resting on reputation, and it turned out to be worse than that — it sponsors no men's cross country at all, so it was cut for times that were never going to exist. The 1500 in May

Every school page in this metro now carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Two readings come out of it.

The event is genuinely competitive here, which is the change the Division 1 rule made. Across the all-divisions version of this ring his 4:01 would have made 67 of 81 conference finals and scored in 35. On the Division 1 board it makes the final at 17 of 31 and scores — top eight — at 3. The fourteen where he would have missed the final are the level marker worth knowing by name: ETSU, UNC Greensboro, Appalachian State, Chattanooga, Coastal Carolina, Queens (Charlotte), The Citadel, West Georgia, Western Carolina, Wofford, Clemson, Georgia Tech, Auburn and Samford. Eight of those fourteen are target tier on cross country, which is the useful surprise: the 8K and the 1500 are not the same question, and a program can be the right depth fit in the fall and out of reach in May.

And the thinness that dominated the wider board is mostly gone. The median program in this ring has a 1500 runner at 3:50.4, nearly eleven seconds faster than he projects, and he would be their fastest man in the event at only 4 of 31 — Elon, The Citadel, Gardner-Webb and Presbyterian. Across the all-divisions sweep it was 33 of 72, so this is the same reversal the front page describes. What has not gone away is programs that sponsor men's track and do not race the metric mile: 7 of the 31 entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference championship — Elon, Campbell, Chattanooga, College of Charleston, The Citadel, Presbyterian and Wofford. College of Charleston is the awkward one: fourth on the six at the top of this page, and no conference-meet 1500 entry to compare him to. That is a question for the coach, not a cut.

Among the six at the top of this page the 1500 splits cleanly. UNC Charlotte is the healthiest number on the page: he would be their #17 of 23 on the season depth list, which means a genuine pack to train in rather than a slot to fill. UNC Asheville (#6 of 11) and ETSU (#8 of 11) are next. Davidson (#7 of 7) and Coastal Carolina (#4 of 4) are the warnings — he would be the slowest 1500 runner on either roster, which at Coastal Carolina is a four-man event group. How big the school is, and how big the town is

Two new columns in the table above, and they only appear on this page: Students (undergraduate enrollment, federal) and Town (the population of the place the campus actually sits in, U.S. Census ACS 2024 5-year). New York and Chicago do not need them. Around Greenville they matter, because the 300-mile ring reaches from Charlotte to towns of six hundred people, and nothing else in the table tells those two apart.

Narrowing to Division 1 changed both of these distributions more than any other column. The median school here now has 6,081 undergraduates in a town of 38,910 — against 1,819 in a town of 27,636 across the full all-divisions sweep, because the small-town private colleges were almost all D2 and D3. Only 4 of the 31 sit in a place under 10,000 people and 11 sit in one over 100,000. The very small campuses are in the archive, and that is a real difference in what freshman year would feel like, not just a statistic.

Three campuses on the board have more undergraduates than their town has residents — Kennesaw State (41,254 students, Kennesaw's 34,605), Clemson (23,300 / 18,072) and Western Carolina (10,024 / 7,973). App State is near-parity at 19,444 in a Boone of 20,032. In all of them the school is the town, which is a real answer to "what is there to do" and not automatically a bad one for someone whose afternoons are spoken for. Two more, UNC Pembroke (5,330 / 2,822) and Montreat (762 / 630), are in the archive.

At the other end, the smallest places left on the board are Buies Creek NC (3,746, where Campbell is target tier), Boiling Springs NC (4,757), Clinton SC (7,676) and Cullowhee NC (7,973) — nothing under three thousand, where the wider sweep reached down to Montreat's 630 and Misenheimer NC's 659. Ask those coaches exactly one question anyway: where does the team run. A 12-mile long run out of a town of four thousand is either trails and gravel, which is ideal, or a state highway with no shoulder, which is not.

Size cuts both ways on the two things that actually decide this. A small college means a small training group — fewer people to chase, which is the whole point of the framing rule — and it means the CS lab-conflict problem below is at its worst. A big state school means depth to chase and multiple lab sections, and it means he is one of forty distance runners competing for a coach's attention rather than one of eight. The target tier still spans most of the range: Queens (Charlotte), 1,211 students, and Kennesaw State, 41,254, are the same tier and completely different lives — a factor of 34 between them, where the all-divisions board spanned a factor of 63.

Neither column moves any tier, and both carry caveats worth knowing before quoting them — Augusta, Macon and Athens are consolidated city-county governments so their numbers are county-wide, four of these places are census-designated rather than incorporated, and a small figure inside Atlanta (Morrow GA, 6,364) describes the address rather than the setting. Read the town column next to the Mi column, never alone. The sources and all four caveats are in §6.

Money and admissions

Recruitment is an admissions lever at the selective schools. Coaches at Davidson get supported slots and will run a pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach informally whether he would be admitted. Initiate this in the spring of junior year. The federal numbers make the case sharper than the earlier estimates did: Davidson admits 13% and he would be their 4th man, which is the single highest-value application on this page. Georgia Tech is the harder version of the same lever — a 14% overall admit rate, and a CS admit that is much harder than that, against a deep rather than target running fit. Emory used to be named here at an 11% admit rate and is Division 3, so it is in the archive.

Where academics are an asset rather than a constraint: UNC Asheville (92%), Appalachian State (90%), UNC Greensboro (89%), Campbell (87%), ETSU (86%) and Chattanooga (81%) — all figures from federal data rather than estimates. Admission is close to automatic, which shifts all the leverage to money. Note that the Division 1 rule removed most of the easy admissions as well as most of the cheap prices: nearly the whole archived D2 list sat above 80%. One estimate on this page was badly wrong before the federal check: Wofford is 52%, not ~70%.

Academic aid often beats athletic aid. D1 cross country and track is a 12.6-scholarship equivalency spread across a 35–45 man roster — quarter and half rides, not full ones. A strong student stacking merit aid at UNC Asheville, ETSU, High Point or Elon can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer somewhere else.

In-state tuition is the cheapest lever available, and Division 1 keeps seven of them. A South Carolina resident pays in-state tuition at SC State ($11,060), Coastal Carolina ($11,640), The Citadel ($12,570) and the College of Charleston ($12,978), alongside USC Upstate, Winthrop and Clemson. Those are sticker tuition figures, not net, and they are lower than the net price of most of the private colleges on this page. Two of the seven — Coastal Carolina and the College of Charleston — are target tier, which is the useful overlap. What the Division 1 rule cost on this axis is the whole D2 layer: the wider sweep found 43 Division 2 programs in this ring, and D2 allows 12.6 scholarship equivalencies in men's cross country and track, the same as D1, on much smaller rosters. That is where partial athletic aid actually lives, and it is all in the archive now.

CS labs collide with practice. Large schools like Charlotte, Kennesaw State and App State run multiple lab sections; small ones — Presbyterian (852 undergraduates), Queens (1,211), Wofford (1,816), Gardner-Webb (1,856) and Davidson (1,867) — may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. Ask every coach how many of their athletes major in CS or engineering and how they handle lab conflicts. This question eliminates programs faster than times do — and every school page now carries the coach's name, title, office phone and email, so asking it is a list of messages to send rather than a research project.
