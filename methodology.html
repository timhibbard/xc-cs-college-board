Read before acting on anything here

Methodology, conversions, and what this data cannot tell you

Every number on this site rests on a small set of conversions, one comparison, and a handful of corrections that had to be made when better data arrived. All of it is worth understanding before using any of it to make a decision — particularly §3, which explains why four of this board's earlier recommendations were withdrawn. He has run a new 5K cross country best of 15:55, which retires the ~15:59 projection the whole cross-country chain was built on and drops his solved fatigue exponent from 1.196 to 1.117 — the upside case §1a described, arriving a season early. Recomputing every school against it also surfaced a real error: the 96.5 second South Atlantic course correction had only ever been applied to the three teams used to derive it, and never to the other seven (§3b). Both changes are in. Before them, the board required both sports — men's cross country and men's outdoor track — which removed seventeen schools that sponsor only the first (§6b), and every school page now carries its team's 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projection dropped into it (§7). Before that, every school but one got a real cross country result behind its tier, which forced a rule for the 45 programs that never finished seven runners (§3a), and every school page names the coach to email and says where that came from (§6).

The 15:20 is now a floor, not a projection

It was reached by projecting his 3200 forward. The 15:55 reaches it a second way and independently: the gap between a 5K run on grass and a track 5000 is about 39 seconds at this level, which puts him at roughly 15:16 on the track today — at the fast edge of the range the board already used. College men race 8K in cross country and 10K at most NCAA championship meets, so the mark has to be carried across distances to be compared to a scoring seven. From his 15:55 5K: 8K = 26:00, and 10K = 8K × 1.26 = 32:46. That 1.26 factor is not assumed — it was checked against teams that raced both an 8K conference championship and a 10K regional in 2025, and the two implied 8K-equivalents agreed within about seven seconds for Big South and Southern Conference squads.

1a. The pace conversions behind those numbers, and why the 1600 is not used

Every distance conversion on this site is a Riegel projection: T₂ = T₁ × (D₂/D₁)e, where e is a fatigue exponent. The textbook value is 1.06, fitted to trained adult distance runners. Run straight off the 1600, that exponent gives an answer that looks great and should not be believed:

A 4:21 1600 at the textbook exponent converts to a 14:33 5000 — nearly two minutes faster than he has actually run for 5K, and fast enough to make him a scoring runner at half the D1 programs on this board. His own exponent now sits between 1.12 and 1.15 against a textbook 1.06, having been 1.12 to 1.20 before the 15:55. That is still the signature of a speed-leaning runner building aerobic mileage, but the lean is smaller than it was, and the direction of travel is the one that matters. It still means the further out from 1600m a projection is carried, the more it overstates him: at the textbook exponent 4:21 claims a 14:33 5K, which is 82 seconds faster than he has run.

The sharper reading is what happens to the older conversion. His 1600→3200 exponent of 1.152 has not moved, because neither of those marks has. Carried out to 5K it predicts 16:10 — and he has now run 15:55. His 5K is 15 seconds ahead of what his own middle-distance marks predict, where it used to be 19 seconds behind them. That is the aerobic side catching up, measured rather than assumed.

So the track 5000 of 15:20 used everywhere on this site is anchored to the 3200, not the 1600: projected 3200 of 9:29 → 3000 of 8:53 → 5000 of 15:20. Anchoring to the nearest race distance is the conservative choice and the defensible one. Checked backwards at the textbook exponent, a 15:30 5000 implies a 9:39 3200 and a 4:38 1600 — which is why a 15:20–15:30 5000 is consistent with his 3200 while a 14:33 is not.

The 15:55 puts pressure on that anchor from the other side. Run backwards at his old 1.196 exponent, a 15:55 5K implies a 3200 of about 9:20 — faster than the 9:29 the 15:20 is built on. Feeding 9:20 through the same chain would give a track 5000 nearer 15:06. That number is not used anywhere on this site, because it stacks a projection on top of a projection: it takes a mark he ran on grass, converts it to a distance he has not raced this year, and then converts it again. The 15:20 stays, and it is now corroborated from two directions instead of one.

Two practical consequences. First, a college coach shown only the 1600 will form a different picture than the data supports, so lead with the 3200 and the 5K. Second — and this is the part that has now happened — an earlier version of this page said that if his exponent fell toward 1.10 as mileage went up, his 5K would improve faster than his 1600 and every gap on this board would narrow without the 1600 moving at all, and called that the upside case and a reason to email schools in the Deep tier.

The upside case happened, and it is worth being precise about how little of it was the PR

The exponent went 1.196 → 1.117 on a single race, and the 1600 did not move. But the 8K only came down seven seconds (26:07 → 26:00), because the 8K was already built on a projected 15:59 rather than on the 16:29 he had actually run. Seven seconds moves two schools: Anderson (SC) from Deep to Target, and Southern Wesleyan from Target to Caution.

What the 15:55 really changed is confidence, not position. The board was scored against a projection; it is now scored against a result. Five further tier changes in this pass came from the course-correction error in §3b, not from the PR — worth keeping straight, because it would be easy to read the new Target count as something the race earned.

1b. The 1500 — the one conversion the 1600 is right for

Colleges race 1500m, not 1600m, and 128 of the 161 programs on this site have an outdoor 1500 on file — marginally more than have a 5000 (124), and 123 of them have both. Comparing him there needs a 1500, and here the objection in §1a does not apply: a projection can only flatter a speed-leaning runner as it moves away from his speed, and 1500m is 100 metres shorter than the race he has actually run. The lean is an asset. So the 1500 on every school page is anchored to the 1600, at the textbook exponent, with no fudge factor:

1600 → 1500 at e = 1.06. The factor is (1500/1600)1.06 = 0.9339.

Two cross-checks. The coach's rule of thumb — subtract about 17 seconds from a 1600 to get a 1500 — gives 4:01 from 4:18, the same answer. And the arithmetic difference between the two rows, 2.8 seconds for a 3-second 1600 improvement, is the sanity check that the conversion is doing nothing clever: a 1500 is 94% of a 1600, so improvements pass through almost one for one.

Read the 1500 gap on its own scale, not the 5000's

A 1500 gap and a 5000 gap are not comparable in seconds. Holding the percentage constant, the healthy 40–60 second band at 5000 (4.4–6.7% of about 920 seconds) is 10 to 15 seconds at 1500. Twelve seconds behind a team's best 1500 is the same relationship as fifty behind their best 5000 — a squad with people to chase. Being ahead of their best 1500 means the same thing it means at 5000: the program is thin.

The 1500 is still a one-athlete, one-event, one-season number with every weakness described in §3. It is on the school pages because it is cheap, dense, and a second independent read on the same question — not because it outranks a cross country result. §7 replaces it with something much better: the whole 1500 field at each team's 2026 conference championship, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season.

2. The framing rule — and the mistake it corrects

Being behind the team's best runner is the goal

For a projected 15:15–15:25 5000, 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best runner is the healthy target. It means training partners ahead of him and a realistic path to scoring by junior year. The aim is to be the 5th to 9th man as a freshman.

Arriving ahead of a team's #1 means the program is thin, not that he is a star. Distance runners develop in packs; there is no substitute for someone to chase in a Tuesday workout. The first version of this analysis treated "he'd be their #1" as a positive signal. That was backwards, and the tiers on this site reflect the corrected reading.

3. Where the times come from, and the limitation that matters

Roster marks come from TFRRS, the Track & Field Results Reporting System — the sport's system of record. Team pages there return the single fastest athlete, in one event, in the 2026 outdoor season only. Three consequences follow, and they are the most important caveats on this site:

  1. It is a floor, never a ceiling. A program whose distance runners focused on the 10K, the steeplechase, or the indoor season will show an artificially slow outdoor 5000. A slow number is weak evidence of a weak program; a fast number is strong evidence of a strong one.
  2. Sparse events return garbage. The mile, 3000, and 10,000 columns are unreliable — only the 5000 and 1500 can be trusted. The tell is internal contradiction: High Point's 1500 of 3:43.9 (about a 4:01 mile) sits beside a "best mile" of 4:21; Lenoir-Rhyne's 3000 of 8:47 sits beside a "best 1500" of 4:19; Catawba's 5000 of 14:37 sits beside a "best 3000" of 10:21. Those tables were empty, not slow.
  3. Team pages carry no depth at all. One athlete per event means no 5th man and no 7th man. This was the largest gap on the site and it has now been closed for 148 of the 150 schools that field a men's team — it was 37 — by going to a different source: individual meet result pages from the 2025 cross country season, which list every team's scoring seven by name and time. Where those exist they supersede the track marks entirely. Where they do not, the school sits in Verify and its tier should be read as a placeholder; only Shorter and Calumet College are left in that state, both of them in the non-Division 1 archive.

3a. How the cross country comparison is actually built

For every school with results, his projection is dropped into that team's finishing order and three numbers come out. All three are averaged across every race of theirs on file, and the per-race detail is on each school's own page:

  • Slot — where he would have finished inside their scoring seven. This is the headline number.
  • vs their 7th — his projection minus their 7th man. Negative means he makes the travel squad.
  • 1–7 spread — the gap from their #1 to their #7. This is the most robust number on the site, because it compares a team only to itself and is therefore immune to course, weather, and conversion error. A 40-second spread is a pack; a 300-second spread is a handful of runners and some bodies.

Teams with no 7th man — the rule that had to be added once everyone was measured

A cross country team scores five and travels seven, so every number above assumes seven finishers. Measuring all 155 schools showed that assumption is wrong far more often than expected: 45 of the 148 measured programs never finished seven runners in any 2025 championship race — 27 around Greenville, 13 around New York, 5 in Chicago. Guessing at the missing men would invent the exact number the board is built on, so instead:

  • Short races store what actually happened. A race with fewer than seven finishers records how many there were and reports his projection minus their last finisher in place of vs-7th. The school page says which it is, race by race, and vs their 7th is left blank in every table rather than filled with a substitute.
  • Fewer than five finishers is not a team result. Five is the minimum to score, so those races are excluded from the averaged slot, gap and spread entirely — 17 of the 228 races on file. They still appear on the school page, because "they entered three men and finished three" is the finding.
  • Five schools never finished five: Roosevelt, Benedict, John Jay, Mount Saint Vincent and York College (CUNY). Being the fastest man on a squad of four is the outcome this board exists to avoid, so all five sit at Caution regardless of how the times read.
  • One- and two-finisher races are read as individual qualifiers, not teams. Every such race on file is an NCAA regional, where a program that did not qualify as a team may still send one or two runners. Those are labelled as regionals on the school page and carry no team inference at all.

Individual athletes are deliberately not named anywhere on this site. Their times are what the comparison needs; their identities are not, and this is a public page. Every race is labelled with its meet and date, so any of it can be checked against the source.

For a measured school the tiers then follow mechanically: more than 45 seconds outside their 7th man is a cut; anything outside it is Deep; a slot of 2 or better is Caution, because he would arrive at or near their #1; and everything else is Target. Where there is no 7th man, the same ladder runs on the last finisher, which is strictly harder to clear.

Verify now means one thing only: no cross country result exists yet. An earlier version also assigned it to measured schools with a slot under 3.5, which conflated "borderline" with "unknown" and made the tier unreadable — a school could sit in Verify either because the data said something ambiguous or because there was no data at all. That band is retired; borderline measured schools land in Caution or Target on the ladder above, and Verify is a to-do list. It currently holds Calumet College and Shorter, plus South Carolina on the cut list.

Course difficulty, and why only two corrections were applied

Cross country courses are not comparable. A hilly 8K can run two minutes slower than a flat one, so comparing his projection to raw times mis-ranks whole conferences. The correction available here is that many teams raced both their conference championship and an NCAA regional, so the difference in their scoring-five averages isolates the course rather than the team — provided enough teams did it. Two meets cleared that bar:

MeetOffset at 8KTeams cross-checked

Single-team deltas were not applied — a CAA offset from Elon alone, or a MAAC offset from a single MAAC team alone, cannot be separated from that one team having a bad day. Sweeping the whole board raised the number of courses in play to roughly fifty and did not raise the number that clear the bar: two. Most of the rest were crossed by one or two teams. So every school whose only result comes from an uncorrected course carries more uncertainty than its tier suggests, and that is now most of the board rather than a footnote.

Getting this wrong is easy and the error is loud. A first pass had the sign inverted, which made the South Atlantic times slower and promoted Wingate from a cut straight to target tier. The fixed version reconciles all three South Atlantic teams' conference and regional reads to within a few seconds of each other, which is what makes the correction believable.

3b. The correction that was only half-applied

Seven of the ten South Atlantic races never got the correction described above

Recomputing the board against the 15:55 exposed this. The −96.5 second offset was applied to Anderson, Catawba and Wingate — the three teams whose conference-to-regional deltas were used to derive it — and then never back-filled to the other seven South Atlantic programs on the board. Their times sat in the tables raw. In two cases the same race object was internally inconsistent: the gap-to-#1 was computed without the correction while the gap-to-last-finisher was computed with it.

The direction matters. The correction makes those teams' times faster, so leaving it out made them look thinner than they are — and thin is exactly what this board demotes. Five schools were sitting in Caution for a reason that was an arithmetic omission: Emory & Henry, Lincoln Memorial, Mars Hill, Tusculum and UVA Wise, all now Target. Two more, Coker and Newberry, had their gaps corrected but stay in Caution on the underlying result.

This is the fourth error on this page that ran in the flattering-to-thin-programs direction, and like the sign inversion above it was only found because everything was recomputed from the raw times rather than edited in place. Every derived number in the data files is now regenerated from the runners' times, so this particular class of error cannot recur silently: the check is that each stored slot, gap and spread reproduces exactly from the times it claims to summarise.

A method that was built, tested, and thrown away

An intermediate approach anchored his projection to each team's own 5000 data: take their #1's cross country time, then add the gap between his 5000 and their best 5000, scaled to 8K. It is appealing because it cancels out course difficulty entirely.

It is also systematically too generous, and the reason is the same flaw that runs through §3: a team's best outdoor 5000 is a floor, set by whichever athlete happened to race it, and that athlete frequently is not their cross country #1. DePaul's best 5000 is 14:22, but their cross country #1 ran the equivalent of 23:44; Hofstra's is 14:32 against a 23:01. The method moved DePaul from 7th man to 4th, ETSU from 4th to 2nd, Wheaton from 5th to 2nd, and Hofstra from 8th to 5th — always in the flattering direction. It was rejected in favour of raw comparison plus the two course corrections above.

Claims that were withdrawn

Clemson. An earlier version concluded that Clemson's distance corps was soft for the ACC, based on a 14:36 outdoor 5000. That was an artifact of a sparse table. Their 1500 best of 3:43 implies a runner around 14:15, and the conclusion has been retracted. Similar "only one distance runner" readings for Gardner-Webb and Guilford have been softened from established to suggestive for the same reason.

Lewis University. When the Chicago radius was 20 miles, this analysis recommended widening it to 30 specifically to reach Lewis, calling it "probably the single best fit in metro Chicago" — a claim made from general knowledge of the program, not from roster data. Once the radius was widened and the data pulled, their team best 5000 came back at 13:45.06, alongside a 29:58 10K and a 3:51 1500. Because the 13:45 was run at the Bryan Clay Invitational (a fast qualifying meet) and the 10K at a tactical conference championship, the marks are consistent rather than contradictory. Lewis is a 95 second gap and belongs in the cut list. The recommendation is withdrawn, and it is the most consequential reversal on this site.

Wheaton College — wrong twice, in opposite directions. First described as a "national-caliber D3 program" from general knowledge and cut on that basis; that was withdrawn when its outdoor table (5000 in 15:19.11, internally consistent across four events) failed to support it. Then a later pass declared the question resolved in Wheaton's favour and upgraded it to Target, which was also wrong. Their CCIW pack of 25:43–26:48 does put him around 5th man, but at the D3 Midwest regional their 4th through 7th ran 27:03–27:35 and he would have been their #2. Correct the CCIW course, which ran about 31 seconds fast, and the two races agree that he arrives near the front. Wheaton is Caution.

Hofstra and Loyola Chicago. Both were recommended as among the best fits in their metros, on outdoor 5000 marks of 14:32 and 14:23. Both then won their conference championships with sevens he would finish roughly 75 seconds outside — Hofstra's spanning 23:02–24:45, Loyola's just 47 seconds wide. Both are now cut.

North Central College — a withdrawal in the other direction. Cut on the reasoning that a perennial D3 national-championship program must be far beyond him. Their actual 7th man ran 25:34 at the CCIW meet and 25:46 at the regional, within about five seconds of his projection once the CCIW course is corrected. It is back on the board, in the Deep tier.

The pattern in all of them is worth naming: every one began as a confident claim from thin data — either general knowledge or a single fastest time — that a fuller result then contradicted. Treat any statement on this site not backed by a named scoring seven as provisional.

5. Contact rules, scholarships, and admissions leverage

Contact rules run one direction. D1 coaches may initiate contact on June 15 after sophomore year; D2 on July 15 after sophomore year; D3 is essentially unrestricted. But he may email any coach, in any division, at any time. The restriction limits coaches, not athletes.

There is no such thing as a full ride in men's distance running. D1 and D2 men's cross country and track share 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across a 35 to 45 man roster, so offers are quarter and half rides. Post-settlement, programs operate to roster limits. D3 offers no athletic aid at all. NAIA allows 12 scholarships with looser eligibility rules.

Academic aid frequently beats athletic aid. A strong student stacking merit aid at a less selective school can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer elsewhere. Model net cost, never scholarship percentage.

At selective schools, the coach is an admissions lever. Coaches at Davidson, Emory, NYU, Fordham, Stevens, and the University of Chicago get supported slots and will run an informal pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach whether he would likely be admitted. This is how running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not. Initiate in the spring of junior year.

Ask about lab scheduling at every school. Required CS lab blocks collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Large universities run several sections; a small college may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. The question — "how many of your athletes major in CS or engineering, and how do you handle lab conflicts?" — eliminates programs faster than times do.

6. Where the cost, admissions, and coach data come from

Every cost, SAT, admit-rate, and CS-program figure on this site now comes from the U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard — the federal collection that every institution is required to report to. All 150 schools with a men’s program on this site — 44 on the board, 12 on the cut list, 17 that field cross country without men’s outdoor track, and 77 archived as non-Division 1 — are matched to exactly one federal record each, using an explicit name map rather than fuzzy matching, because fuzzy matching produced confident false positives at a similarity score of 1.0 (Georgia State matched to Georgia Tech, among six others).

That exercise was worth doing. It found 33 SAT ranges wrong by 60 points or more and nine admit rates wrong by 15 points or more versus the estimates this site previously showed. King University is open-admission at 100%, not ~70%; Fairfield is 33%, not ~57%; Wofford 52%, not ~70%; Lander 81%, not ~55%. Anyone who had used the old figures to decide where to apply would have been misled.

Two size figures sit beside the money on the Greenville table and on every school page. Undergraduate enrollment is from the same federal record, and every one of the 44 schools on the board has it. Town population is not federal-education data at all — it comes from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey, table B01003, 2024 5-year estimates (2020–2024), read through the Census Reporter API and matched to the exact place each campus sits in. It is collected for the Greenville ring only: how big New York and Chicago are is not a question this board needs to answer, and a column that repeats the same eight-figure number down every row is noise.

Four caveats apply to reading them:

  • Public schools show the rate he would actually pay — the out-of-state rate, since he is a South Carolina resident. In-state figures apply only to the eleven South Carolina publics on the site: Clemson, South Carolina, Coastal Carolina, the College of Charleston, The Citadel, Winthrop, Lander, USC Upstate, USC Aiken, Francis Marion and SC State. Which rate a school shows is a fact about the school, not a setting.
  • Average net price is an average across all incoming students after grants and scholarships — not a quote. A strong student stacking merit aid usually lands below it; it is a planning number, not a prediction. At a public school the federal average is also computed from in-state students only, which is not the basis that applies to him — see how residency enters the net cost below.
  • Town population is the place, not the setting. Three of these figures are consolidated city-county governments — Augusta and Macon in Georgia, and Athens for the University of Georgia — so they are county-wide and read larger than what surrounds the campus. Four are census-designated places rather than incorporated towns (Buies Creek, Cullowhee, Tigerville, and Emory VA), where the boundary is a statistical convenience. And a small number inside a large metro describes the address rather than the experience: Morrow GA is 6,364 people and is Atlanta. Always read the town column next to the Mi column. Where it is useful is the opposite case, and there are five of them: at Montreat, Western Carolina, Kennesaw State, Clemson and UNC Pembroke there are more undergraduates than town residents — and only the middle three are still on the Division 1 board.
  • The CS share is CIP family 11 only. A computer science degree housed administratively under mathematics reads as zero — which is exactly what happened at SUNY Purchase (now out of range), where the real program was a combined BA in Mathematics/Computer Science. A low share is not proof of absence. One school on the board reports no CS degree: Queens (Charlotte), and because it is Target tier on the running it is the only place this caveat could change a real decision on the board. It reports 0% computing degrees and a live mathematics program, which at a small college is usually the signature of CS filed under mathematics rather than of no CS at all, and its page says so rather than asserting the degree does not exist. Twelve of the archived non-D1 rows report none either, and they are not all the same case. Three were checked by hand and disqualified on their own course catalogues: Mount Saint Vincent (no CS and no men's track), the Merchant Marine Academy (five marine majors and a service obligation), and North Park. Five more carry the same explicit warning as Queens instead of a hand check — Brevard, Belmont Abbey, Young Harris, Allen University and Oglethorpe. Emory & Henry is the row to re-check first if the Division 1 rule is ever relaxed: it is the only Target-tier row in the archive with no confirmed CS degree, and the §3b course correction is what moved it into Target, so its CS question was previously moot and now is not. The last three — Lees-McRae, SUNY Maritime and Sarah Lawrence — report 0% and have not been checked either way; all three are thin-squad cautions on the running side, so the CS question is not what rules them out.

How residency enters the net cost

This is the one place where two federal figures sitting next to each other are measured on different bases, so it is worth stating plainly. The tuition line on a school page is the rate that would apply to him: the in-state rate at the eleven South Carolina publics, the out-of-state rate at the other 52 publics, and the single rate every student pays at a private. The average net price line is a different federal field, and at a public institution the Scorecard computes it from in-state students only. Out-of-state students are not in that average at all.

So at an out-of-state public the two numbers describe two different people, and the net price is the optimistic one. Stony Brook’s federal net price is $18,784 — for a New Yorker. Its own residents pay $10,931 in tuition and everyone else pays $32,741. Reading $18,784 as what he would pay understates it by the whole $21,810 gap.

Every page that shows a net price for an out-of-state public therefore shows federal net price + (out-of-state tuition − in-state tuition), marked with a ≈ and a dotted underline to say it is an estimate rather than a reported figure. That arithmetic holds average grant aid constant and moves only the tuition, which is the only part of the bill that depends on residency — room and board do not. It applies to 17 of the 44 schools on the board (14 around Greenville, 2 around New York, 1 in Chicago) and to 50 publics site-wide. Two publics are unaffected because they charge one rate to everyone: Chicago State and the Merchant Marine Academy. Privates are unaffected by definition.

The correction is not cosmetic. On the Division 1 board it moves the median net price from $17,527 to $25,613 — and from $24,423 to $29,844 around New York, where most of the cheap options are public. It cuts “targets under $15,000” from five schools to one: only Coastal Carolina ($13,966) survives, because it is in South Carolina. UNC Asheville, UNC Greensboro, Chattanooga and West Georgia all read under $15,000 on the raw federal figure and none of them does on his residency. The largest single moves on the board are Georgia Tech ($12,116 → $34,542), Auburn ($24,323 → $46,355) and Stony Brook ($18,784 → $40,594).

Three things could make the estimate too high, and all three are worth asking about:

  • A non-resident tuition waiver. Some publics can waive part of the premium for recruited athletes, and some do it academically by merit threshold. Where that happens the added amount disappears in whole or in part. It is a coach-and-financial-aid question, not something a federal file can answer.
  • Regional tuition agreements. The Academic Common Market can grant in-state rates to a South Carolina student in a specific major not offered in-state; computer science will not usually qualify, but the mechanism exists.
  • Athletic money. Division I and II programs can award it; Division III cannot, and roughly half this board is Division III. Where it exists in cross country it is normally partial and split many ways.

What could make it too low: a non-resident whose family qualifies for less need-based aid than the in-state average would pay more than the estimate, not less. The estimate is a planning midpoint on his residency, and the sticker price above it is still the ceiling.

The coach on every school page

Each of the 151 school pages that has a coach ends with a Who to email block: the name of the coach who would actually recruit a distance runner there, the title the school gives them, an office phone, and an email address. It is read off the employing school's own athletics staff directory and nowhere else — three platforms cover almost all of them (Sidearm Sports at /sports/<sport>/coaches or /staff-directory, PrestoSports at /information/directory/index, and a handful of hand-built department pages) — and each page links the exact directory it came from.

Four things about this data specifically:

  • Coaches are named; athletes are not. That is not an inconsistency. A head coach is a public professional whose employer publishes their name, title and work email in order to be contacted. The runners in a race result are mostly 18-to-22-year-olds who published nothing, and their times are all this comparison needs (§3a).
  • Who counts as "the coach" varies by division. At a large D1 program the right contact is the distance or cross country coach, not the director of track and field; at a small D3 or NAIA school one person often holds cross country, indoor and outdoor, sometimes alongside another sport. The title is printed verbatim rather than normalised, because the title is the signal about how much attention the event group gets.
  • Two schools publish no email at all — Fayetteville State and West Georgia. Their pages say so and point at the phone number or the team's own recruit form instead. Nothing was guessed from a first-initial-plus-surname pattern; a wrong address is worse than a missing one.
  • Sidearm obfuscates addresses in the page source, splitting them across two JavaScript variables that are concatenated in the browser, so an address that looks absent to a naive fetch is usually present. Reassembling those is the difference between the coverage above and roughly half of it.

This data decays faster than anything else on the site. Coaching staffs turn over every year, and these were read in August 2026. The link matters more than the name: if the name is stale, the directory behind it is not.

6a. Known gaps in this data

  1. Every school on the board now has a cross country result — down from 93 unmeasured, which was the largest gap on this site for most of its life, and down from one. The tier Verify, which meant unmeasured, not borderline, is now unused: all 44 Division 1 schools on the board and all 12 on the cut list rest on a 2025 championship result. The two rows that were still open both left the board for reasons unconnected to measurement: Shorter is Division 2 and is archived under §6c (it may have been unmeasurable rather than unmeasured — it entered three men at its only 2025 meet and all three did not start), and South Carolina turned out to sponsor no men's cross country at all (§6b). The archive itself is not complete in this sense: 7 of the 96 archived non-D1 rows have no result on file, though only one of those was a live candidate.
  2. Depth is now measured, but only from championship races. The 228 races on file are conference championships and NCAA regionals, because those are the ones where a program fields its actual seven. That is the right sample for the question, and it also means most schools rest on one or two races, run on a course whose difficulty is usually uncorrected (§3a). A regular-season result would not change a tier, but a second championship result could, and 45 of the measured programs have a further problem on top of that: no 7th man to compare against at all.
  3. How the candidate lists were built, and why the earlier version of this claim was false. An earlier README said this board listed every school inside each radius that sponsors men's cross country. It did not. The lists were originally assembled from general knowledge and then verified individually, which validates what is present and says nothing about what is missing — and about 45 schools were missing from Greenville alone, including one 25 driving miles from home. The lists are now built the other way round: conference by conference, from each conference's own men's-sponsored-sports table. That sweep covered every NCAA D1, D2 and D3 conference with a member inside a ring; the board has since been narrowed to Division 1 (§6c), and the non-D1 half of the sweep is kept whole in the archive rather than discarded. The guarantee is materially stronger than the old one, but it inherits the conferences' errors — a stale sponsored-sports table is wrong in both directions, and each school's sponsorship was not re-confirmed on the school's own athletics site.
  4. NAIA and NCCAA coverage is incomplete and known to be — which the Division 1 rule has made moot for the board and not for the archive. Since the board is Division 1 only, no NAIA or NCCAA program can appear on it, so this gap no longer affects any ranking. It does affect the archive: the four NAIA rows in NOT_D1 are the only non-NCAA programs the sweep ever found, and at least these NAIA and NCCAA programs in the Greenville footprint were never checked — Toccoa Falls, Truett McConnell, Brenau, Columbia International, Columbia College SC, Milligan, Johnson University, Reinhardt, Voorhees, Tennessee Wesleyan, Morris College, Bryan College, Bluefield University, Point University, Life University, Spartanburg Methodist and Warren Wilson. NAIA allows 12 scholarships for men's XC and track and has looser eligibility rules, so if the Division 1 rule is ever relaxed this gap becomes live again, in exactly the place where money is easiest to find.
  5. Greenville driving distances are real routes; the other two metros' are not. The Mi column for every school in the Greenville ring is now a road distance returned by OSRM driving directions from the search center and rounded to the mile. It used to be an estimate — straight-line distance × 1.18, or × 1.35 through the mountain states — and that estimate was off by as much as 54 miles, which is the difference between inside and outside a ring. New York and Chicago mileages are still the looked-up or estimated figures the board started with; at a 20-mile radius the error cannot move a school across the line, so they have not been re-routed.
  6. The both-sports check is a two-signal check, not a third-party dataset. No public file records which schools sponsor men's outdoor track, so §6b's seventeen removals — three Division 1 schools (Mercer, Tennessee Tech and Jacksonville State) plus the fourteen non-D1 rows now sitting in the §6c archive — rest on two independent signals per school rather than on an authority. Both can be wrong the same way: a school that publishes a stale sport list and a women's-only track roster and sent no men to its conference championships — a program suspended for one season, say — would read as no-track when it is not. That failure mode is why each removed school keeps its full record and its page rather than being deleted, and why the reversal is one line of data away.
  7. Only two of roughly fifty championship courses have a difficulty correction. The 228 races on file span about fifty distinct conference and regional courses, and exactly two — the South Atlantic and CCIW championships — had enough teams racing both a conference meet and a regional to isolate the course from the team (§3a). Everywhere else the raw time is used. That is the largest remaining source of error on the board, and it is worst for schools whose only result came from a single uncorrected course: those could move a tier once a second race exists.
  8. Meet schedules cover 150 of the 161 schools with a page, and the venue is read off each meet's own results page. An earlier version of this board had schedules for eleven schools only, and could not place 21% of even those, because it guessed a venue from the host named in the meet title — which fails on exactly the meets that matter most, the conference and NCAA championships whose host rotates every year. Every meet page is now fetched directly, so a rotating championship is placed where it was actually held this season. The sweep covers 712 distinct meets and 3,241 school-meet appearances, of which 3,221 (99.4%) are placed. The gap is two meets — LIU's Fall Festival and the St. Joseph's Invitational — whose pages print no venue line at all. The eleven schools with no schedule are the ten that field no men's cross country team — where the absence is the finding, not a gap — plus Calumet College, whose TFRRS page holds no result after 2024; that is consistent with its removal under the both-sports rule (§6b), and its page says so instead of showing an empty map. South Carolina is the instructive one. It has a schedule, and every meet on it is a track meet: 22 outdoor and indoor meets in 2026 and not one cross country race, which is the same conclusion its empty TFRRS cross country page reaches by a different route.
  9. Venue coordinates carry three precision levels, and most are town-level. TFRRS prints a venue as free text, sometimes with a facility and ZIP and sometimes with neither. Of the 419 distinct venues, 76 resolved to the facility itself, 112 only to the ZIP the results page printed, and 231 only to the town it named — so the majority of pins are a town centroid, which is right for judging travel and wrong for finding the start line. The looser two levels are labelled ZIP area and town on the map and in the table. Geocoding is OpenStreetMap's Nominatim. Eleven venue strings it could not resolve were placed by hand, most of them because TFRRS misspells the town (Bourbannais, Genev, Athen) and two because the place is not a town at all (West Point, Mississippi State); a further dozen had the town silently glued to the end of the facility name and were relabelled by hand. Two TFRRS venue records also contained wrong postal codes (a New Hampshire fairground coded to Massachusetts, an Indiana course coded to Missouri); both were corrected.
  10. Instagram handles were collected for the Division 1 board only. Every school on the board and every school on the cut list has one, and each was read off an official page — the athletics site's own social links or the program's TFRRS/roster header — rather than searched for by name, because a name search on a school like Wingate or Manhattan returns accounts that have nothing to do with the team. Preference is the program account (the men's cross country / track and field team), and where a school links no cross country or track account of its own the fallback is the athletics department's, marked with a degree sign in the tables so it reads as what it is — six schools on the board fall back that way: Appalachian State, the College of Charleston, SC State, West Georgia, Western Carolina and Samford. No school needed a coach's personal account, and where nothing could be verified on an official page no handle is listed rather than a guess. Where a school runs separate cross country and track accounts — UNC Charlotte, Gardner-Webb, Campbell, Charleston Southern, LIU — the cross country one is used, which is why Charleston Southern's reads @csudistance rather than the track account. The 96 archived non-D1 rows have no handles at all — that lookup was not run for them — so an empty Instagram cell on an archived school's page means "not looked up," not "does not exist." Five of the six schools with no men's cross country team have none for the obvious reason; the exception is South Carolina, which sponsors men's track and lists a combined track and cross country account, so its page carries it. The three Division 1 schools removed under the both-sports rule — Mercer, Tennessee Tech and Jacksonville State — keep theirs as well.
  11. Piedmont University's CS program is still unconfirmed — federal data codes the degree but reports roughly zero graduates, and the school's own site was unreachable. It is the last open lookup on the board.
  12. Ten schools were removed because they field no men's cross country team, and one because it is closing. Georgia State, NYIT, Montclair State, William Paterson and North Georgia were all carrying a Verify tier, which is precisely how that tier fails: it reads as a program awaiting measurement rather than one that does not exist. St. John's and South Carolina were sitting on the cut list, cut on reputation for times that were never going to exist; both sponsor women's cross country only. Northwestern is the eighth. The 300-mile Greenville sweep then added two more: UAB came into range at 294 miles and sponsors no men's cross country, and Georgia Southern at 198 miles was inside even the old ring and sponsors none either — both have an empty TFRRS men's cross country page and women's distance programs only. Trinity Christian College announced on 4 November 2025 that it will close after the 2025–26 academic year. The six Division 1 cases have their own section on each metro page; the rest are in the §6c archive, tagged so their pages say the same thing. All are kept on the maps rather than deleted so the corrections stay visible. Ten errors of this kind have now surfaced; assume there are others.

6b. The both-sports rule — cross country and track

This board was built on men's cross country, because cross country championship results are where team depth is visible. That let in a category of school it should not have: programs that sponsor men's cross country and no men's outdoor track. He wants to run both seasons, and a program that offers one cannot give him the other, so seventeen schools came off the board under this rule. It is the only rule on the site that removes a school for a reason that has nothing to do with how fast anybody runs.

No public dataset answers the question, which is why this took two signals per school rather than one lookup:

  1. The school's own sport list. Every athletics site publishes the sports it sponsors. A men's cross country page with no men's track and field page beside it — and, at Mercer, Tennessee Tech, Jacksonville State, Winston-Salem State and Clark Atlanta, a women's track and field page, or a single "track and field" roster that is entirely women, with no men's equivalent — is the school stating it directly.
  2. TFRRS, for the school's men at their conference indoor and outdoor championships. This is the second signal, and it is deliberately narrower than the test used at first. A single open-meet mark is not evidence of a program: men on a cross country roster enter April invitationals in the 1500 and the 5000 whether or not the school sponsors men's track, and Tennessee Tech is exactly that case. A sponsored program has to appear at its own conference championship. Absence from every conference championship, indoor and outdoor, is the signal.

Both had to agree. Two schools were checked and kept on exactly this basis: Fayetteville State and Allen University both sponsor men's outdoor track, and Allen simply fields no distance events — a real answer to a different question, and one for its coach rather than a reason to remove the school. Where the two signals disagreed, the school stayed.

The removed schools keep their pages, their tiers and their numbers

Every one of the seventeen still has a school page, still shows its cross country measurement, and still shows the tier that measurement earned — because the numbers are true and it is the rule, not the running, that removed them. Mercer is the expensive case: measured on cross country it was target tier, his 4th man, 196 miles from home, with a verified CS degree. If a cross-country-only program ever becomes acceptable, that page is the first one to reopen, and it says so at the top.

Widening the Greenville ring to 300 miles then produced two more Division 1 cases of the same shape, and one of them is nearly as expensive as Mercer. Tennessee Tech measured target at their 5th-to-6th man, 270 miles out, at about $18,446 on his residency — the better value of the two — and it is the row that forced the narrower second signal, since its men do post open-meet 1500s and 5000s and appear at no OVC championship. Jacksonville State measured deep, so it costs least to lose; its head coach's title reads "Cross Country & Track and Field," which is the trap on that row, because the track half of the job is the women's team.

Ten of the seventeen are in New York, which is a fact about the metro rather than a coincidence: small private and CUNY colleges there routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop. Six are around Greenville — including all three Division 1 cases — and one is in Chicago.

6c. The Division 1 rule — the search narrowed, and what it cost

The board was built as a sweep of every NCAA Division 1, 2 and 3 school inside each ring, plus a few NAIA programs. It has since been narrowed to Division 1 only. That is a decision about the search, not a finding about any school, and it is the second rule on this site — after the both-sports rule in §6b — that removes a school for a reason unconnected to how fast anybody runs.

Ninety-six schools came off the board. None of the work on them is thrown away: every one is kept whole in the NOT_D1 archive in data.js — measured slot, gap to their #1, 1–7 spread, tier, coach, contact details and reasoning all intact — so the rule can be relaxed later without re-measuring anything. Each keeps its own school page, each is a toggleable layer on its metro map, and each metro page tables them.

Two exclusions can apply to the same school, so each archived row records where it sat before the filter. That matters for reading the cost honestly:

  • 76 were live candidates — tier and all. These are the only rows the Division 1 rule actually cost him.
  • 1 was already cut on its roster times (Wingate, where he would have been a walk-on).
  • 14 had already failed the both-sports rule — cross country without men's outdoor track (§6b).
  • 5 had no men's program at all and were on the board in error.
The cost, stated plainly: thirteen target-tier schools, and the two extremes of the whole board

Thirteen of the 76 were at target tier — inside a scoring seven, which is the entire point of this exercise — and all thirteen are in the Greenville ring. Two of them were the extreme points of the entire project:

Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, was the closest target-tier school anywhere on the board and the most carefully checked row on the site: their 7th man, six seconds inside the scoring seven, on a course correction verified three ways (§3b). Fayetteville State was the cheapest, at a $7,892 federal net price with a verified CS degree, where he would have been their 4th-to-5th man. Both are Division 2. Both are archived.

The rest of the thirteen: Mars Hill (79 mi), Augusta University (115), Tusculum (121), Pfeiffer (138), Catawba (141), Emory (141, and one of the strongest CS departments on any version of this board), Emory & Henry (181), Lincoln Memorial (184), UVA Wise (195), Berry College (204) and UNC Pembroke (214).

The rule is not purely a loss, and it is worth being precise about why. Of the 96, 40 are Division 3 — and D3 cannot offer athletic money at all, so on the scholarship axis those rows were never going to compete with a partial D1 or D2 offer. The real price is the 52 Division 2 programs, which can fund a distance runner and where he would frequently have been a scoring man rather than a squad member. That is the trade the filter makes: a shorter, more uniform list of programs that can all offer athletic aid, bought by giving up the schools where he would have counted for the team soonest.

Reversing it is one line of data. Nothing in the archive is degraded, and the school pages of archived rows say at the top what tier they held before the filter and that the school itself did not fail anything.

7. How the 1500 comparison is built

§1b converts his 1600 to a 4:01 1500 and compares it to each team's single fastest 1500 runner. That is a floor with all the weaknesses of any one-athlete number. This section is what replaced it: for all 120 schools with a 2026 field on file, the entire 1500 field at that program's 2026 outdoor conference championship, with his projection dropped into it on time, plus the program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. 40 conference championship result pages, one per conference, were read, plus four postseason result pages carrying ten programs' runners.

Four numbers come out of it, and they answer different questions:

  • His slot on their season depth chart. Every man who ran a 1500 for that program outdoors in 2026, best mark each, with his projection inserted. This is the 1500's equivalent of "his slot in their 7" and it is the closest thing to a depth measurement the event offers. Read it against the 10–15 second band in §1b, not the 5000's 40–60.
  • His place in the whole conference field. Every man who ran the event at that meet — prelims included, best time per athlete, merged across sections — ranked on time. This is the headline number on each card, and the field size is quoted with him in it: 34 men ran, so he is one of 35.
  • Whether he would have made the final. The slowest man in the final, minus his projection. Positive means he gets in on this year's marks.
  • Where he would have placed among that team's own entrants at the meet, which is often a much smaller number than the depth chart because most programs enter two or three men in a championship 1500.
Why the whole-field placing is the honest headline, and the final's placing is not

Championship 1500 finals are frequently tactical: the field jogs the first 1000 and sprints the last 300, and the winning time is slower than the prelims that fed it. The Atlantic 10 final was won in 4:03.61 while a prelim went 3:47.69. Dropping a 4:01 into that final produces "would have won it," which is nonsense — nobody races a tactical final on time. So every card leads with his placing among everyone who ran the event at the meet, prelims included, and flags a tactical final explicitly wherever the final's winning time is slower than the fastest round.

The same caution applies in the other direction. A program that enters nobody in its conference 1500 is not weak at it by that fact alone — it may simply race the 800 and the 5000 — but 29 of the 120 did exactly that, and six of those ran no 1500 anywhere all season. Each card says so plainly and suggests the question rather than assigning a verdict.

"Regional" means different things by division, and the pages say which. Division 1 has a real qualifying round above the conference meet — the NCAA East and West First Rounds — so a runner can appear there. Divisions 2 and 3 have no regional round in outdoor track at all: the conference meet feeds the national championship on descending-order marks, so a fast time anywhere counts and there is no round to miss. The NAIA is the same. Ten of the 120 programs had a 1500 runner in a postseason round of any kind, which is the honest scale of the thing: getting past the conference meet in this event is rare.

The decathlon's 1500 is excluded, and it was not excluded at first

TFRRS files the 1500 run at the end of a decathlon as a second "1500 Meters" event page at the same meet: no scoring column, seven to twenty men, and a winning time 40 to 90 seconds off the open race. The first build of this section merged both pages, because both are honestly labelled "Men's 1500 Meters" at that meet. The effect was to inflate every field size and to drop multi-eventers into teams' entrant lists — High Point appeared to have taken a 1500 runner to the NCAA Division 1 championships when what it took was a decathlete who ran 4:31.47 on the second day, and the Big South field read as 28 men rather than 21.

The rule now is: at each meet, keep only the 1500 page with the fastest winning time; any other 1500 page at that meet is the multi-event round. That removed 23 pages, cut the postseason cards from fifteen to nine of the programs then on file, and moved four programs into the "entered nobody" column because their only entrants had been decathletes. It changed no placing — every decathlon 1500 on file was slower than 4:01, so his rank was never affected, only the size of the field he was ranked in. Season depth charts are filtered the same way wherever the meet is one of the 39 in the cache; a stray decathlon mark from an ordinary April invitational may still sit at the bottom of a program's depth count, which affects how many men ran the event and not his slot.

Individual athletes are not named anywhere in this data, on the same basis as the cross country results (§6): times are what the comparison needs, identities are not. Names were used transiently to de-duplicate one man's several marks and then discarded. Every card links the TFRRS results page it was read from, so any figure can be checked at source.

8. The maps, and what the two distance numbers mean

Each metro page plots its schools on a Leaflet map with OpenStreetMap data. Two distances are in play and they are not the same measurement:

  • The "Mi" column in every table is approximate driving distance from the metro center — the number that matters for how often he gets home.
  • The dashed outline on each map is a straight-line boundary, because that is the only thing a drawn shape can be.

Driving distance is always the larger of the two, so a school sitting right on the line can plot just outside the boundary. On the New York map the boundary is not a circle at all, because the rule is not a radius: it is 20 miles from Midtown plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance. The map draws that rule literally — a 20-mile circle around Midtown unioned with a traced outline of the two Long Island counties — so Adelphi, Molloy, SUNY Old Westbury, Farmingdale State, St. Joseph's Long Island and Stony Brook plot inside the shape despite being well past 20 miles, as does Hofstra on the cut list. Long Island is included because it is a commutable corridor; Westchester and central New Jersey are excluded at the same distance because they are a different decision, and the shape shows that asymmetry rather than hiding it behind a caption. The Long Island outline is a simplified county boundary, not a coastline: it follows the Queens–Nassau line, runs offshore of the barrier beaches, and closes across Gardiners Bay, which correctly encloses both forks, Shelter Island and Plum Island. An earlier version of this map drew a 20-mile circle centred on a framing point out in Long Island Sound rather than on Midtown, which put the ring around open water.

Campus coordinates are approximate main-campus points, accurate to roughly a kilometre — enough to place a pin, not a routing engine. Every one was checked programmatically against its recorded driving distance; a coordinate implying a straight-line distance greater than the recorded road distance is impossible, and that check is what surfaced two bad pins in the first pass. It cannot be run on the 73 schools added at the wider radii, because their mileage is derived from the coordinate rather than measured independently. Where two schools sit within about a mile of each other — Concordia and Dominican in River Forest, NYU and Baruch, LIU and St. Joseph's in Brooklyn, Manhattan College and Mount Saint Vincent in Riverdale, and now several of the CUNY campuses — the pins are nudged roughly a mile apart so both stay clickable, and each popup says so. On the 300-mile Greenville map a pin covers several miles of ground at the default zoom, so the Atlanta, Charlotte, Columbia and Charleston clusters overlap without being nudged; zoom in to separate them.

9. Each school's own page, and the meet maps

Every school on the board has a page of its own, reached by clicking its name in any table or its pin on any map. It carries the federal cost and admissions record, the CS share, the full scoring seven from each cross country result with his projection dropped into place, the 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with the same projection dropped into that (§7), the coach to email, and a map of every meet that team travelled to in 2025–26, read off its own TFRRS results page and colored and lettered by season: X for cross country, I for indoor track, O for outdoor. Every school with a page has that map except Calumet College, which has no result on file for the season. Each page also ends with an explicit list of what is verified and what is missing for that school, so a gap reads as a gap rather than as an absence of a problem.

10. A note on the colors

The four fit tiers each carry a distinct shape and a text label, never color alone. That is deliberate: green and red are separated by an OKLab ΔE of about 4 under deuteranopia, which is far too close to carry meaning on their own. The gap chart uses a blue-to-red diverging pair with direct numeric labels on every bar for the same reason. The three season colors on the meet maps are the only three that clear the all-pairs colorblind separation floors in both light and dark mode (worst pair ΔE 9.2 and 9.4), and each pin carries its season's letter as well. Every palette was checked with a validator rather than by eye.
