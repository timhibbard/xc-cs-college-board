Methodology, conversions, and what this data cannot tell you

Every number on this site rests on a small set of conversions and one significant data limitation. Both are worth understanding before using any of it to make a decision.

1. The athlete's marks and how they convert

Converted to the events colleges actually report: 1500 in 4:03 · 3000 in 8:53 · track 5000 in roughly 15:15–15:25 · 8K cross country in 26:00–26:15. Everything on this site is scored against the midpoint of that 5000 range, 15:20. Using the slower end of the range shifts every gap by five seconds; it does not change any school's tier.

2. The framing rule — and the mistake it corrects

Being behind the team's best runner is the goal

For a projected 15:15–15:25 5000, 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best runner is the healthy target. It means training partners ahead of him and a realistic path to scoring by junior year. The aim is to be the 5th to 9th man as a freshman.

Arriving ahead of a team's #1 means the program is thin, not that he is a star. Distance runners develop in packs; there is no substitute for someone to chase in a Tuesday workout. The first version of this analysis treated "he'd be their #1" as a positive signal. That was backwards, and the tiers on this site reflect the corrected reading.

3. Where the times come from, and the limitation that matters

Roster marks come from TFRRS, the Track & Field Results Reporting System — the sport's system of record. Team pages there return the single fastest athlete, in one event, in the 2026 outdoor season only. Three consequences follow, and they are the most important caveats on this site:

  1. It is a floor, never a ceiling. A program whose distance runners focused on the 10K, the steeplechase, or the indoor season will show an artificially slow outdoor 5000. A slow number is weak evidence of a weak program; a fast number is strong evidence of a strong one.
  2. Sparse events return garbage. The mile, 3000, and 10,000 columns are unreliable — only the 5000 and 1500 can be trusted. The tell is internal contradiction: High Point's 1500 of 3:43.9 (about a 4:01 mile) sits beside a "best mile" of 4:21; Lenoir-Rhyne's 3000 of 8:47 sits beside a "best 1500" of 4:19; Catawba's 5000 of 14:37 sits beside a "best 3000" of 10:21. Those tables were empty, not slow.
  3. There is no team depth available. One athlete per event means no 5th man and no 7th man. "Gap to their #1" is the only comparison the data supports — which is why getting conference-championship results is the top task below.
A claim that was withdrawn

An earlier version of this analysis concluded that Clemson's distance corps was soft for the ACC, based on a 14:36 outdoor 5000. That was an artifact of a sparse table. Their 1500 best of 3:43 implies a runner around 14:15, and the conclusion has been retracted. Similar "only one distance runner" readings for Gardner-Webb and Guilford have been softened from established to suggestive for the same reason.

4. What "recruiting standards" actually means

Distance programs essentially never publish recruiting standards. The real benchmarks are (a) the times currently on the roster and (b) the time it takes to score at the conference championship. Approximate top-8 scoring 5000s, for calibration:

5. Contact rules, scholarships, and admissions leverage

Contact rules run one direction. D1 coaches may initiate contact on June 15 after sophomore year; D2 on July 15 after sophomore year; D3 is essentially unrestricted. But he may email any coach, in any division, at any time. The restriction limits coaches, not athletes.

There is no such thing as a full ride in men's distance running. D1 and D2 men's cross country and track share 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across a 35 to 45 man roster, so offers are quarter and half rides. Post-settlement, programs operate to roster limits. D3 offers no athletic aid at all. NAIA allows 12 scholarships with looser eligibility rules.

Academic aid frequently beats athletic aid. A strong student stacking merit aid at a less selective school can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer elsewhere. Model net cost, never scholarship percentage.

At selective schools, the coach is an admissions lever. Coaches at Davidson, Emory, NYU, Fordham, Stevens, and the University of Chicago get supported slots and will run an informal pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach whether he would likely be admitted. This is how running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not. Initiate in the spring of junior year.

Ask about lab scheduling at every school. Required CS lab blocks collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Large universities run several sections; a small college may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. The question — "how many of your athletes major in CS or engineering, and how do you handle lab conflicts?" — eliminates programs faster than times do.

6. Known gaps in this data

  1. No 5th or 7th man times anywhere. The highest-value missing input by a wide margin. Source: 2025 conference cross country championship results, which show every team's top seven. This is what settles the boundary cases — App State, UNC Charlotte, UIC, and NYU.
  2. CS degrees are unverified at most schools. Small-college websites were frequently unreachable, so those entries are marked Confirm rather than guessed at. Anderson (SC) is the specific case to watch: its site surfaces a BA in Applied AI and a cybersecurity center, but a traditional BS in Computer Science was not confirmed.
  3. SAT and acceptance figures are approximate and unverified, drawn from general knowledge rather than this cycle's data. Check each school's Common Data Set before relying on them.
  4. Several TFRRS pages could not be resolved, leaving schools with no times at all. The University of Chicago is the costliest of these — potentially the best combined fit in its metro, and currently a blank.
  5. Program existence needs confirming in two cases — whether NYIT still sponsors men's cross country, and whether each program carries full indoor and outdoor track rather than cross country alone.
  6. Web search was not available while building this. The geographic candidate lists were assembled from general knowledge, then verified against live TFRRS, athletics, and Wikipedia pages. A school could plausibly have been missed; the substance of what is listed was checked.

7. A note on the colors

The three fit tiers each carry a distinct shape and a text label, never color alone. That is deliberate: green and red are separated by an OKLab ΔE of about 4 under deuteranopia, which is far too close to carry meaning on their own. The gap chart uses a blue-to-red diverging pair with direct numeric labels on every bar for the same reason. The palette was checked with a validator rather than by eye.
