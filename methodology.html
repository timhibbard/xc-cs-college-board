Read before acting on anything here

Methodology, conversions, and what this data cannot tell you

Every number on this site rests on a small set of conversions, one comparison, and a handful of corrections that had to be made when better data arrived. All of it is worth understanding before using any of it to make a decision — particularly §3, which explains why four of this board's earlier recommendations were withdrawn. Two things changed most recently: every school but two now has a real cross country result behind its tier, which forced a rule for the 43 programs that never finished seven runners (§3a), and every school page now names the coach to email and says where that came from (§6).

1. The athlete's marks and how they convert

Converted to the events colleges actually report: 1500 in 4:01 · 3000 in 8:53 · track 5000 in roughly 15:15–15:25 · 8K cross country in 26:00–26:15. Everything on this site is scored against the midpoint of that 5000 range, 15:20. Using the slower end of the range shifts every gap by five seconds; it does not change any school's tier.

College men race 8K in cross country and 10K at most NCAA championship meets, so the projection has to be carried across distances to be compared to a scoring seven. From a 15:59 5K: 8K = 26:07, and 10K = 8K × 1.26 = 32:54. That 1.26 factor is not assumed — it was checked against teams that raced both an 8K conference championship and a 10K regional in 2025, and the two implied 8K-equivalents agreed within about seven seconds for Big South and Southern Conference squads.

1a. The pace conversions behind those numbers, and why the 1600 is not used

Every distance conversion on this site is a Riegel projection: T₂ = T₁ × (D₂/D₁)e, where e is a fatigue exponent. The textbook value is 1.06, fitted to trained adult distance runners. Run straight off the 1600, that exponent gives an answer that looks great and should not be believed:

A 4:21 1600 at the textbook exponent converts to a 14:33 5000 — nearly two minutes faster than he has actually run for 5K, and fast enough to make him a scoring runner at half the D1 programs on this board. It is wrong, and the way to see that it is wrong is to stop assuming the exponent and instead solve for his own, using pairs of marks he has actually run:

The 1600 flatters his 5K, so the board does not use it

His own exponent sits between 1.12 and 1.20 against a textbook 1.06. That is the signature of a speed-leaning runner still building aerobic mileage — which is normal for a high school junior and is not a criticism. But it means the further out from 1600m a projection is carried, the more it overstates him. Applying his exponent instead of the textbook one, 4:21 converts to about 16:08 for 5K — close to the 16:29 he has actually run, and 95 seconds off the 14:33 the textbook figure claims.

So the track 5000 of 15:20 used everywhere on this site is anchored to the 3200, not the 1600: projected 3200 of 9:29 → 3000 of 8:53 → 5000 of 15:20. Anchoring to the nearest race distance is the conservative choice and the defensible one. Checked backwards at the textbook exponent, a 15:30 5000 implies a 9:39 3200 and a 4:38 1600 — which is why a 15:20–15:30 5000 is consistent with his 3200 while a 14:33 is not.

Two practical consequences. First, a college coach shown only the 1600 will form a different picture than the data supports, so lead with the 3200 and the 5K. Second, if his exponent falls toward 1.10 as mileage goes up — the normal direction of travel — his 5K improves faster than his 1600 does, and every gap on this board narrows without the 1600 moving at all. That is the upside case, and it is a reason to email schools in the Deep tier.

1b. The 1500 — the one conversion the 1600 is right for

Colleges race 1500m, not 1600m, and 111 of the 131 programs on this board have an outdoor 1500 on file — marginally more than have a 5000 (107), and 107 of them have both. Comparing him there needs a 1500, and here the objection in §1a does not apply: a projection can only flatter a speed-leaning runner as it moves away from his speed, and 1500m is 100 metres shorter than the race he has actually run. The lean is an asset. So the 1500 on every school page is anchored to the 1600, at the textbook exponent, with no fudge factor:

1600 → 1500 at e = 1.06. The factor is (1500/1600)1.06 = 0.9339.

Two cross-checks. The coach's rule of thumb — subtract about 17 seconds from a 1600 to get a 1500 — gives 4:01 from 4:18, the same answer. And the arithmetic difference between the two rows, 2.8 seconds for a 3-second 1600 improvement, is the sanity check that the conversion is doing nothing clever: a 1500 is 94% of a 1600, so improvements pass through almost one for one.

Read the 1500 gap on its own scale, not the 5000's

A 1500 gap and a 5000 gap are not comparable in seconds. Holding the percentage constant, the healthy 40–60 second band at 5000 (4.4–6.7% of about 920 seconds) is 10 to 15 seconds at 1500. Twelve seconds behind a team's best 1500 is the same relationship as fifty behind their best 5000 — a squad with people to chase. Being ahead of their best 1500 means the same thing it means at 5000: the program is thin.

The 1500 is still a one-athlete, one-event, one-season number with every weakness described in §3. It is on the school pages because it is cheap, dense, and a second independent read on the same question — not because it outranks a cross country result.

2. The framing rule — and the mistake it corrects

Being behind the team's best runner is the goal

For a projected 15:15–15:25 5000, 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best runner is the healthy target. It means training partners ahead of him and a realistic path to scoring by junior year. The aim is to be the 5th to 9th man as a freshman.

Arriving ahead of a team's #1 means the program is thin, not that he is a star. Distance runners develop in packs; there is no substitute for someone to chase in a Tuesday workout. The first version of this analysis treated "he'd be their #1" as a positive signal. That was backwards, and the tiers on this site reflect the corrected reading.

3. Where the times come from, and the limitation that matters

Roster marks come from TFRRS, the Track & Field Results Reporting System — the sport's system of record. Team pages there return the single fastest athlete, in one event, in the 2026 outdoor season only. Three consequences follow, and they are the most important caveats on this site:

  1. It is a floor, never a ceiling. A program whose distance runners focused on the 10K, the steeplechase, or the indoor season will show an artificially slow outdoor 5000. A slow number is weak evidence of a weak program; a fast number is strong evidence of a strong one.
  2. Sparse events return garbage. The mile, 3000, and 10,000 columns are unreliable — only the 5000 and 1500 can be trusted. The tell is internal contradiction: High Point's 1500 of 3:43.9 (about a 4:01 mile) sits beside a "best mile" of 4:21; Lenoir-Rhyne's 3000 of 8:47 sits beside a "best 1500" of 4:19; Catawba's 5000 of 14:37 sits beside a "best 3000" of 10:21. Those tables were empty, not slow.
  3. Team pages carry no depth at all. One athlete per event means no 5th man and no 7th man. This was the largest gap on the site and it has now been closed for 129 of 131 schools — it was 37 — by going to a different source: individual meet result pages from the 2025 cross country season, which list every team's scoring seven by name and time. Where those exist they supersede the track marks entirely. Where they do not, the school sits in Verify and its tier should be read as a placeholder; only Calumet College and Shorter are left in that state, plus South Carolina on the cut list.

3a. How the cross country comparison is actually built

For every school with results, his projection is dropped into that team's finishing order and three numbers come out. All three are averaged across every race of theirs on file, and the per-race detail is on each school's own page:

  • Slot — where he would have finished inside their scoring seven. This is the headline number.
  • vs their 7th — his projection minus their 7th man. Negative means he makes the travel squad.
  • 1–7 spread — the gap from their #1 to their #7. This is the most robust number on the site, because it compares a team only to itself and is therefore immune to course, weather, and conversion error. A 40-second spread is a pack; a 300-second spread is a handful of runners and some bodies.

Teams with no 7th man — the rule that had to be added once everyone was measured

A cross country team scores five and travels seven, so every number above assumes seven finishers. Measuring all 131 schools showed that assumption is wrong far more often than expected: 43 of the 129 measured programs never finished seven runners in any 2025 championship race — 25 around Greenville, 13 around New York, 5 in Chicago. Guessing at the missing men would invent the exact number the board is built on, so instead:

  • Short races store what actually happened. A race with fewer than seven finishers records how many there were and reports his projection minus their last finisher in place of vs-7th. The school page says which it is, race by race, and vs their 7th is left blank in every table rather than filled with a substitute.
  • Fewer than five finishers is not a team result. Five is the minimum to score, so those races are excluded from the averaged slot, gap and spread entirely — 16 of the 212 races on file. They still appear on the school page, because "they entered three men and finished three" is the finding.
  • Five schools never finished five: Roosevelt, Benedict, John Jay, Mount Saint Vincent and York College (CUNY). Being the fastest man on a squad of four is the outcome this board exists to avoid, so all five sit at Caution regardless of how the times read.
  • One- and two-finisher races are read as individual qualifiers, not teams. Every such race on file is an NCAA regional, where a program that did not qualify as a team may still send one or two runners. Those are labelled as regionals on the school page and carry no team inference at all.

Individual athletes are deliberately not named anywhere on this site. Their times are what the comparison needs; their identities are not, and this is a public page. Every race is labelled with its meet and date, so any of it can be checked against the source.

For a measured school the tiers then follow mechanically: more than 45 seconds outside their 7th man is a cut; anything outside it is Deep; a slot of 2 or better is Caution, because he would arrive at or near their #1; and everything else is Target. Where there is no 7th man, the same ladder runs on the last finisher, which is strictly harder to clear.

Verify now means one thing only: no cross country result exists yet. An earlier version also assigned it to measured schools with a slot under 3.5, which conflated "borderline" with "unknown" and made the tier unreadable — a school could sit in Verify either because the data said something ambiguous or because there was no data at all. That band is retired; borderline measured schools land in Caution or Target on the ladder above, and Verify is a to-do list. It currently holds Calumet College and Shorter, plus South Carolina on the cut list.

Course difficulty, and why only two corrections were applied

Cross country courses are not comparable. A hilly 8K can run two minutes slower than a flat one, so comparing his projection to raw times mis-ranks whole conferences. The correction available here is that many teams raced both their conference championship and an NCAA regional, so the difference in their scoring-five averages isolates the course rather than the team — provided enough teams did it. Two meets cleared that bar:

MeetOffset at 8KTeams cross-checked
South Atlantic Conference championship−96.5s (course ran slow)Anderson, Catawba, Wingate
CCIW championship+31.4s (course ran fast)four CCIW teams

Single-team deltas were not applied — a CAA offset from Elon alone, or a MAAC offset from a single MAAC team alone, cannot be separated from that one team having a bad day. Sweeping the whole board raised the number of courses in play to roughly fifty and did not raise the number that clear the bar: two. Most of the rest were crossed by one or two teams. So every school whose only result comes from an uncorrected course carries more uncertainty than its tier suggests, and that is now most of the board rather than a footnote.

Getting this wrong is easy and the error is loud. A first pass had the sign inverted, which made the South Atlantic times slower and promoted Wingate from a cut straight to target tier. The fixed version reconciles all three South Atlantic teams' conference and regional reads to within a few seconds of each other, which is what makes the correction believable.

A method that was built, tested, and thrown away

An intermediate approach anchored his projection to each team's own 5000 data: take their #1's cross country time, then add the gap between his 5000 and their best 5000, scaled to 8K. It is appealing because it cancels out course difficulty entirely.

It is also systematically too generous, and the reason is the same flaw that runs through §3: a team's best outdoor 5000 is a floor, set by whichever athlete happened to race it, and that athlete frequently is not their cross country #1. DePaul's best 5000 is 14:22, but their cross country #1 ran the equivalent of 23:44; Hofstra's is 14:32 against a 23:01. The method moved DePaul from 7th man to 4th, ETSU from 4th to 2nd, Wheaton from 5th to 2nd, and Hofstra from 8th to 5th — always in the flattering direction. It was rejected in favour of raw comparison plus the two course corrections above.

Claims that were withdrawn

Clemson. An earlier version concluded that Clemson's distance corps was soft for the ACC, based on a 14:36 outdoor 5000. That was an artifact of a sparse table. Their 1500 best of 3:43 implies a runner around 14:15, and the conclusion has been retracted. Similar "only one distance runner" readings for Gardner-Webb and Guilford have been softened from established to suggestive for the same reason.

Lewis University. When the Chicago radius was 20 miles, this analysis recommended widening it to 30 specifically to reach Lewis, calling it "probably the single best fit in metro Chicago" — a claim made from general knowledge of the program, not from roster data. Once the radius was widened and the data pulled, their team best 5000 came back at 13:45.06, alongside a 29:58 10K and a 3:51 1500. Because the 13:45 was run at the Bryan Clay Invitational (a fast qualifying meet) and the 10K at a tactical conference championship, the marks are consistent rather than contradictory. Lewis is a 95 second gap and belongs in the cut list. The recommendation is withdrawn, and it is the most consequential reversal on this site.

Wheaton College — wrong twice, in opposite directions. First described as a "national-caliber D3 program" from general knowledge and cut on that basis; that was withdrawn when its outdoor table (5000 in 15:19.11, internally consistent across four events) failed to support it. Then a later pass declared the question resolved in Wheaton's favour and upgraded it to Target, which was also wrong. Their CCIW pack of 25:43–26:48 does put him around 5th man, but at the D3 Midwest regional their 4th through 7th ran 27:03–27:35 and he would have been their #2. Correct the CCIW course, which ran about 31 seconds fast, and the two races agree that he arrives near the front. Wheaton is Caution.

Hofstra and Loyola Chicago. Both were recommended as among the best fits in their metros, on outdoor 5000 marks of 14:32 and 14:23. Both then won their conference championships with sevens he would finish roughly 82 seconds outside — Hofstra's spanning 23:02–24:45, Loyola's just 47 seconds wide. Both are now cut.

North Central College — a withdrawal in the other direction. Cut on the reasoning that a perennial D3 national-championship program must be far beyond him. Their actual 7th man ran 25:34 at the CCIW meet and 25:46 at the regional, within about ten seconds of his projection. It is back on the board, in the Deep tier.

The pattern in all of them is worth naming: every one began as a confident claim from thin data — either general knowledge or a single fastest time — that a fuller result then contradicted. Treat any statement on this site not backed by a named scoring seven as provisional.

5. Contact rules, scholarships, and admissions leverage

Contact rules run one direction. D1 coaches may initiate contact on June 15 after sophomore year; D2 on July 15 after sophomore year; D3 is essentially unrestricted. But he may email any coach, in any division, at any time. The restriction limits coaches, not athletes.

There is no such thing as a full ride in men's distance running. D1 and D2 men's cross country and track share 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across a 35 to 45 man roster, so offers are quarter and half rides. Post-settlement, programs operate to roster limits. D3 offers no athletic aid at all. NAIA allows 12 scholarships with looser eligibility rules.

Academic aid frequently beats athletic aid. A strong student stacking merit aid at a less selective school can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer elsewhere. Model net cost, never scholarship percentage.

At selective schools, the coach is an admissions lever. Coaches at Davidson, Emory, NYU, Fordham, Stevens, and the University of Chicago get supported slots and will run an informal pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach whether he would likely be admitted. This is how running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not. Initiate in the spring of junior year.

Ask about lab scheduling at every school. Required CS lab blocks collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Large universities run several sections; a small college may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. The question — "how many of your athletes major in CS or engineering, and how do you handle lab conflicts?" — eliminates programs faster than times do.

6. Where the cost, admissions, and coach data come from

Every cost, SAT, admit-rate, and CS-program figure on this site now comes from the U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard — the federal collection that every institution is required to report to. All 143 schools on this site — 131 on the board and 12 on the cut list — are matched to exactly one federal record each, using an explicit name map rather than fuzzy matching, because fuzzy matching produced confident false positives at a similarity score of 1.0 (Georgia State matched to Georgia Tech, among six others).

That exercise was worth doing. It found 33 SAT ranges wrong by 60 points or more and nine admit rates wrong by 15 points or more versus the estimates this site previously showed. King University is open-admission at 100%, not ~70%; Fairfield is 33%, not ~57%; Wofford 52%, not ~70%; Lander 81%, not ~55%. Anyone who had used the old figures to decide where to apply would have been misled.

Two size figures sit beside the money on the Greenville table and on every school page. Undergraduate enrollment is from the same federal record, and every one of the 131 schools has it. Town population is not federal-education data at all — it comes from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey, table B01003, 2024 5-year estimates (2020–2024), read through the Census Reporter API and matched to the exact place each campus sits in. It is collected for the Greenville ring only: how big New York and Chicago are is not a question this board needs to answer, and a column that repeats the same eight-figure number down every row is noise.

Four caveats apply to reading them:

  • Public schools show the out-of-state rate, since he is a South Carolina resident. In-state figures apply only to the nine South Carolina publics on the list: Lander, USC Upstate, Winthrop, USC Aiken, SC State, Francis Marion, Coastal Carolina, the College of Charleston and The Citadel.
  • Average net price is an average across all incoming students after grants and scholarships — not a quote. A strong student stacking merit aid usually lands below it; it is a planning number, not a prediction.
  • Town population is the place, not the setting. Three of these figures are consolidated city-county governments — Augusta and Macon in Georgia, and Athens for the University of Georgia — so they are county-wide and read larger than what surrounds the campus. Four are census-designated places rather than incorporated towns (Buies Creek, Cullowhee, Tigerville, and Emory VA), where the boundary is a statistical convenience. And a small number inside a large metro describes the address rather than the experience: Morrow GA is 6,364 people and is Atlanta. Always read the town column next to the Mi column. Where it is useful is the opposite case, and there are five of them: at Montreat, North Georgia, Western Carolina, Kennesaw State and UNC Pembroke there are more undergraduates than town residents.
  • The CS share is CIP family 11 only. A computer science degree housed administratively under mathematics reads as zero — which is exactly what happened at SUNY Purchase (now out of range), where the real program was a combined BA in Mathematics/Computer Science. A low share is not proof of absence. Thirteen schools on the board report no CS degree and they are not all the same case. Three were checked by hand and disqualified on their own course catalogues: Mount Saint Vincent (no CS and no men's track), the Merchant Marine Academy (five marine majors and a service obligation), and North Park. Seven more carry an explicit warning on their own pages instead of a hand check — Brevard, Belmont Abbey, Young Harris, Queens (Charlotte), Allen, Oglethorpe and Emory & Henry all report 0% computing degrees and a live mathematics program, which at a small college is usually the signature of CS filed under mathematics rather than of no CS at all. Queens is the one that matters: it is the only Target-tier school on the board with no confirmed CS degree, so it is the only place this caveat could change a real decision. The last three — Lees-McRae, SUNY Maritime and Sarah Lawrence — report 0% and have not been checked either way; all three are thin-squad cautions on the running side, so the CS question is not what rules them out.

The coach on every school page

Each of the 143 school pages ends with a Who to email block: the name of the coach who would actually recruit a distance runner there, the title the school gives them, an office phone, and an email address. It is read off the employing school's own athletics staff directory and nowhere else — three platforms cover almost all of them (Sidearm Sports at /sports/<sport>/coaches or /staff-directory, PrestoSports at /information/directory/index, and a handful of hand-built department pages) — and each page links the exact directory it came from.

Four things about this data specifically:

  • Coaches are named; athletes are not. That is not an inconsistency. A head coach is a public professional whose employer publishes their name, title and work email in order to be contacted. The runners in a race result are mostly 18-to-22-year-olds who published nothing, and their times are all this comparison needs (§3a).
  • Who counts as "the coach" varies by division. At a large D1 program the right contact is the distance or cross country coach, not the director of track and field; at a small D3 or NAIA school one person often holds cross country, indoor and outdoor, sometimes alongside another sport. The title is printed verbatim rather than normalised, because the title is the signal about how much attention the event group gets.
  • Two schools publish no email at all — Fayetteville State and West Georgia. Their pages say so and point at the phone number or the team's own recruit form instead. Nothing was guessed from a first-initial-plus-surname pattern; a wrong address is worse than a missing one.
  • Sidearm obfuscates addresses in the page source, splitting them across two JavaScript variables that are concatenated in the browser, so an address that looks absent to a naive fetch is usually present. Reassembling those is the difference between the coverage above and roughly half of it.

This data decays faster than anything else on the site. Coaching staffs turn over every year, and these were read in August 2026. The link matters more than the name: if the name is stale, the directory behind it is not.

6a. Known gaps in this data

  1. Two schools still have no cross country results — down from 93, which was the largest gap on this site for most of its life. A tier of Verify means unmeasured, not borderline: it is a placeholder for a lookup nobody has done, and it is deliberately not a judgement. What is left is Calumet College (NAIA, and its CS program is unconfirmed too), Shorter, and South Carolina on the cut list. Shorter may be unmeasurable rather than unmeasured: it entered three men at its only 2025 meet and all three did not start.
  2. Depth is now measured, but only from championship races. The 212 races on file are conference championships and NCAA regionals, because those are the ones where a program fields its actual seven. That is the right sample for the question, and it also means most schools rest on one or two races, run on a course whose difficulty is usually uncorrected (§3a). A regular-season result would not change a tier, but a second championship result could, and 43 of the measured programs have a further problem on top of that: no 7th man to compare against at all.
  3. How the candidate lists were built, and why the earlier version of this claim was false. An earlier README said this board listed every school inside each radius that sponsors men's cross country. It did not. The lists were originally assembled from general knowledge and then verified individually, which validates what is present and says nothing about what is missing — and about 45 schools were missing from Greenville alone, including one 25 driving miles from home. The lists are now built the other way round: conference by conference, from each conference's own men's-sponsored-sports table, for every NCAA D1, D2 and D3 conference with a member inside a ring. That is a materially stronger guarantee, but it inherits the conferences' errors — a stale sponsored-sports table is wrong in both directions, and each new school's sponsorship was not re-confirmed on the school's own athletics site.
  4. NAIA and NCCAA coverage is incomplete and known to be. Only three non-NCAA programs are on the board, all of them NAIA — Saint Xavier and Calumet College in Chicago, and Montreat near Greenville. At least these NAIA and NCCAA programs in the Greenville footprint are not covered: Toccoa Falls, Truett McConnell, Brenau, Columbia International, Columbia College SC, Milligan, Johnson University, Reinhardt, Voorhees, Tennessee Wesleyan, Morris College, Bryan College, Bluefield University, Point University, Life University, Spartanburg Methodist and Warren Wilson. NAIA allows 12 scholarships for men's XC and track and has looser eligibility rules, so this gap is not academic — it is a gap in exactly the place where money is easiest to find.
  5. Driving distances for the newly added schools are estimates, not routes. The Mi column for schools already on the board was looked up. For the 73 added at the wider radii it is computed: straight-line distance × 1.18, or × 1.35 for the mountain states (Tennessee, Virginia, West Virginia, Kentucky), rounded to the nearest 5 miles. Those factors were calibrated against the existing board, whose implied road-to-straight-line ratios run from 1.01 to 1.47 with a median near 1.15. Expect ±15% on any one of them, which matters only for the handful sitting near the 250-mile line: Campbell, Savannah State and NC Central at an estimated 250 could each be over it.
  6. Cross country without men's track. Five programs on this board sponsor men's cross country and no men's indoor or outdoor track and field, and all five are in New York — John Jay, Brooklyn College, SUNY Maritime, Sarah Lawrence and SUNY Old Westbury. Not one has been identified around Greenville or Chicago, which almost certainly means the check is incomplete in those two metros rather than that the number there is zero. It is recorded in prose on each school's page because it is not in any dataset. For someone who wants both seasons it is close to disqualifying, so confirm it with the coach.
  7. Only two of roughly fifty championship courses have a difficulty correction. The 212 races on file span about fifty distinct conference and regional courses, and exactly two — the South Atlantic and CCIW championships — had enough teams racing both a conference meet and a regional to isolate the course from the team (§3a). Everywhere else the raw time is used. That is the largest remaining source of error on the board, and it is worst for schools whose only result came from a single uncorrected course: those could move a tier once a second race exists.
  8. Meet schedules were pulled for eleven schools — the target-tier list at the time, minus Fairfield, which the tighter New York ring has since removed — so the large majority of school pages have no meet map. Of the 289 appearances collected, 79% could be placed on a map; the 46 unplaced meets are almost entirely conference and NCAA championships whose host rotates annually, and each school page names its own unplaced meets rather than hiding them.
  9. Venue coordinates carry two provenance levels. Most were read off the meet's own results page; the rest were inferred from the host named in the meet title, which places the campus but not necessarily the course, and those are labelled host campus on the map and in the table. Two TFRRS venue records contained wrong postal codes (a New Hampshire fairground coded to Massachusetts, an Indiana course coded to Missouri); both were corrected by hand.
  10. Piedmont University's CS program is still unconfirmed — federal data codes the degree but reports roughly zero graduates, and the school's own site was unreachable. Calumet College is in the same position.
  11. Seven schools were removed from the board because they field no men's cross country team, and one because it is closing. Georgia State, NYIT, Montclair State and William Paterson were all carrying a Verify tier, which is precisely how that tier fails: it reads as a program awaiting measurement rather than one that does not exist. North Georgia was carrying the same tier and St. John's was sitting on the cut list; both sponsor women's cross country only, so there was never a men's program to verify or to cut. Northwestern is the seventh — it has no men's cross country program at all. Trinity Christian College announced on 4 November 2025 that it will close after the 2025–26 academic year. All eight are kept on the maps rather than deleted so the corrections stay visible. Seven errors of this kind have now surfaced; assume there are others.

7. The maps, and what the two distance numbers mean

Each metro page plots its schools on a Leaflet map with OpenStreetMap data. Two distances are in play and they are not the same measurement:

  • The "Mi" column in every table is approximate driving distance from the metro center — the number that matters for how often he gets home.
  • The dashed outline on each map is a straight-line boundary, because that is the only thing a drawn shape can be.

Driving distance is always the larger of the two, so a school sitting right on the line can plot just outside the boundary. On the New York map the boundary is not a circle at all, because the rule is not a radius: it is 20 miles from Midtown plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance. The map draws that rule literally — a 20-mile circle around Midtown unioned with a traced outline of the two Long Island counties — so Adelphi, Molloy, SUNY Old Westbury, Farmingdale State, St. Joseph's Long Island and Stony Brook plot inside the shape despite being well past 20 miles, as does Hofstra on the cut list. Long Island is included because it is a commutable corridor; Westchester and central New Jersey are excluded at the same distance because they are a different decision, and the shape shows that asymmetry rather than hiding it behind a caption. The Long Island outline is a simplified county boundary, not a coastline: it follows the Queens–Nassau line, runs offshore of the barrier beaches, and closes across Gardiners Bay, which correctly encloses both forks, Shelter Island and Plum Island. An earlier version of this map drew a 20-mile circle centred on a framing point out in Long Island Sound rather than on Midtown, which put the ring around open water.

Campus coordinates are approximate main-campus points, accurate to roughly a kilometre — enough to place a pin, not a routing engine. Every one was checked programmatically against its recorded driving distance; a coordinate implying a straight-line distance greater than the recorded road distance is impossible, and that check is what surfaced two bad pins in the first pass. It cannot be run on the 73 schools added at the wider radii, because their mileage is derived from the coordinate rather than measured independently. Where two schools sit within about a mile of each other — Concordia and Dominican in River Forest, NYU and Baruch, LIU and St. Joseph's in Brooklyn, Manhattan College and Mount Saint Vincent in Riverdale, and now several of the CUNY campuses — the pins are nudged roughly a mile apart so both stay clickable, and each popup says so. On the 250-mile Greenville map a pin covers several miles of ground at the default zoom, so the Atlanta, Charlotte, Columbia and Charleston clusters overlap without being nudged; zoom in to separate them.

8. Each school's own page, and the meet maps

Every school on the board has a page of its own, reached by clicking its name in any table or its pin on any map. It carries the federal cost and admissions record, the CS share, the full scoring seven from each cross country result with his projection dropped into place, and — for the eleven schools whose schedules were collected — a map of every meet the team travelled to last season, colored and lettered by season: X for cross country, I for indoor track, O for outdoor. Each page also ends with an explicit list of what is verified and what is missing for that school, so a gap reads as a gap rather than as an absence of a problem.

9. A note on the colors

The four fit tiers each carry a distinct shape and a text label, never color alone. That is deliberate: green and red are separated by an OKLab ΔE of about 4 under deuteranopia, which is far too close to carry meaning on their own. The gap chart uses a blue-to-red diverging pair with direct numeric labels on every bar for the same reason. The three season colors on the meet maps are the only three that clear the all-pairs colorblind separation floors in both light and dark mode (worst pair ΔE 9.2 and 9.4), and each pin carries its season's letter as well. Every palette was checked with a validator rather than by eye.
