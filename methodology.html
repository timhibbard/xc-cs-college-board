Methodology, conversions, and what this data cannot tell you

Every number on this site rests on a small set of conversions, one comparison, and a handful of corrections that had to be made when better data arrived. All of it is worth understanding before using any of it to make a decision — particularly §3, which explains why four of this board's earlier recommendations were withdrawn. He has run a new 5K cross country best of 15:40 — fifteen seconds faster than the 15:55 the previous version of this board was scored against, and nineteen inside the ~15:59 projection the whole cross-country chain was originally built on. It drops his solved fatigue exponent to 1.082, within 0.02 of the textbook value, from 1.196 two races ago; the 8K projection is now 25:36 and the 10K 32:40, and eight schools changed tier. §1c is where that was forecast before it happened, and it now also says why three of the eleven moves it predicted did not arrive. Recomputing every school against the 15:55 had already surfaced a real error — and a later audit against the source pages found the fix for it was wrong too: the 96.5 second South Atlantic course correction was being applied twice to seven programs, which had lifted five of them into Target on arithmetic alone. All five are back in Caution (§3b). Both changes are in. Before them, the board required both sports — men's cross country and men's outdoor track — which removed twenty-nine schools that sponsor only the first (§6b), and every school page now carries its team's 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projection dropped into it (§7). Before that, every school but one got a real cross country result behind its tier, which forced a rule for the 45 programs that never finished seven runners (§3a), and every school page names the coach to email and says where that came from (§6).

His own exponent now sits between 1.08 and 1.15 against a textbook 1.06, having been 1.12 to 1.20 two races ago. The sharper reading is what happens to the older conversion. His 1600→3200 exponent of 1.152 has not moved, because neither of those marks has. Carried out to 5K it predicts 16:10 — and he has now run 15:40. His 5K is 30 seconds ahead of what his own middle-distance marks predict, where two races ago it was 15 ahead and before that 19 behind. Read the other way: his 3200 at the textbook exponent predicts a 15:31 5K and he ran 15:40, so he is nine seconds off textbook from the 3200 against 24 at the 15:55. So the track 5000 of 15:20 used everywhere on this site is anchored to the 3200, not the 1600: projected 3200 of 9:29 → 3000 of 8:53 → 5000 of 15:20. Anchoring to the nearest race distance is the conservative choice and the defensible one. Checked backwards at the textbook exponent, a 15:30 5000 implies a 9:39 3200 and a 4:38 1600 — which is why a 15:20–15:30 5000 is consistent with his 3200 while a 14:33 is not.

The pressure the 15:55 put on that anchor has resolved, and in the anchor's favour. Run backwards at the 1.117 exponent that version of this page carried, a 15:40 5K implies a 3200 of 9:31 — within two seconds of the 9:29 the 15:20 is built on, where the 15:55 at its own old 1.196 exponent implied 9:20 and disagreed by nine. Two marks that used to argue now agree. There is still a faster number available from a more aggressive chain, and it is not used anywhere on this site, because it stacks a projection on top of a projection: it takes a mark he ran on grass, converts it to a distance he has not raced this year, and then converts it again. The 15:20 stays, corroborated from two directions instead of one.

Two practical consequences. First, a college coach shown only the 1600 will form a different picture than the data supports, so lead with the 3200 and the 5K. Second — and this is the part that has now happened — an earlier version of this page said that if his exponent fell toward 1.10 as mileage went up, his 5K would improve faster than his 1600 and every gap on this board would narrow without the 1600 moving at all, and called that the upside case and a reason to email schools in the Deep tier.

The upside case happened twice, and the second time it moved the board

The exponent has gone 1.196 → 1.117 → 1.082 across two races, and the 1600 has not moved at either. The first of those races barely touched the board: the 15:55 brought the 8K down only seven seconds (26:07 → 26:00), because the 8K was already built on a projected 15:59 rather than on the 16:29 he had actually run, and seven seconds moved two schools — Anderson (SC) from Deep to Target and Southern Wesleyan from Target to Caution.

The 15:40 is the one that paid. Once the projection has been beaten, every second of a new best passes straight through the conversion: fifteen seconds at 5K is 24 seconds at 8K (26:00 → 25:36) and 31 at 10K, and that moved eight schools — Clemson, Stony Brook, Charleston Southern, Lee University, NYU and the University of Chicago out of Deep into Target, and UNC Pembroke and Berry College out of Target into Caution, because he now arrives at or ahead of their #1. §1c has the arithmetic, including the three predicted moves that did not happen.

What the 15:55 changed was confidence rather than position — the board went from being scored against a projection to being scored against a result — and five further tier changes in that pass came from the course-correction error in §3b, not from the race. Worth keeping straight, because it would be easy to read the Target count as something a single race earned.

1b. The 1500 — the one conversion the 1600 is right for

Colleges race 1500m, not 1600m, and 128 of the 240 rows on this site have an outdoor 1500 on file — marginally more than have a 5000 (124), and 123 of them have both. Both counts stopped growing with the eight-metro sweep: none of the 62 rows it added has a track mark of any kind yet, which is why those pages are scored on cross country alone. Comparing him there needs a 1500, and here the objection in §1a does not apply: a projection can only flatter a speed-leaning runner as it moves away from his speed, and 1500m is 100 metres shorter than the race he has actually run. The lean is an asset. So the 1500 on every school page is anchored to the 1600, at the textbook exponent, with no fudge factor:

Read the 1500 gap on its own scale, not the 5000's

A 1500 gap and a 5000 gap are not comparable in seconds. Holding the percentage constant, the healthy 40–60 second band at 5000 (4.4–6.7% of about 920 seconds) is 10 to 15 seconds at 1500. Twelve seconds behind a team's best 1500 is the same relationship as fifty behind their best 5000 — a squad with people to chase. Being ahead of their best 1500 means the same thing it means at 5000: the program is thin.

The 1500 is still a one-athlete, one-event, one-season number with every weakness described in §3. It is on the school pages because it is cheap, dense, and a second independent read on the same question — not because it outranks a cross country result. §7 replaces it with something much better: the whole 1500 field at each team's 2026 conference championship, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season.

2. The framing rule — and the mistake it corrects

Being behind the team's best runner is the goal

For a projected 15:15–15:25 5000, 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best runner is the healthy target. It means training partners ahead of him and a realistic path to scoring by junior year. The aim is to be the 5th to 9th man as a freshman. Those seconds are 8K seconds: the same standing is 51 seconds at a 10K and 24 at a 5K, so every averaged gap on this site is converted to 8K-equivalent before it is held against this window — §3c has the arithmetic.

Arriving ahead of a team's #1 means the program is thin, not that he is a star. Distance runners develop in packs; there is no substitute for someone to chase in a Tuesday workout. The first version of this analysis treated "he'd be their #1" as a positive signal. That was backwards, and the tiers on this site reflect the corrected reading.

3. Where the times come from, and the limitation that matters

Roster marks come from TFRRS, the Track & Field Results Reporting System — the sport's system of record. Team pages there return the single fastest athlete, in one event, in the 2026 outdoor season only. Three consequences follow, and they are the most important caveats on this site:

  1. It is a floor, never a ceiling. A program whose distance runners focused on the 10K, the steeplechase, or the indoor season will show an artificially slow outdoor 5000. A slow number is weak evidence of a weak program; a fast number is strong evidence of a strong one.
  2. Sparse events return garbage. The mile, 3000, and 10,000 columns are unreliable — only the 5000 and 1500 can be trusted. The tell is internal contradiction: High Point's 1500 of 3:43.9 (about a 4:01 mile) sits beside a "best mile" of 4:21; Lenoir-Rhyne's 3000 of 8:47 sits beside a "best 1500" of 4:19; Catawba's 5000 of 14:37 sits beside a "best 3000" of 10:21. Those tables were empty, not slow.
  3. Team pages carry no depth at all. One athlete per event means no 5th man and no 7th man. This was the largest gap on the site and it has now been closed for 148 of the 150 schools that field a men's team — it was 37 — by going to a different source: individual meet result pages, which list every team's scoring seven by name, time and class year. Every comparable race of the 2025 and 2026 seasons is now read rather than one or two per school: 904 races, a median of six per school. Where those exist they supersede the track marks entirely. Where they do not, the school sits in Verify and its tier should be read as a placeholder; of those 150 only Calumet College (removed under the both-sports rule) is left in that state. Shorter (on the board, D2) is the other of the two with no averageable race, and it is a different finding: it holds no 2025 result because it has stopped racing, and the three-man championship field it last posted in 2024 is a measurement of the squad rather than an absence of one, so that row reads Caution set by hand.

3a. How the cross country comparison is actually built

For every school with results, his projection is dropped into that team's finishing order and three numbers come out. The per-race detail is on each school's own page, and the averages are kept in three separate groups rather than one pool — 2025 championships, 2025 invitationals, and the 2026 season so far — for the reason measured below:

  • Slot — where he would have finished inside their scoring seven. This is the headline number.
  • vs their 7th — his projection minus their 7th man. Negative means he makes the travel squad.
  • 1–7 spread — the gap from their #1 to their #7. This is the most robust number on the site, because it compares a team only to itself and is therefore immune to course, weather, and conversion error. A 40-second spread is a pack; a 300-second spread is a handful of runners and some bodies.

Why the tier rests on championships only, and what the other races are worth

The first version of this board read one or two championship races per school. Sweeping every comparable race of both seasons — 904 races: 303 championships, 475 invitationals, and 126 from the 2026 season — makes it possible to test the assumption behind that choice instead of asserting it. The test: for the 141 schools with both, compare his gap to their #1 at their championships against the same gap at their invitationals.

  • The invitationals flatter him by a median 14.5 seconds (mean 13.2; 91 of the 141 schools move in that direction). At an invitational a team's best man is running in a pack rather than for a place, and squads get rested and split. The shrinkage is the calendar, not the runner, so pooling the two groups would make every program on the board look closer than it is.
  • The slot barely moves at all: median difference zero places. His position inside a team is roughly level-independent even though the gap in seconds is not — which is one more reason the slot, not the gap, is the headline number.
  • So the tier uses the championships and nothing else, exactly as before. The invitationals are kept, averaged separately, and shown on each school page behind a fold: they are the best evidence there is about the men behind a team's front, because that is where a program's 5th through 9th runners actually race.

What counts as a championship, and the 24 races that were labelled wrong

Level is matched on the meet name, and the first sweep called anything containing the word "championship" a conference championship. That is wrong for a whole class of meet: IC4A/ECAC, the NEICAAA and Metropolitan championships, DIII North, and the Private College and Little Three Championships are open to whoever enters from a region, not to the members of one league. 24 races across 20 schools were being described on their own school pages as that school's conference championship when they were nothing of the kind — Fordham, Iona, Seton Hall and Hofstra worst affected.

  • They are now labelled area championship, and they still count in the championship group. That second half is a measurement rather than a preference: for the eight schools with both an area championship and their own conference meet at the same 8K, the two came out a median 3.9 seconds and 0.0 slots apart. Eight schools is a weak test and the individual schools swing hard in both directions — Iona's IC4A field was four slots softer than its MAAC meet, Seton Hall's Metropolitan three slots harder — but there is no systematic discount to apply, and these are raced rather than jogged, so throwing them out would discard evidence.
  • The NCCAA and USCAA championships moved to national championship, for the same reason inverted: those are governing associations with no conference above them, so their title meet is the top of their own pyramid rather than a league meet.
  • Not one aggregate changed. The relabelling moved races between labels inside the championship group, never in or out of it, so every slot, gap and spread on the board is the same number it was before. The finding is that the pages were saying something untrue about 24 races, not that the tiers were wrong.

8K and 10K are not the same measurement — two errors, both now fixed

A conference meet is usually 8K and an NCAA regional is always 10K, so most schools' championship average spans both distances: 68 of the 148 with races on file, and 104 of them once the invitationals are counted too. Reading a season that mixes distances turned out to involve two separate mistakes, and this section is what they were, what they were worth, and what is left.

  • The 10K projection itself was too fast. It came off 8K × 1.26, and 1.26 is below the entire range teams actually produce. The 10K is now 32:40 where the old factor would give 32:15 — a 25-second correction at this anchor as at the last one, derived and cross-checked in §1.
  • Seconds at 8K and seconds at 10K were being averaged as if they were the same unit. They are not. The same standing against a team is 40 seconds at 8K, 51 at 10K and 24 at a 5K, because his time and theirs both grow with the race. Every averaged gap on this board is now converted to 8K-equivalent first — each race's gap multiplied by his 8K projection over his projection for that race's distance (a 10K gap × 0.784, a 5K gap × 1.634), which is the same as reading the gap as a share of his own projected time. Per-race numbers are untouched: each race card compares him against the projection for the distance that was run, which was always right.

What is left is real, and it is about their 7th man rather than about him. Even with both fixes, his gap to a team's 7th man still reads a median 26 seconds kinder at 10K (36 of the 49 in that direction). The reason is measurable: on the 84 board schools with their own conference 8K and their own 10K regional, a team's #1 runs the 10K at 1.277× his 8K, and its 7th man at 1.297× — the back of a scoring seven fades harder over the extra 2K than its leader does, with the team score already settled and the man buried in a 30-team field. Their 1–7 spread runs 24 seconds wider at 10K for the same reason — which is exactly what those two ratios predict, 1.297 against 1.277 applied across an 80-second spread — and his slot moves a median 0.00 places, which is the whole argument for the slot being the headline. That 26 seconds is not folded into his projection, because it is a fact about how their seventh man races a 10K, not about how he would.

No race time changed and no race was added or dropped: 68 of the 148 school aggregates are re-derived by the currency conversion, every school whose championship pool is not all-8K. The 15:40 has taken both of them back inside it — 29.4 and 25.5 seconds outside their seventh man — so that cost has been refunded by a race rather than by an argument, and both keep the deep tier they have carried throughout.

Class years, and what a team looks like after its seniors go

Every finisher's class year is now read off the results page alongside the time — 5,775 of 5,798 finishers, 99.6%, with 894 of the 904 races fully labelled. A gap in a team's seven that closes when three seniors graduate is a different opportunity from the same gap in a team of freshmen, and no time comparison can tell the two apart.

  • The class mix is read off one race, not pooled. The same seven men appear in every race a team runs, and no athlete names are stored here to deduplicate by, so pooling would count one sophomore five times. The most recent season wins, because its years are the current ones; within that season the deepest race wins, because it shows the most of the squad. For 100 of the 144 schools with a class mix that reference race is from 2026.
  • One race is only the men who ran that day, and the page says so. Mount Saint Vincent's last 2025 race was five seniors with its freshmen sitting out, which alone would read as a team with nobody coming back. So the largest count of each class seen in any other race of that season is carried as a floor and shown when it beats the reference race — "at least 2 freshmen and 1 junior also raced for this team." It is a floor and never a total: without names there is no way to know whether the two freshmen in one race are the two in another. 45 of the 144 carry such a floor.
  • Incoming freshmen are invisible in all of this. It counts men who have already raced, which is the only thing a results page can support. So is a transfer who has not yet run, and so is anyone who quits in April.
  • Names are still not stored anywhere. A class year is not identifying; a name is. The years are read from the same row as the time and the name in that row is discarded.

Teams with no 7th man — the rule that had to be added once everyone was measured

A cross country team scores five and travels seven, so every number above assumes seven finishers. Measuring all 155 schools showed that assumption is wrong far more often than expected: 40 of the 148 measured programs never finished seven runners in any 2025 championship race, and 113 of the 303 championship races on file ended short of seven. Guessing at the missing men would invent the exact number the board is built on, so instead:

  • Short races store what actually happened. A race with fewer than seven finishers records how many there were and reports his projection minus their last finisher in place of vs-7th. The school page says which it is, race by race, and vs their 7th is left blank in every table rather than filled with a substitute.
  • Fewer than five finishers is not a team result. Five is the minimum to score, so those races are excluded from the averaged slot, gap and spread entirely — 58 of the 447 championship races on file. They still appear on the school page, because "they entered three men and finished three" is the finding. No invitational or 2026 race on file is that short, which is itself a sign of what a championship entry means: teams turn up to their conference meet with whoever is left.
  • Ten schools never finished five: Roosevelt, Benedict, John Jay, Mount Saint Vincent, York College (CUNY), Marymount, Carlow, Hilbert, Greensboro College and William Peace — the last two off the board on the both-sports rule, but carrying a published cross country tier all the same. Being the fastest man on a squad of four is the outcome this board exists to avoid, so all ten sit at Caution regardless of how the times read — the last five only after a correction, because with no aggregate to average they had fallen through to Verify, which would have called a measured thin squad an unmeasured one.
  • Most one- and two-finisher races are read as individual qualifiers, not teams — but the level decides that, not the field size. There are 36 of them and 32 are an NCAA regional (14) or a national championship (18), meets a program that did not qualify as a team may still send one or two runners to; those are labelled with their level on the school page and carry no team inference at all. The other four are conference championships (Marymount, Carlow, Hilbert and Greensboro College), and there the inference runs the opposite way: a conference meet is the race a program brings everyone to, so turning up with one or two men is the measurement of its depth. Those rows are still excluded from the averages, because two men cannot be averaged into a team result, but they are tiered on the finding rather than parked at Verify.

Individual athletes are deliberately not named anywhere on this site. Their times are what the comparison needs; their identities are not, and this is a public page. Every race is labelled with its meet and date, so any of it can be checked against the source.

For a measured school the tiers follow this ladder: more than 45 seconds outside their 7th man is a cut; anything outside it is Deep; a slot of 2 or better is Caution, because he would arrive at or near their #1; and everything else is Target. Where there is no 7th man, the same ladder runs on the last finisher, which is strictly harder to clear.

3b. The correction that was applied twice

Seven of the ten South Atlantic races got the −96.5 second correction counted twice, and it reversed five tiers

An earlier version of this section reported the opposite problem: that seven South Atlantic programs never received the correction at all, that their times “sat in the tables raw,” and that fixing it lifted five schools out of Caution into Target. The correction went in. It went in twice.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it is the kind of bug a regeneration check is supposed to catch and did not. Every race object on this site stores the finishers' raw page times in runners and the course offset separately in corr; the gap to their #1 is then derived as projection − (runners[0] + corr). The fix for those seven races wrote the offset into the runners array — the stored times were already 96.5 seconds faster than the page — and left corr: −96.5 sitting on the object. So the derivation subtracted it again. Anderson, Catawba and Wingate, the three teams whose conference-to-regional deltas were used to derive the offset, stored raw times all along and were always right.

The direction is the same one that has caught this project out four times before. Double-counting a correction that makes times faster makes a team look deeper than it is, and every one of the seven gaps was inflated by exactly 96.5 seconds — the giveaway that this was arithmetic rather than a data problem.

A method that was built, tested, and thrown away

An intermediate approach anchored his projection to each team's own 5000 data: take their #1's cross country time, then add the gap between his 5000 and their best 5000, scaled to 8K. It is appealing because it cancels out course difficulty entirely.

It is also systematically too generous, and the reason is the same flaw that runs through §3: a team's best outdoor 5000 is a floor, set by whichever athlete happened to race it, and that athlete frequently is not their cross country #1. DePaul's best 5000 is 14:22, but their cross country #1 ran the equivalent of 23:44; Hofstra's is 14:32 against a 23:01. The method moved DePaul from 7th man to 4th, ETSU from 4th to 2nd, Wheaton from 5th to 2nd, and Hofstra from 8th to 5th — always in the flattering direction. It was rejected in favour of raw comparison plus the two course corrections above.

5. Contact rules, scholarships, and admissions leverage

Contact rules run one direction. D1 coaches may initiate contact on June 15 after sophomore year; D2 on July 15 after sophomore year; D3 is essentially unrestricted. But he may email any coach, in any division, at any time. The restriction limits coaches, not athletes.

There is no such thing as a full ride in men's distance running. D1 and D2 men's cross country and track share 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across a 35 to 45 man roster, so offers are quarter and half rides. Post-settlement, programs operate to roster limits. D3 offers no athletic aid at all. NAIA allows 12 scholarships with looser eligibility rules.

Academic aid frequently beats athletic aid. A strong student stacking merit aid at a less selective school can land below the net cost of a bigger athletic offer elsewhere. Model net cost, never scholarship percentage.

At selective schools, the coach is an admissions lever. Coaches at Davidson, Emory, NYU, Fordham, Stevens, and the University of Chicago get supported slots and will run an informal pre-read: send transcript and scores, and admissions tells the coach whether he would likely be admitted. This is how running unlocks a school his transcript alone might not. Initiate in the spring of junior year.

Ask about lab scheduling at every school. Required CS lab blocks collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Large universities run several sections; a small college may offer a required course in exactly one afternoon slot. The question — "how many of your athletes major in CS or engineering, and how do you handle lab conflicts?" — eliminates programs faster than times do.

6. Where the cost, admissions, and coach data come from

Every cost, SAT, admit-rate, and CS-program figure on this site now comes from the U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard — the federal collection that every institution is required to report to. All 226 schools with a men’s program on this site — 186 on the board, 12 on the cut list and 29 that field cross country without men’s outdoor track — are matched to exactly one federal record each, using an explicit name map rather than fuzzy matching, because fuzzy matching produced confident false positives at a similarity score of 1.0 (Georgia State matched to Georgia Tech, among six others).

That exercise was worth doing. It found 33 SAT ranges wrong by 60 points or more and nine admit rates wrong by 15 points or more versus the estimates this site previously showed. King University is open-admission at 100%, not ~70%; Fairfield is 33%, not ~57%; Wofford 52%, not ~70%; Lander 81%, not ~55%. Anyone who had used the old figures to decide where to apply would have been misled.

Every admit rate re-checked, one row at a time

Because a single wrong admit rate can move a school from "apply" to "do not bother", all of them were re-verified against the live Scorecard rather than trusted from the earlier import. Each of the 150 rows with a men's program was queried by its own IPEDS unit ID and its published percentage compared to the federal admission_rate.overall field. 148 numeric rates match the federal figure exactly, to the rounded percentage point, with zero mismatches. Two things came out of the exercise that were worth changing.

  • Three rows said “not reported” and should not have. Savannah State, Felician and Calumet College return no admission_rate.overall, which is why they were blank — but all three carry open_admissions_policy = 1 and a consumer admission rate of 1.0 in the same federal record. That is not missing data; it is an open-admission school, which is a meaningful and favourable fact rather than an absence. All three now read 100%, which is the same convention two other rows on the board already used, and every one of the 150 rows now carries a federal admit rate.
  • Two rows legitimately share one federal record. St. Joseph's Long Island (Patchogue) and St. Joseph's Univ NY (Brooklyn) both map to IPEDS 195544. That is correct rather than a matching error: the two are one institution — St. Joseph's University, New York — reporting federally as a single unit while fielding two separate NCAA programs. The consequence is that the admit rate, SAT range, net price and enrollment shown on both pages describe the combined university, not the campus named at the top of the page. Neither row is on the board (both sponsor cross country without men's outdoor track), so nothing on the ranking depends on it, but the two pages should not be read as two independent measurements.

Two size figures sit beside the money on the Greenville table and on every school page. Undergraduate enrollment is from the same federal record, and every one of the 185 schools on the board has it. Town population is not federal-education data at all — it comes from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey, table B01003, 2024 5-year estimates (2020–2024), read through the Census Reporter API and matched to the exact place each campus sits in. It is collected for the Greenville ring only: how big New York and Chicago are is not a question this board needs to answer, and a column that repeats the same eight-figure number down every row is noise.

Four caveats apply to reading them:

  • Public schools show the rate he would actually pay — the out-of-state rate, since he is a South Carolina resident. In-state figures apply only to the eleven South Carolina publics on the site: Clemson, South Carolina, Coastal Carolina, the College of Charleston, The Citadel, Winthrop, Lander, USC Upstate, USC Aiken, Francis Marion and SC State. Which rate a school shows is a fact about the school, not a setting.
  • Average net price is an average across all incoming students after grants and scholarships — not a quote. A strong student stacking merit aid usually lands below it; it is a planning number, not a prediction. At a public school the federal average is also computed from in-state students only, which is not the basis that applies to him — see how residency enters the net cost below.
  • Town population is the place, not the setting. Three of these figures are consolidated city-county governments — Augusta and Macon in Georgia, and Athens for the University of Georgia — so they are county-wide and read larger than what surrounds the campus. Four are census-designated places rather than incorporated towns (Buies Creek, Cullowhee, Tigerville, and Emory VA), where the boundary is a statistical convenience. And a small number inside a large metro describes the address rather than the experience: Morrow GA is 6,364 people and is Atlanta. Always read the town column next to the Mi column. Where it is useful is the opposite case, and there are five of them: at Montreat, Western Carolina, Kennesaw State, Clemson and UNC Pembroke there are more undergraduates than town residents.
  • The CS share is CIP family 11 only. A computer science degree housed administratively under mathematics reads as zero — which is exactly what happened at SUNY Purchase (now out of range), where the real program was a combined BA in Mathematics/Computer Science. A low share is not proof of absence. Twelve schools on the board report no CS degree, and they are not all the same case. One of the twelve is Target tier on the running, which is where this caveat could change a real decision: Queens (Charlotte). Queens reports 0% computing degrees and a live mathematics program, which at a small college is usually the signature of CS filed under mathematics rather than of no CS at all, and its page says so rather than asserting the degree does not exist. Emory & Henry was the second such row for exactly one revision of this site: the §3b course correction appeared to move it into Target, and the follow-up audit found that correction had been counted twice. It is back in Caution on the running, so its CS question is moot again — and it is a good illustration of why this caveat is written in terms of which tier a row sits in rather than in terms of a fixed list. Four were checked by hand and disqualified on their own course catalogues: the Merchant Marine Academy (five marine majors and a service obligation), North Park, Warren Wilson (a Data Science major and no computer science one, which is the closest miss on the board), and Mount Saint Vincent, which is off the board anyway for having no men's track. Wingate is the twelfth and the only one at Deep: it reports 0% and has not been checked either way, and the running is the reason it is not a target. Five more carry the same explicit warning as Queens instead of a hand check — Brevard, Belmont Abbey, Young Harris, Allen University and Oglethorpe — and Lees-McRae reports 0% and has not been checked either way; all six are thin-squad cautions on the running side, so the CS question is not what rules them out. Two schools removed under the both-sports rule, SUNY Maritime and Sarah Lawrence, report none as well.

How residency enters the net cost

This is the one place where two federal figures sitting next to each other are measured on different bases, so it is worth stating plainly. The tuition line on a school page is the rate that would apply to him: the in-state rate at the eleven South Carolina publics, the out-of-state rate at the other 52 publics, and the single rate every student pays at a private. The average net price line is a different federal field, and at a public institution the Scorecard computes it from in-state students only. Out-of-state students are not in that average at all.

So at an out-of-state public the two numbers describe two different people, and the net price is the optimistic one. Stony Brook’s federal net price is $18,784 — for a New Yorker. Its own residents pay $10,931 in tuition and everyone else pays $32,741. Reading $18,784 as what he would pay understates it by the whole $21,810 gap.

Every page that shows a net price for an out-of-state public therefore shows federal net price + (out-of-state tuition − in-state tuition), marked with a ≈ and a dotted underline to say it is an estimate rather than a reported figure. That arithmetic holds average grant aid constant and moves only the tuition, which is the only part of the bill that depends on residency — room and board do not. It applies to 49 of the 186 schools on the board (24 around Greenville, 12 around New York — which now includes the three New Jersey publics the retired Newark page used to count, NJIT, Kean and Rutgers–Newark — three in Baltimore, two each in Philadelphia, Washington and Buffalo, one each in Chicago, Boston, Pittsburgh and Hampton Roads) and to 68 publics site-wide. Two publics are unaffected because they charge one rate to everyone: Chicago State and the Merchant Marine Academy. Privates are unaffected by definition.

The correction is not cosmetic. It moves the board's median net price from $18,831 to $20,994 — and from $19,244 to $22,969 around New York, where most of the cheap options are public. It cuts “targets under $15,000” from nine schools to three: Coastal Carolina ($13,966, in-state South Carolina), Fayetteville State (≈$11,892) and UNC Pembroke (≈$14,260) survive. UNC Asheville, UNC Greensboro, Chattanooga, West Georgia, Augusta University and UVA Wise all read under $15,000 on the raw federal figure and none of them does on his residency. The largest single moves on the board are Georgia Tech ($12,116 → $34,542), Auburn ($24,323 → $46,355) and Stony Brook ($18,784 → $40,594).

Three things could make the estimate too high, and all three are worth asking about:

  • A non-resident tuition waiver. Some publics can waive part of the premium for recruited athletes, and some do it academically by merit threshold. Where that happens the added amount disappears in whole or in part. It is a coach-and-financial-aid question, not something a federal file can answer.
  • Regional tuition agreements. The Academic Common Market can grant in-state rates to a South Carolina student in a specific major not offered in-state; computer science will not usually qualify, but the mechanism exists.
  • Athletic money. Division I and II programs can award it; Division III cannot, and 63 of the 186 schools on this board are Division III (against 64 D1, 55 D2, three NAIA and one USCAA) — the eight-metro sweep added 35 D3 rows in one pass and made Division III the second-largest block on the board. Where it exists in cross country it is normally partial and split many ways.

What could make it too low: a non-resident whose family qualifies for less need-based aid than the in-state average would pay more than the estimate, not less. The estimate is a planning midpoint on his residency, and the sticker price above it is still the ceiling.

The coach on every school page

Each of the 228 school pages that has a coach ends with a Who to email block: the name of the coach who would actually recruit a distance runner there, the title the school gives them, an office phone, and an email address. It is read off the employing school's own athletics staff directory and nowhere else — three platforms cover almost all of them (Sidearm Sports at /sports/<sport>/coaches or /staff-directory, PrestoSports at /information/directory/index, and a handful of hand-built department pages) — and each page links the exact directory it came from. That is now every row on the site that has a men's program — all 227 — plus South Carolina, which sponsors men's track and no men's cross country. The last 76 of them, everything the eight-metro sweep added, were read in September 2026; 226 of the 228 publish an email and 148 publish a direct office phone.

Seven things about this data specifically:

  • Coaches are named; athletes are not. That is not an inconsistency. A head coach is a public professional whose employer publishes their name, title and work email in order to be contacted. The runners in a race result are mostly 18-to-22-year-olds who published nothing, and their times are all this comparison needs (§3a).
  • Who counts as "the coach" varies by division. At a large D1 program the right contact is the distance or cross country coach, not the director of track and field; at a small D3 or NAIA school one person often holds cross country, indoor and outdoor, sometimes alongside another sport. The title is printed verbatim rather than normalised, because the title is the signal about how much attention the event group gets.
  • Two schools publish no email at all — Fayetteville State and West Georgia. Their pages say so and point at the phone number or the team's own recruit form instead. Nothing was guessed from a first-initial-plus-surname pattern; a wrong address is worse than a missing one. Two more name a head coach but publish only a program inbox for them — Tufts (xctf@tufts.edu) and John Jay — and their pages say that too, because a mail to a shared inbox needs the coach named in the first line to get read.
  • Sidearm obfuscates addresses in the page source, splitting them across two JavaScript variables that are concatenated in the browser, so an address that looks absent to a naive fetch is usually present. Reassembling those is the difference between the coverage above and roughly half of it.
  • The title carries the bad news. Where the directory does not simply name a men's cross country head coach, the title field says what it does say instead of promoting whoever was listed. East Tennessee State is the pattern — an assistant, with the head job listed vacant — and the sweep found six more of them: Marymount, where the only two names under cross country are graduate assistants and no head is listed; Rosemont, which lists a men's and women's track and field staff and no cross country coach at all; Temple, where an assistant is the only coach the directory attaches to men's cross country; Carolina University, which names a track and field head and no cross country head; and Lesley and Greensboro, both interim. At the large programs the same field names the person who was not recorded and why — Harvard's director of track and field above its distance coach, Northeastern's endowed head above its distance lead, Saint Joseph's recruiting coordinator beside its head — because which of two names to write to is exactly the thing a recruit cannot tell from the outside.
  • An extension is not a phone number. Villanova and Morgan State publish bare four-digit extensions with no prefix anywhere on the page, so those rows carry no phone rather than a composed one. George Washington looks identical and is not: its column header reads Phone (202), which is the prefix, published.
  • The staff directory wins when it disagrees with the sport's own coaches page, for the reason Radford gives: its /sports/mens-cross-country/coaches is a 2017 archive that still names a staff nine years gone and renders exactly like a current page, so a row sourced there would have sent mail to two people who left before this athlete was in middle school. Two rows of the 76 go the other way and say why in the title. Hilbert's directory omits cross country entirely, so its source is the sport's own coaches page; Carlow's coaches page names the coach but publishes no address, so its source is the directory. A search snippet naming a different head coach was not enough to move a row either: Chatham came up that way and the live directory's name was kept.

This data decays faster than anything else on the site, and there is now a measured rate for it. The first 151 were read in August 2026 and every one of the 150 with a men's program was re-checked against the same directories in September 2026: five names had already changed in about a month (see below), and three more turned up on a second look at the pages themselves. The 76 rows the eight-metro sweep added were read in September 2026 and have not yet had a re-check, which means the youngest column on this site is also the one with no decay measurement of its own. The link matters more than the name: if the name is stale, the directory behind it is not.

The coach's own Instagram — 17 of the 150 searched, and why that number is honest

The program handles elsewhere on this site are read off school pages (§6a). A coach's personal account is never on those pages: all 150 staff directories were scanned for one and not a single school links a coach's personal Instagram. Instagram itself cannot be queried either — a profile page returns a login wall to anything that is not a browser. So this column could only be built by search, one coach at a time: a site:instagram.com "<coach name>" <school> query against Google's index, a plain-language fallback query where that returned no profile, and then a third query on each surviving handle by itself, because a search on a person's name truncates the bio while a search on the handle returns it in full. That bio is the evidence, and each accepted row stores the words it rests on.

The standard is the whole story here. A handle is recorded only when the account itself ties the person to this program: the name in the handle, the name on the account, the school named in the account's own bio or its posts, and a coaching or running word. A matching name is never enough on its own. Under that rule, of 150 coaches searched, 43 produced at least one plausible candidate and 17 were accepted — 14 of the 186 rows on the board, and 7 of the 47 at target tier. No coach was searched for at all on the 76 rows the eight-metro sweep added, so that column is silent there rather than empty.

The rejections are the reason to trust the acceptances:

  • Nine different accounts belong to a “Chris Bailey” and eight to a “Mark Elliott”, the coaches at the College of Charleston and Clemson. Not one of the seventeen says anything about coaching, running, or either school. Picking the plausible one would have meant publishing a stranger's account under a coach's name.
  • Three accounts belong to a “Sterling Jones”, ETSU's coach. The one with 20,000 followers has no bio at all; another sells a food business. None of them coach.
  • @riley.macon is the right name for UNC Charlotte's coach, has nine posts and no bio, and proves nothing. @mark_elliston has zero posts. Both were rejected.
  • @coachmattlogan is the instructive one: right name, right sport, wrong school — and it turned out the school was wrong on this site, not in the bio (above).

What an empty cell means, precisely: either the coach keeps no public account, or nothing found identifies one as theirs. It does not mean there is none. The search can only see a coach whose handle contains part of their own name or who is named in someone else's caption, so a coach posting under an unrelated handle is invisible to it — and about a third of the searches returned no Instagram result of any kind, which for coaches in their fifties and sixties at small colleges is a believable answer rather than a failure. One accepted account, Duke's, is private: the handle is right and the bio is public, but nothing behind it is readable without being let in.

Read these; do not write to them. A coach's account is the most useful window on the site — who they signed, what they say about their distance group, whether they post about running at all — and the worst possible channel for a recruiting question. A direct message is off the record, easy to miss, and reaches the person as a person rather than as a coach. The email address in the same table is the one that gets an answer.

The program's Instagram on the last 76 rows — read off the sport page, never searched for

The 76 rows the eight-metro sweep added were the last rows on this site with an empty Instagram cell, and an empty cell there meant “not looked up” rather than “none exists” — the only to-do left masquerading as a finding. All 76 have now been read the same way the first 150 were: off the school's own men's cross country page, never by searching Instagram for the school's name. A name search is what produces confident nonsense — on a Wingate or a Manhattan it returns accounts with no connection to the team — and it is not used here at all. 53 of the 76 have a program account, 21 link only their athletics department's, and two link no Instagram account anywhere on their own pages.

What made this readable rather than a guess is that athletics sites say which sport owns a handle, in one of three shapes. In order of how directly they say it:

  • The sport record — 37 rows. A Sidearm page publishes the record for the sport it is about, and that record has an instagram field. The men's cross country page's own record naming @stevensontfxc is the sport itself saying so, and it is the strongest evidence available short of asking the coach.
  • The link's own label — 9 rows. Both platforms label social links for screen readers: aria-label="Men's Cross Country Instagram". That is how Carnegie Mellon's @carnegiemellon_xctf and Hampton's @hupirates_track were read.
  • The sport navigation block — 6 rows. Where neither of those exists, each sport's social links still sit inside that sport's own block of the navigation, so the nearest sport title above a handle names its owner. PrestoSports makes this easier by serving one page per sport: a handle in the navigation of /sports/mxc/ is the men's cross country account, which is how La Roche's @lru_xc and Moody Bible's @archerscrosscountry were read.
  • The page's own copy — 1 row. Boston College links no handle at all, but a video on its men's cross country page carries the team's description of itself: “Follow us on social for all things Boston College Track and Field”, and then the account, @BCXC_TF. It is the weakest of the four and it is still the program naming its own account on its own page.

Counting how often a handle appears would have got this wrong, and Arcadia is the proof. On Arcadia's men's cross country page @arcadia_tf appears twelve times and @Arcadia_XC six — and the twelve belong to men's indoor track. The frequent handle is usually the one the site templates into every sport's page; the correct one is whichever block says cross country. Babson is the case that decided the rule's edge: its men's cross country record names the department account, @BabsonAthletics, while its men's track and field block links @BabsonXCTrack. That is an account of the program's own, so the row carries it and is not counted as a fallback.

The 21 fallbacks are a finding, not a shortfall. At every one of them the entire athletics site links exactly one Instagram account — the department's — and eleven go further and publish an empty instagram field on the men's cross country record itself, which is a school stating that the sport has no account rather than a page that failed to load. Those rows carry the department handle with a degree sign, and the school page says what that means: no public window on the distance squad, and a fair thing to ask the coach about. Rutgers–Camden is the only one whose site links two accounts, a department handle in the navigation and a second in a rotating promo; the navigation one is kept. Pittsburgh's took a browser to see at all — its site renders the icon with JavaScript and points it at a click-tracking redirect rather than at Instagram, so @pitt_athletics is invisible to a plain fetch and appeared only in a rendered copy of the page.

Two schools link none, and their pages now say exactly that. George Mason publishes Instagram accounts for ten of its sports and leaves the cross country slot deliberately blank — the navigation entry is there with no address behind it. Emerson links not one Instagram account anywhere on its athletics site: its homepage has an Instagram feed section whose account is configured as an empty string, so the section renders nothing. Both were checked with a rendered copy of the page as well as a fetched one, because Pittsburgh had just shown what JavaScript can hide. With those two recorded, an empty Instagram cell on this site now means the same thing everywhere: the school's own pages link none.

6a. Known gaps in this data

  1. 175 of the 186 schools on the board have a cross country result — down from 93 unmeasured, which was the largest gap on this site for most of its life. All 12 schools on the cut list rest on a 2025 championship result too. Six rows are still open, and the eight-metro sweep is why: it added every one of them (Thomas Jefferson, Rosemont, Gallaudet, Chatham, Bryant & Stratton and Carolina University), most being small privates whose TFRRS page holds no 2025 championship. Four more used to be counted here — Marymount, Carlow, Hilbert and Shorter — and they were miscounted rather than unmeasured: each ran a conference championship with one to three men, which is too short a field to average but is itself a measurement of the squad, so all four now read Caution (the three the ladder could not run on, and Shorter, whose result the name map had been hiding). Shorter was the oldest row on that list and is the one that turned out to be unmeasurable rather than unmeasured: its last result is from October 2024, and it entered three men at its only 2025 meet who all did not start. Two other rows that were open have since left the board for reasons unconnected to measurement: Calumet College failed the both-sports rule (§6b) and South Carolina turned out to sponsor no men's cross country at all.
  2. Depth is measured, but the tier still comes from championship races only. 303 of the 904 races on file are conference championships, NCAA regionals or national championships, because those are the ones where a program fields its actual seven — and §3a measures what the alternative would cost: the invitationals run a median 14.5 seconds kinder. The consequence is that the median school's tier rests on two scoring-five championship races, 49 of the 148 rest on one or none, and the course is usually uncorrected. 40 of the measured programs have a further problem on top of that: no 7th man to compare against at all.
  3. How the candidate lists were built, and why the earlier version of this claim was false. An earlier README said this board listed every school inside each radius that sponsors men's cross country. It did not. The lists were originally assembled from general knowledge and then verified individually, which validates what is present and says nothing about what is missing — and about 45 schools were missing from Greenville alone, including one 25 driving miles from home. The lists are now built the other way round: conference by conference, from each conference's own men's-sponsored-sports table. That sweep covered every NCAA D1, D2 and D3 conference with a member inside a ring, and all of it is on the board: a Division-1-only rule was applied and then reversed (§6c). The guarantee is materially stronger than the old one, but it inherits the conferences' errors — a stale sponsored-sports table is wrong in both directions, and each school's sponsorship was not re-confirmed on the school's own athletics site.
  4. NAIA and NCCAA coverage is incomplete and known to be — and with the Division 1 rule reversed (§6c) this is a live gap again. The sweep was built from NCAA conference sponsored-sports tables, so the four NAIA rows on this site are only the non-NCAA programs it happened to run into. At least these NAIA and NCCAA programs in the Greenville footprint were never checked — Toccoa Falls, Truett McConnell, Brenau, Columbia International, Columbia College SC, Milligan, Johnson University, Reinhardt, Voorhees, Tennessee Wesleyan, Morris College, Bryan College, Bluefield University, Point University, Life University, Spartanburg Methodist and Warren Wilson. NAIA allows 12 scholarships for men's XC and track and has looser eligibility rules, so this gap sits in exactly the place where money is easiest to find. It is the largest known hole in the candidate list.
  5. Greenville and the eight new metros carry real routes; New York and Chicago do not. The Mi column for every school in the Greenville ring is a road distance returned by OSRM driving directions from the search center and rounded to the mile, and so is every distance in Boston, Philadelphia, Washington, Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Buffalo and Hampton Roads — the eight-metro sweep routed each candidate from each nearby centre, which is also how a school ends up with a miBy entry for two metros. Its eighth centre was downtown Newark, and although that page has been retired its twelve surviving routes are the reason the New Jersey rows still carry a second, usually shorter, distance. Greenville's used to be an estimate — straight-line distance × 1.18, or × 1.35 through the mountain states — and that estimate was off by as much as 54 miles, which is the difference between inside and outside a ring. New York and Chicago mileages are still the looked-up or estimated figures the board started with; at a 20-mile radius the error cannot move a school across the line, so they have not been re-routed. One New York figure was routed, and it decided a school: Drew sat on the Newark ring at 16.2 miles, and folding that page in meant measuring it from Midtown for the first time — 29.6 miles, against a 20-mile radius, so it stayed on the cut list rather than becoming the one row outside its ring. The estimate on that cut list had said 30 for as long as it existed. Every row on every metro page is inside its own rule, with no exception to explain.
  6. The both-sports check is a two-signal check, not a third-party dataset. No public file records which schools sponsor men's outdoor track, so §6b's twenty-nine removals — Mercer, Tennessee Tech, Jacksonville State, Temple, George Washington, Loyola Maryland and Canisius in Division 1, plus twenty-two D2, D3, NAIA and NCCAA schools — rest on two independent signals per school rather than on an authority. Both can be wrong the same way: a school that publishes a stale sport list and a women's-only track roster and sent no men to its conference championships — a program suspended for one season, say — would read as no-track when it is not. That failure mode is why each removed school keeps its full record and its page rather than being deleted, and why the reversal is one line of data away.
  7. Only two of roughly fifty championship courses have a difficulty correction. The championship races on file span 62 distinct conference, regional and national meets — 194 counting the invitationals — and exactly two, the South Atlantic and CCIW championships, had enough teams racing both a conference meet and a regional to isolate the course from the team (§3a). Everywhere else the raw time is used. That is the largest remaining source of error on the board, and it is worst for schools whose only result came from a single uncorrected course: those could move a tier once a second race exists.
  8. Meet schedules now cover 227 of the 243 rows with a page, and the venue is read off each meet's own results page. That is every school on the board but one, every school on a cut list, and every no-track removal but one. 6b. The both-sports rule — cross country and track

This board was built on men's cross country, because cross country championship results are where team depth is visible. That let in a category of school it should not have: programs that sponsor men's cross country and no men's outdoor track. He wants to run both seasons, and a program that offers one cannot give him the other, so twenty-nine schools came off the board under this rule — seventeen in the original pass and twelve more since, eight of them in the eight new metros, where it took Temple, George Washington, Loyola Maryland and Canisius, four Division 1 programs, at once. The other four turned up on a recheck of the Greenville and Chicago rings. It is the only rule on the site that removes a school for a reason that has nothing to do with how fast anybody runs.

No public dataset answers the question, which is why this took two signals per school rather than one lookup:

  1. The school's own sport list. Every athletics site publishes the sports it sponsors. A men's cross country page with no men's track and field page beside it — and, at Mercer, Tennessee Tech, Jacksonville State, Winston-Salem State and Clark Atlanta, a women's track and field page, or a single "track and field" roster that is entirely women, with no men's equivalent — is the school stating it directly.
  2. TFRRS, for the school's men at their conference indoor and outdoor championships. This is the second signal, and it is deliberately narrower than the test used at first. A single open-meet mark is not evidence of a program: men on a cross country roster enter April invitationals in the 1500 and the 5000 whether or not the school sponsors men's track, and Tennessee Tech is exactly that case. A sponsored program has to appear at its own conference championship. Absence from every conference championship, indoor and outdoor, is the signal.

Both had to agree. Two schools were checked and kept on exactly this basis: Fayetteville State and Allen University both sponsor men's outdoor track, and Allen simply fields no distance events — a real answer to a different question, and one for its coach rather than a reason to remove the school. Where the two signals disagreed, the school stayed.

The removed schools keep their pages, their tiers and their numbers

Every one of the twenty-nine still has a school page, still shows its cross country measurement, and still shows the tier that measurement earned — because the numbers are true and it is the rule, not the running, that removed them. Mercer is the expensive case: measured on cross country it was target tier, their 2nd-to-3rd man, 196 miles from home, with a verified CS degree. If a cross-country-only program ever becomes acceptable, that page is the first one to reopen, and it says so at the top.

Widening the Greenville ring to 300 miles then produced two more Division 1 cases of the same shape, and one of them is nearly as expensive as Mercer. Tennessee Tech measured target at their 5th man, 270 miles out, at about $18,446 on his residency — the better value of the two — and it is the row that forced the narrower second signal, since its men do post open-meet 1500s and 5000s and appear at no OVC championship. Jacksonville State measured deep, so it costs least to lose; its head coach's title reads "Cross Country & Track and Field," which is the trap on that row, because the track half of the job is the women's team.

Ten of the twenty-nine are in New York, which is a fact about the metro rather than a coincidence: small private and CUNY colleges there routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop. Six are around Greenville — including all three Division 1 cases, which are the expensive ones — and one is in Chicago.

6c. The Division 1 rule — tried, and reversed

The board is a sweep of every NCAA Division 1, 2 and 3 school inside each ring that sponsors men's cross country, plus the NAIA programs the sweep happened to find. For one version it was narrowed to Division 1 only. That rule has been reversed, and this section is kept because the reasoning for it was sound, the price was not obvious until it was paid, and somebody reading this board a year from now will have the same idea.

The rule removed 96 schools in one line — 52 D2, 40 D3 and 4 NAIA. It was implemented as an archive rather than a deletion, so nothing had to be re-measured to undo it: all 96 are now back in SCHOOLS, REMOVED, NO_TRACK and NO_PROGRAM with the slot, gap, spread, tier, coach and reasoning they always had. Division is a column and a filter on every table on this site, so the D1-only view is still one click away; it is no longer the only view.

Where the 96 sat before the rule, which is also where they sit again now:

  • 76 were live candidates — tier and all. These are the rows the rule actually cost him.
  • 1 was already cut on its roster times — Wingate, which was a walk-on prospect at the time and is since back on the board at deep (the cut line).
  • 14 had already failed the both-sports rule — cross country without men's outdoor track (§6b).
  • 5 had no men's program at all and were on the board in error.
What the rule cost, and why it was reversed: thirteen target-tier schools, and both extremes of the board

Thirteen of the 76 were at target tier — inside a scoring seven, which is the entire point of this exercise — and all thirteen are in the Greenville ring. Two of them were the extreme points of the entire project:

Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, is the closest target-tier school anywhere on the board and the most carefully checked row on the site: their 7th man, six seconds inside the scoring seven, on a course correction verified three ways (§3b). Fayetteville State is the cheapest, at a $7,892 federal net price with a verified CS degree, where he would be their 4th-to-5th man. Both are Division 2 — the division that can actually fund a distance runner.

The rest of the thirteen: Mars Hill (79 mi), Augusta University (115), Tusculum (121), Pfeiffer (138), Catawba (141), Emory (141, and one of the strongest CS departments on any version of this board), Emory & Henry (181), Lincoln Memorial (184), UVA Wise (195), Berry College (204) and UNC Pembroke (214).

The case for the rule, recorded honestly, because two of its three arguments were real. First, a uniform list: 44 programs that can all offer athletic aid, all measured against the same kind of championship, is easier to reason about than 185 across five divisions. Second, squad depth: the D1-only board had no program below five finishers, where the full board has six that could not field the scoring five at all (Roosevelt, Benedict, York College (CUNY), Marymount, Carlow and Hilbert — §5). Third, and this one was an illusion: the median team's fastest 1500 improved from 3:58.8 to 3:48.2 and the number of programs where he would arrive as their #1 fell from 57 in 128 to 8 in 57. That is not the board getting better, it is the sample being selected for programs that recruit milers — the same error as judging a program on one fast 5000, one level up.

The case against, which won. Of the 96, 40 are Division 3 and cannot offer athletic money at all — so on the scholarship axis those rows were never going to beat a partial D1 or D2 offer, and cutting them cost little. The 52 Division 2 programs are a different matter: D2 splits the same 12.6 equivalencies across a much smaller squad, which makes a real partial offer likelier there than in D1, and ten of those D2 rows are at target tier. The rule spent the cheapest school on the board, the closest target-tier school on the board, and the ten places where he would have counted for the team soonest, in exchange for a shorter list. That is the wrong trade for this athlete, and the reversal is the finding.

7. How the 1500 comparison is built

§1b converts his 1600 to a 4:01 1500 and compares it to each team's single fastest 1500 runner. That is a floor with all the weaknesses of any one-athlete number. This section is what replaced it: for all 195 schools with a 2026 field on file, the entire 1500 field at that program's 2026 outdoor conference championship, with his projection dropped into it on time, plus the program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. 59 conference championship result pages, one per conference, were read, plus seven postseason result pages carrying 33 programs' runners. The last fourteen rows of the original pass were added when #5 was closed: every school on the board that has a 1500 mark on file now has a field to drop him into, and tools/render-check.js asserts it, because the fourteen that were missing had been the fourteen with the fastest 1500s — a gap that quietly flattered him. The last of them was South Carolina, which sits in the no-men's-program list because it sponsors no men's cross country: the 1500 passes had skipped an SEC track program whose best man ran 3:35.07. #3 then did the same for the eight new metros — 60 more fields, read from 37 conference meets, 19 of which no earlier pass had needed — which leaves Carlow and Hilbert as the only rows on the board with no section at all, because neither posted an outdoor track result in 2026.

Four numbers come out of it, and they answer different questions:

  • His slot on their season depth chart. Every man who ran a 1500 for that program outdoors in 2026, best mark each, with his projection inserted. This is the 1500's equivalent of "his slot in their 7" and it is the closest thing to a depth measurement the event offers. Read it against the 10–15 second band in §1b, not the 5000's 40–60.
  • His place in the whole conference field. Every man who ran the event at that meet — prelims included, best time per athlete, merged across sections — ranked on time. This is the headline number on each card, and the field size is quoted with him in it: 34 men ran, so he is one of 35.
  • Whether he would have made the final. The slowest man in the final, minus his projection. Positive means he gets in on this year's marks.
  • Where he would have placed among that team's own entrants at the meet, which is often a much smaller number than the depth chart because most programs enter two or three men in a championship 1500.
Why the whole-field placing is the honest headline, and the final's placing is not

Championship 1500 finals are frequently tactical: the field jogs the first 1000 and sprints the last 300, and the winning time is slower than the prelims that fed it. The Atlantic 10 final was won in 4:03.61 while a prelim went 3:47.69. Dropping a 4:01 into that final produces "would have won it," which is nonsense — nobody races a tactical final on time. So every card leads with his placing among everyone who ran the event at the meet, prelims included, and flags a tactical final explicitly wherever the final's winning time is slower than the fastest round.

The same caution applies in the other direction. A program that enters nobody in its conference 1500 is not weak at it by that fact alone — it may simply race the 800 and the 5000 — but 43 of the 195 did exactly that, and nine of those ran no 1500 anywhere all season. Each card says so plainly and suggests the question rather than assigning a verdict.

"Regional" means different things by division, and the pages say which. Division 1 has a real qualifying round above the conference meet — the NCAA East and West First Rounds — so a runner can appear there. Divisions 2 and 3 have no regional round in outdoor track at all: the conference meet feeds the national championship on descending-order marks, so a fast time anywhere counts and there is no round to miss. The NAIA is the same. 33 of the 195 programs had a 1500 runner in a postseason round of any kind, which is the honest scale of the thing: getting past the conference meet in this event is rare — and ten of those arrived with the fourteen rows #5 added, which is what the missing fields were hiding.

The decathlon's 1500 is excluded, and it was not excluded at first

TFRRS files the 1500 run at the end of a decathlon as a second "1500 Meters" event page at the same meet: no scoring column, seven to twenty men, and a winning time 40 to 90 seconds off the open race. The first build of this section merged both pages, because both are honestly labelled "Men's 1500 Meters" at that meet. The effect was to inflate every field size and to drop multi-eventers into teams' entrant lists — High Point appeared to have taken a 1500 runner to the NCAA Division 1 championships when what it took was a decathlete who ran 4:31.47 on the second day, and the Big South field read as 28 men rather than 21.

The first rule was: at each meet, keep only the 1500 page with the fastest winning time; any other 1500 page at that meet is the multi-event round. That removed 23 pages, cut the postseason cards from fifteen to nine of the programs then on file, and moved four programs into the "entered nobody" column because their only entrants had been decathletes. It changed no placing — every decathlon 1500 on file was slower than 4:01, so his rank was never affected, only the size of the field he was ranked in.

That rule is right at a championship and wrong at an invitational, which is what #3 found when it applied the same filter to 157 meets instead of 46: a meet that scores nothing has an unscored open 1500 too, and "fastest winner" cannot separate two races when the field that showed up to the open 1500 at a small April invitational is slower than a good decathlete. The exact test is the meet's own event list: a TFRRS meet page links every event the meet actually held, and the decathlon's internal 1500 is never one of them — the decathlon is listed as the decathlon. So a 1500 mark whose event id is missing from its meet's published 1500 list is a decathlon leg, with no reference to how fast anybody ran. Applying it to the eight new metros dropped 51 marks at 29 events, and every one of those 29 meets does list a decathlon — the check that the test is not throwing away real races. It also corrected a row already on the board: Shorter's only two 1500 marks, at Berry Field Day and at its own conference meet, are both decathlon legs, so the program raced no open 1500 in 2026 and its depth chart is empty rather than one man deep.

The trap does not stay caught by itself. Three of the fourteen rows #5 added arrived carrying decathlon marks — Duke had three decathletes in its depth count and Virginia Tech one, both from the ACC meet, and Georgia's man at the national championships was a decathlete running 4:07.25 on his second day, which is also why he briefly appeared to have reached a national final. Applying the rule dropped Duke from 13 men to 12 and Virginia Tech from 14 to 13, and moved Georgia's fourth man to the 4:07.64 he ran at an uncached April invitational. As predicted, it moved no slot.

Individual athletes are not named anywhere in this data, on the same basis as the cross country results (§6): times are what the comparison needs, identities are not. Names were used transiently to de-duplicate one man's several marks and then discarded. Every card links the TFRRS results page it was read from, so any figure can be checked at source.

8. The maps, and what the two distance numbers mean

Each metro page plots its schools on a Leaflet map with OpenStreetMap data. Two distances are in play and they are not the same measurement:

  • The "Mi" column in every table is approximate driving distance from the metro center — the number that matters for how often he gets home.
  • The dashed outline on each map is a straight-line boundary, because that is the only thing a drawn shape can be.

Driving distance is always the larger of the two, so a school sitting right on the line can plot just outside the boundary. On the New York map the boundary is not a circle at all, because the rule is not a radius: it is 20 miles from Midtown plus all of Nassau and Suffolk County regardless of distance. The map draws that rule literally — a 20-mile circle around Midtown unioned with a traced outline of the two Long Island counties — so Adelphi, Molloy, SUNY Old Westbury, Farmingdale State, St. Joseph's Long Island and Stony Brook plot inside the shape despite being well past 20 miles, as does Hofstra on the cut list. Long Island is included because it is a commutable corridor; Westchester and central New Jersey are excluded at the same distance because they are a different decision, and the shape shows that asymmetry rather than hiding it behind a caption. The Long Island outline is a simplified county boundary, not a coastline: it follows the Queens–Nassau line, runs offshore of the barrier beaches, and closes across Gardiners Bay, which correctly encloses both forks, Shelter Island and Plum Island. An earlier version of this map drew a 20-mile circle centred on a framing point out in Long Island Sound rather than on Midtown, which put the ring around open water.

Campus coordinates are approximate main-campus points, accurate to roughly a kilometre — enough to place a pin, not a routing engine. Every one was checked programmatically against its recorded driving distance; a coordinate implying a straight-line distance greater than the recorded road distance is impossible, and that check is what surfaced two bad pins in the first pass. It cannot be run on the 73 schools added at the wider radii, because their mileage is derived from the coordinate rather than measured independently. Where two schools sit within about a mile of each other — Concordia and Dominican in River Forest, NYU and Baruch, LIU and St. Joseph's in Brooklyn, Manhattan College and Mount Saint Vincent in Riverdale, and now several of the CUNY campuses — the pins are nudged roughly a mile apart so both stay clickable, and each popup says so. On the 300-mile Greenville map a pin covers several miles of ground at the default zoom, so the Atlanta, Charlotte, Columbia and Charleston clusters overlap without being nudged; zoom in to separate them.

9. Each school's own page, and the meet maps

Every school on the board has a page of its own, reached by clicking its name in any table or its pin on any map. It carries the federal cost and admissions record, the CS share, the full scoring seven from every cross country result on file with his projection dropped into place and each runner's class year beside his time — grouped into the 2025 championships the tier rests on, the 2026 season as it runs, and the 2025 invitationals behind a fold — a panel reading off how much of that seven is not graduating and how far he would be from the fastest man who stays, the 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with the same projection dropped into that (§7), the coach to email, and a map of every meet that team travelled to in 2025–26, read off its own TFRRS results page and colored and lettered by season: X for cross country, I for indoor track, O for outdoor. Every school with a page has that map except Calumet College, which has no result on file for the season. Each page also ends with an explicit list of what is verified and what is missing for that school, so a gap reads as a gap rather than as an absence of a problem.

The season letter is read off the events a meet actually held, not off its date. A meet page links a result for each event it ran, and some of those events exist in only one season, so the list settles what the date cannot: a warm-weather program will hold an outdoor meet in December, and the two seasons overlap for a week in March. Every one of the 503 track meets on the board dated before 1 April 2026 has been re-derived this way, from its own refetched page, which corrected fourteen in total. Twelve are 6–14 March outdoor meets a date rule had lettered I while they were running the 100, the 110 hurdles and the javelin. The other two land outside any plausible March cutoff and in opposite directions — an 8 November throws series that is outdoor, and a 4 December meet that is indoor — which is the argument against cutoffs. The 203 meets not refetched are all dated April to June, after the indoor season has ended.

Which events count had to be audited too, and most of the obvious markers did not survive. The list was derived rather than asserted: every event slug on 606 meet pages was cross-tabulated against two anchors that cannot be faked — a 60-metre race (no outdoor meet contests one) and a javelin or hammer (no arena does) — and a marker was kept only if it never once appeared on the wrong side of them. The mile, the distance medley and the 4×800 are all run outdoors at relay meets; the 1500 is run indoors at eight meets here; a fall throws series contests the weight throw next to the hammer; and Youngstown State's field house has a discus ring, which is the single page in the corpus where an indoor 60 and a discus appear together. What is left is narrow and exact: the 60 and 55 and their hurdles, the 500, 600 and 1000 indoors; the 100, the high hurdles, the 400 hurdles, the 4×100, the 10,000, the steeplechase, the javelin and the hammer outdoors; and the multis by gender, since the men's indoor multi is the heptathlon and the women's outdoor multi is the heptathlon as well. One meet in 503 — a December meet at an indoor banked track that ran nothing longer than a 200 and nothing but flat races — links no marker at all, and keeps its I on the strength of the facility.

9b. Campus setting — and why only half of it is here

The question behind this column was “which of these campuses are genuinely urban,” and the honest shape of the answer is two axes, not one score. Whether he can do errands without a car and whether he can train here are different questions, and a single urban-ness number would blend them into something that answers neither. A downtown campus with a 96 Walk Score can be a miserable place to run six miles; a rural campus with a 6 can have forty miles of trail out the back gate.

The errands axis is built and is on every school page and in the master table. The training axis is not. Park acreage, total soft-surface mileage, the largest connected stretch of it, signalised road crossings per mile, campus acreage and the nearest running track come from OpenStreetMap, and that pass runs at about 110 seconds a row — six hours for the board. It is issue #24 and 45 of the 243 rows are done. Until it lands, every page that shows the errands axis says in as many words that it is one axis of two, and the school-page completeness table lists the training axis as missing rather than leaving it out — a page that showed half a design without saying so would overstate what is known.

What each field is, and which are not what they look like

Three of these carry a trap, and all three had already produced a wrong answer before they were caught.

  • Walk Score and Bike Score are third-party numbers, reproduced with a link to the exact page they came from — not recomputed here, and not averaged with anything. Every school page links its own source page so the number can be checked against its source.
  • An absent Transit Score is not a zero. That site prints one only where the city publishes a transit feed it has ingested, so 121 of the 243 rows have none — Columbia among them, which has the 1 train at the door. Reading that score alone made 120 rows read as having no transit at all, and 48 of those 120 turned out to have named rail or bus lines on the very same page. So the named lines with their distances are the primary answer here and the score is only a summary. Where the score is absent the page says “no feed published” and lists the lines; where there are no lines either, that is a fact about the place — Covenant College on Lookout Mountain has neither.
  • The federal locale is the density measure, and it is the only one that covers every row. IPEDS LOCALE classifies the campus address twelve ways — city, suburb, town and rural, each large, midsize and small, or fringe, distant and remote. Town population is collected for the Greenville ring only, so it could not carry this. The locale column sorts most urban first.
  • “Its town” is not the Mi column. It is the Census place or minor civil division the campus sits in, with its land area and how far the campus is from its centre. Mi measures driving distance from home to the metro centre; this measures the campus's position inside its own town. A campus 6 miles from the centre of a 227-square-mile city is a different thing from one 6 miles from the centre of a 4-square-mile village.

A wrong address still returns a page, with somebody else's scores on it

This is the whole accuracy of the column. That site's lat/lng URL form is not merely imprecise — asked about MIT's own coordinate it answered about a village in Maharashtra. So the address form is the only usable one, and the address comes from the federal IPEDS record rather than a geocoder.

Nothing was stored unless the coordinate in the page's own map tiles landed near the campus coordinate this board already holds. Checking the place name instead would have been wrong in both directions and threw away ten good rows on the first pass: that site calls Manhattan College's address Bronx, York College's Queens, Daemen's Buffalo rather than Amherst, and Harvard's simply “this location.” Of the slugs tried for a row, the closest accepted answer wins rather than the first — taking the first made the column depend on which earlier attempt happened to fail, and two runs over the same rows disagreed on 25 of them.

Each row records how well its answer matches: 230 rows are scored on the campus's own street address; 12 are a nearby street, with the distance shown, because that geocoder would not take the exact address; and one — Erskine — is the town's own page, because every form of its address landed 78 miles away in Cayce. Each page says which of the three applies to it. Three rows needed an address by hand, each with its reason recorded in the tooling: DePaul, LIU and St. Joseph's Long Island, all cases where the federal address is the campus that holds the president and not the campus the team is on.

Two board coordinates turned out to be wrong and this check is what found them. Saint Xavier's pin sat 1.6 miles from its campus, in the wrong Chicago neighbourhood; St. Joseph's Long Island carried the Brooklyn coordinate of the unit ID it shares with St. Joseph's NY. Both were fixed against IPEDS and OpenStreetMap, and the second one proved its own Mi had been right all along while its pin pointed at the wrong borough.

10. A note on the colors

The four fit tiers each carry a distinct shape and a text label, never color alone. That is deliberate: green and red are separated by an OKLab ΔE of about 4 under deuteranopia, which is far too close to carry meaning on their own. The gap chart uses a blue-to-red diverging pair with direct numeric labels on every bar for the same reason. The three season colors on the meet maps are the only three that clear the all-pairs colorblind separation floors in both light and dark mode (worst pair ΔE 9.2 and 9.4), and each pin carries its season's letter as well. Every palette was checked with a validator rather than by eye.
