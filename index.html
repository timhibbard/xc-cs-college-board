Distance running + computer science

A recruiting board built around one number

Eleven metro areas, every NCAA college inside the radius — Division 1, 2, 3 and the NAIA programs the sweep found — that sponsors both men's cross country and men's outdoor track, ranked by the one question that predicts whether a distance runner develops: where would he finish inside this team's scoring seven? 175 of the 185 schools on the board have a real 2025 cross country result behind that answer — a version ago 93 were unmeasured guesses, and the ten that are still unmeasured say so. Every one of the 226 rows with a men's program now carries the name and email of the coach who would actually recruit him, read off that school's own staff directory. The 123 rows that predate the eight-metro sweep also carry their 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it; the 62 rows that sweep added do not have that yet, and their metro pages say so. A Division-1-only rule was tried on this board and reversed: division is a column and a filter here, not a gate, because D2 is where partial athletic aid actually lives and nine of the schools that rule removed are at target tier.

The rule this whole board is built on

Being 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best 5000 runner is the good outcome. That is a 5th-to-9th man as a freshman: he trains in a pack, gets pulled along, and improves. Arriving as the team's fastest runner is not a sign he is a star — it means the program is thin and there is nobody to train with. A first pass at this analysis had that backwards; the tiers below reflect the corrected framing.

Recommendations this board previously made and has withdrawn

Cross country results contradicted the track data in four places. Hofstra and Loyola Chicago were both recommended as among the best fits in their metros on the strength of a 14:2x outdoor 5000 — but each won its conference championship with a seven he would finish about 51 seconds outside. Both are still cut, and now for a stated reason rather than by default. Loyola's 10K regional puts him 37 seconds inside their seven in 8K-equivalent terms; Hofstra's reads 50.2 seconds outside, which is what its own conference 8K says, so only Loyola's two races actually disagree. A 10K still reads a median 26 seconds kinder at a team's 7th man, even after both distances were re-anchored, so the conference 8K is the sharper test and it is the one the cut rests on. The 15:40 has moved both of them inside the cut line on the wider average — Hofstra 17.3 seconds outside their 7th man across all three of its championships, Loyola 7.3 — but neither has been reinstated, because both still read 50-odd seconds outside on the conference 8K the cut rests on. The six cut rows the new mark pulled back inside the line, and which three of them clear the conference-8K test too. Wheaton College, called resolved and upgraded by an earlier version of this page, was the reverse error: at the D3 Midwest regional he would have been their #2, so it dropped to caution. Lewis University was the school an earlier page recommended widening the Chicago radius to reach; its roster data killed it, and the radius has since come back to 20 miles, which removes it anyway. The pattern is the same every time: a single fastest 5000 is a floor, not a measure of depth. Where the two disagree, the cross country result wins. How that comparison is built.

A fifth withdrawal is about the list rather than a school. This page used to claim it held every college inside each radius that sponsors men's cross country. That was false. Checking the Greenville ring conference by conference found 41 schools that had been inside the original 200 miles all along and never appeared — Southern Wesleyan is 27 miles from home. Sixteen more were missing around New York, ten of them CUNY. Ten schools also came off it for having no men's cross country team to join, and Trinity Christian is closing. Pushing the ring to 300 miles then added four more. The claim it makes now is the narrow one it can support: every NCAA school inside each ring that sponsors men's cross country, in any division, plus the NAIA programs the sweep happened to find.

And the rings themselves were the last false claim. Three metros were on this board because three metros had been searched, not because the search was complete. Sweeping every metro in range the same way found 81 colleges this board held no row for — eighty genuinely new, plus Montclair State, which was already here — and they are now seven new pages: Boston, Philadelphia, Washington, Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Buffalo and Hampton Roads. The sweep built an eighth, Newark, and it has since been retired: twelve of its thirteen rows were already on the New York page, so those twelve stayed and the page went, with the downtown-Newark mileage kept as a second reading of them. The thirteenth, Drew, is the one row the fold cost: routing it from Midtown for the first time returned 29.6 miles against a 20-mile radius, so it stayed on that page's cut list rather than become the only row on the board outside its own ring. Sixty-two of those rows went onto the board, two were cut on the measurement, twelve sponsor no men's track and three field no men's cross country. The list is now 240 rows, 185 of them on the board. Fifty-nine of those sixty-two arrived tiered by the published ladder rather than by hand — the other three are hand-tiered because they fielded too few men at a championship for the ladder to have anything to average, which is a measurement of a thin program, not a gap — and without coach contacts, track marks or a 1500 field — the same gap the Greenville additions had when they arrived, stated rather than hidden. The coach contacts have since been read, one staff directory at a time, so that part of the gap is closed; the track marks and the 1500 field are still open.

Those additions entered unmeasured, and measuring them changed the board. They have been run through the same 2025 championship results as everyone else, which took the target band from 12 schools to 32; the 300-mile ring, a fresh sweep of every 2025-26 schedule and a re-average over every championship each team ran brought it to 29, the new 15:40 5K moved it to 33, and the eight new metros have taken it to 47. The nearest of them is Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, where he would be their 7th man with 13 seconds to spare — the most carefully checked row on the site, and a D2 program, which is the division that can actually fund a distance runner. The lesson of the measuring itself has not changed: a tier assigned without a cross country result is a guess in either direction, which is why the ten rows that still carry one are labelled unmeasured rather than borderline.

Six schools were cut in error and are back on the board, three of them at the 15:40 mark. Clemson and Columbia were both removed on their track marks alone — a 14:36 and a 13:43 5000 read as out of reach. Their actual championship results say otherwise: he would have been Clemson's 8th man at the ACC meet and their 7th at the Southeast regional, and Columbia's #3 at Ivy Heps before slipping to 8th at the Northeast regional. Both are now at target — Clemson came up from deep at the 15:40 mark, and Columbia sits 5 miles from midtown. Georgia Tech is the third, and it is the one this board caught itself on: it was carried as a cut on reputation, and when the times were finally transcribed the average came to 25.5 seconds outside their seventh man — inside the 45-second cut line the site publishes. Keeping it cut would have meant the board disagreeing with its own rule, so it is back at deep. Cutting a school on one fast 5000 is the same mistake as recommending one on it.

The 15:40 brought back three more, all at deep, and one of them is seven miles from home. Tennessee is the softest of the SEC cuts: at the SEC championship, the one race where they ran a full squad, he is 21.3 seconds outside their seventh man, and only six finished the regional. Wingate is the strongest D2 distance program in the region and its South Atlantic seven — once that course's 96.5 second offset is removed — puts him 19.5 seconds inside their seventh as their 5th man, though the D2 regional and the national championship after it read 42 and 109 outside, so the average is 33 outside. Furman is the closest school to home anywhere on this board and the hardest call on it: 39.1 seconds outside the seven it won the Southern Conference with, 73 outside at the regional, and 48.1 averaged — three seconds the wrong side of the 45-second line. It is on the board by decision rather than by the rule, and the tier ladder is listed as disagreeing rather than quietly overruled. In the other direction the cross country data confirmed the cuts at Georgia, Wake Forest, UNC Chapel Hill and Duke — 63 to 105 seconds outside their sevens, and Georgia never finished one — which is what a working rule looks like: it changes some answers and leaves the rest alone.

And eleven schools have no men's team to join. North Georgia sat at Verify here and St. John's and South Carolina sat among the cuts; all three sponsor women's cross country only. There was never a men's program at any of them to verify or to cut, and South Carolina is the sharpest version of the error — it was cut for times that were never going to exist, and its own meet schedule proves it: 22 track meets in 2026 and not one cross country race. There are eleven such schools in all, listed below with what each does and does not sponsor.

The largest withdrawal is a rule, not a school. This board was built on men's cross country alone, and seventeen of its schools sponsor cross country without men's outdoor track. He wants to run both, so all seventeen came off — including Mercer, which was sitting at target as their 2nd-to-3rd man 196 miles from home, and Tennessee Tech, another target, which the 300-mile ring added and the rule removed in the same pass. All seventeen, with what they do and do not sponsor.

A sixth withdrawal is one this board made and then took back: for one version it was Division 1 only. That rule removed 96 schools in a single line — 52 D2, 40 D3 and 4 NAIA — of which 76 were live candidates and nine are at target tier at the 15:40 anchor: seven in the Greenville ring, plus the University of Chicago and NYU, which are the only target-tier schools their metros have that are not D1. It cost the closest target-tier school anywhere (Anderson (SC), 29 miles, D2) and the cheapest (Fayetteville State, a $7,892 federal net price, D2, where he would have been their 4th-to-5th man), and it reversed this board's own 1500 finding by selecting for the programs with the deepest miler. All 96 are back, in the same lists and with the same measurements they always had. The rule is recorded rather than deleted because the reasoning behind it was sound and the price was not obvious until it was paid. The rule, and what reversing it restored.

Four structural findings worth stating plainly

49 of the 175 measured programs never finished seven runners in 2025

A cross country team scores five, and the 7th man is the depth number this whole board is built on. At 49 programs the 7th man does not exist: they never got seven men to the finish of any championship race. Those pages compare him to the team's last finisher instead and say so. Another 27 had a short race and a full seven in another, which is usually a regional entry list rather than a roster problem — 73 of 175 have at least one championship race with fewer than seven finishers. The eight new metros made this worse, not better: all four Hampton Roads programs and five of Boston's sixteen never finished seven, while Pittsburgh is the one metro on the board where every measured program did. These counts are firmer than they were: the board now holds every comparable championship each team ran rather than the one or two read by hand, so “never finished seven” means never in up to four races.

Three programs could not field the scoring five at all. Roosevelt (four finishers), Benedict (three) and York College (CUNY) (four) did not reach five men at their own conference meet, and a cross country team that cannot put five across the line does not have a team score. Being the fastest man on a squad of four is the exact outcome this board exists to avoid, so those three carry the warning on their pages regardless of tier. This was the strongest argument for the Division 1 rule — all three are D2 or D3, and narrowing the board removed the case entirely. It was not a strong enough argument to give up the 96.

Eleven schools on these maps have no men's program to join

At half these programs he would be the fastest 1500 runner on the roster

Every school page carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Across the board the median program's fastest 1500 runner went 4:01.1 — a tenth of a second slower than his projection — and at 57 of the 114 programs with a 1500 mark on file he would be their #1. Read against the rule at the top of this page, that is a warning about half the board, and it is a different warning than cross country gives: a program can have seven deep milers and no distance depth, or the reverse.

This is also the finding the Division 1 rule inverted, which is worth knowing about the rule rather than about the 1500. On the D1-only board the median team's fastest 1500 was 3:49.7, eleven seconds faster than he projects, and he was #1 at only 8 of 44 — because filtering for Division 1 filters for programs that recruit milers. The number did not improve; the sample changed.

His 4:01 would have made the conference final at 79 of the 91 championships where his school entered anybody, and finished top eight in 50 of them. The twelve meets where he would have missed the final are the level marker worth knowing by name: ETSU, UNC Greensboro, Appalachian State, Coastal Carolina, Queens (Charlotte), West Georgia, NJIT, Western Carolina, Clemson, Georgia Tech, Auburn and Samford. NJIT and Queens are target-tier on cross country and out of reach in the 1500. And the event is not universally contested even where men's track is sponsored: 29 of the 120 with a 1500 on file entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference championship, including Elon, Seton Hall, Campbell, Chattanooga, College of Charleston, The Citadel, Presbyterian, Wofford and Fairleigh Dickinson. Two of those, Seton Hall and Elon, are schools where he would be the fastest 1500 runner on the roster.

Chicago is thin on its own merits, not because of the radius

This has now been tested in both directions. An earlier version of this page blamed the 20-mile radius and recommended widening it to 30 to reach Lewis University — "a real distance program, a CS degree, and D2 scholarship money." The radius was widened, Lewis's roster data reversed that conclusion (a team best 5000 of 13:45, a 95 second gap, the same band Furman and Wingate were cut in, before the 15:40 brought both back at deep), and the extra ten miles added four other schools and not one target-tier fit. The radius has since come back to 20 miles.

Through five versions of this page, DePaul was the only target-tier school in Chicago, and at the 15:40 mark the University of Chicago joined it — 7 driving miles out, their 6th-to-7th man and 12 seconds inside a scoring seven averaged over all three of their championships, though still 3 seconds off the back of their seven at the UAA meet itself, and D3, so no athletic money either way. That still leaves eight of the eleven schools in range where he would arrive as their #1, or where the squad is too small to say: the only other school with a man in front of him is UIC, at deep, their 6th man on an average that mixes an 8K and a 10K and disagrees with itself. The tighter ring had already cost North Central, a D3 national-championship program whose 7th man ran 30 and 10 seconds outside his projection at its two championships, and Purdue Northwest, the metro's only D2 scholarship program. Saint Xavier, NAIA and 13 miles out, is the last scholarship door in range, and he would arrive as its #1 too. Roosevelt, one mile from the Loop, was supposed to fill that gap and then its GLIAC result came in: four finishers, where five are needed to score. Nothing fills it.

What to do next

  1. Start with the 47 target-tier schools, in order of distance. The measurement job is all but done: 175 of the 185 schools on the board and every school on the cut list has a 2025 championship result behind its tier. The six still at ◆Verify — unmeasured, not borderline — are five rows from the new metros and Carolina University in the Greenville ring, all of which posted no championship result to read and several of which may not still field a team; each one says which on its page. Three others — Carlow, Marymount and Hilbert — left that list without a single new result being collected: a two-man conference championship field cannot be averaged but is still a measurement, so they read caution. Shorter left it the other way, by being fetched at last — this repo's name map had no entry for it, so it had never been asked for — and what came back was three men at a 2024 conference championship and no men's cross country result since, which reads caution for the same reason. The nearest target is Anderson (SC) at 29 miles, and the nearest Division 1 target is UNC Asheville at 64.
  2. Email the coaches, then follow the programs. Every row with a men's program now carries the coach's name, title, office phone and email, each read off that school's own staff directory and linked to it — the 76 rows the eight-metro sweep added were the last gap and are now filled. The program's Instagram is a column behind: the 123 rows that predate the sweep carry it — the team's own cross country / track account at 96 of them, the athletics department's at the 25 that link none of their own, and nothing at the two whose sites link none at all (Hunter College and Dominican University) — and the 62 rows the sweep put on the board have none yet. He can contact any coach in any division at any time; the NCAA restrictions only limit when coaches may initiate. Two schools on the board publish no address at all (West Georgia and Fayetteville State) and 67 publish no direct phone — use the recruit form there. Where the school lists no head coach, or only an interim or an assistant, the title says exactly that instead of guessing.
  3. Confirm the CS degree at every school marked Confirm — a BA in applied AI is not a BS in computer science. Eleven schools on the board grant no computer science degree at all, and one of those is target-tier on running: Queens (Charlotte). That is the hard failure — the running fits and the degree does not exist. Emory & Henry was the second until the §3b arithmetic was corrected and it fell back to Caution, which settles its CS question by making it moot again.
  4. Ask the thin-squad schools how many men are coming back. A 2025 result showing four or five finishers is one season of evidence, not a verdict; the coach knows whether the roster is rebuilding or collapsing.
  5. Ask about lab scheduling. Required CS labs collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Big schools run multiple sections; small ones may run one.
  6. Model net cost, not scholarship. D1 men's track shares 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across 35–45 athletes, so a partial offer is the realistic outcome and academic merit aid frequently beats it outright. D2 is where the money is easiest to find — 12.6 equivalencies across a much smaller squad, and 55 of the 185 schools here are D2, five of them at target tier. D3 offers no athletic aid at all, so at the 62 D3 schools the only levers are academic merit aid and the federal net price, which is why the CUNY colleges at $3,000–$4,500 are on this board on cost alone. The eight new metros are where that bites hardest: of the 62 rows they added, 34 are Division 3 and only 8 are Division 2. Five of the board's nine Division 3 targets came from that pass — MIT, Tufts, Haverford, Swarthmore and Carnegie Mellon — and none of them can offer a dollar of athletic money. Nor can the Ivy League: Harvard, Penn and Columbia are Division 1 and award need-based aid only.
  7. Ask every coach about the 1500 specifically. Sponsoring men's track is not the same as racing the metric mile: 29 of the 120 programs with a 1500 on file entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference meet, and the three schools the 15:40 reinstated have no 1500 record here at all yet — nor do the 62 rows the eight new metros added, so on those pages this question is unanswered rather than answered badly. The 1500 section on each school page names the meet and links the results, so the question can be asked with the evidence in hand.
