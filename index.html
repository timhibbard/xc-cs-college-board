Distance running + computer science

A recruiting board built around one number

Three metro areas, every NCAA Division 1 college inside the radius that sponsors both men's cross country and men's outdoor track, ranked by the one question that predicts whether a distance runner develops: where would he finish inside this team's scoring seven? Every school on the board has a real 2025 cross country result behind that answer — there are no unmeasured placeholders left, where a version ago there were 93. Every school also carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it, the name and email of the coach who would actually recruit him, and the program's Instagram. The board used to sweep D1, D2, D3 and a few NAIA programs; the non-Division-1 half of that sweep is not thrown away but archived whole, and each metro page states what the narrowing cost.

The rule this whole board is built on

Being 40 to 60 seconds behind a team's best 5000 runner is the good outcome. That is a 5th-to-9th man as a freshman: he trains in a pack, gets pulled along, and improves. Arriving as the team's fastest runner is not a sign he is a star — it means the program is thin and there is nobody to train with. A first pass at this analysis had that backwards; the tiers below reflect the corrected framing.

Recommendations this board previously made and has withdrawn

Cross country results contradicted the track data in four places. Hofstra and Loyola Chicago were both recommended as among the best fits in their metros on the strength of a 14:2x outdoor 5000 — but each won its conference championship with a seven he would finish about 75 seconds outside. Both are still cut, and both are Division 1, so both are still on this page. Wheaton College, called resolved and upgraded by an earlier version of this page, was the reverse error: at the D3 Midwest regional he would have been their #2, so it dropped to caution. Lewis University was the school an earlier page recommended widening the Chicago radius to reach; its roster data killed it. Those last two are D3 and D2 and now sit in the Division 1 archive, with the reversals recorded on their pages. The pattern is the same every time: a single fastest 5000 is a floor, not a measure of depth. Where the two disagree, the cross country result wins. How that comparison is built.

A fifth withdrawal is about the list rather than a school. This page used to claim it held every college inside each radius that sponsors men's cross country. That was false. Checking the Greenville ring conference by conference found 41 schools that had been inside the original 200 miles all along and never appeared — Southern Wesleyan is 27 miles from home. Sixteen more were missing around New York, ten of them CUNY. Ten schools also came off it for having no men's cross country team to join, and Trinity Christian is closing. That sweep is what makes the Division 1 rule below affordable: the wider list was built properly before it was narrowed, so the narrowing removes rows from a complete search rather than pruning a partial one.

Those additions entered unmeasured, and measuring them changed the board. They have been run through the same 2025 championship results as everyone else, which took the target band from 12 schools to 32 across all divisions — and then the Division 1 rule cut it back to 19, because thirteen of those targets were D2 or D3. Pushing the ring out to 300 miles has since added two more, Auburn and Samford, so the band stands at 21. Anderson (SC), 29 driving miles from home, was the nearest target on the board and is the single most expensive row the Division 1 rule removed; it is archived, not deleted, and its page still says he would have been their 7th man by six seconds. On the Division 1 board the nearest target is now UNC Asheville at 64 miles. The lesson of the measuring itself has not changed: a tier assigned without a cross country result is a guess in either direction, which is why no row on this page carries one any more.

Three schools were cut in error and are back on the board. Clemson and Columbia were both removed on their track marks alone — a 14:36 and a 13:43 5000 read as out of reach. Their actual championship results say otherwise: he would have been Clemson's 8th man at the ACC meet and their 7th at the Southeast regional, and Columbia's #5 at Ivy Heps before slipping to 8th at the Northeast regional. Clemson returns at deep and Columbia at target, 5 miles from midtown. Georgia Tech is the third, and it is the one this board caught itself on: it was carried as a cut on reputation, and when the times were finally transcribed the average came to 41.8 seconds outside their seventh man — inside the 45-second cut line the site publishes. Keeping it cut would have meant the board disagreeing with its own rule, so it is back at deep. Cutting a school on one fast 5000 is the same mistake as recommending one on it. In the other direction the cross country data confirmed the cuts at Tennessee, Georgia, Wake Forest, UNC Chapel Hill and Duke, which is what a working rule looks like: it changes some answers and leaves the rest alone.

And ten schools have no men's team to join. North Georgia sat at Verify here and St. John's and South Carolina sat among the cuts; all three sponsor women's cross country only. There was never a men's program at any of them to verify or to cut, and South Carolina is the sharpest version of the error — it was cut for times that were never going to exist, and its own meet schedule proves it: 22 track meets in 2026 and not one cross country race. Six of the ten are Division 1 and are listed below; the rest are in the archive.

The second-largest withdrawal is a rule, not a school. This board was built on men's cross country alone, and seventeen of its schools sponsor cross country without men's outdoor track. He wants to run both, so all seventeen came off — including Mercer, which was sitting at target with his 4th man 196 miles from home, and Tennessee Tech, another target, which the 300-mile ring added and the rule removed in the same pass; Jacksonville State is the third Division 1 case. All three, with what they do and do not sponsor; the other fourteen are non-Division-1 and are tabled once, in the archive on their metro pages.

And the largest withdrawal of all is also a rule: the board is Division 1 only. That removed 96 schools in one line — 52 D2, 40 D3 and 4 NAIA — of which 76 were live candidates and thirteen were at target tier. It is a decision about the search, not a finding about any school, so nothing is deleted: every archived row keeps its measurements, its coach, its contact details and its own page, and each metro page tables what the rule cost it. The one thing archived rows do not carry is an Instagram handle: that lookup was run for the Division 1 board only. Almost all of the cost is in Greenville; in New York and Chicago every archived live candidate but two would have had him arriving as their #1, which by the rule at the top of this page is a warning rather than a loss. The rule and its price, written out.

Cross country but no men's track

Seventeen schools in the sweep sponsor men's cross country — several were measured on it, two at target tier — and no men's outdoor track. He wants to run both, and a program that offers one cannot give him the other, so they came off the board as a group rather than one at a time. Each was checked twice: the sport list and published track roster the school puts out itself — two of these list a track and field page whose roster is entirely women — and then TFRRS for the school's men at their conference indoor and outdoor championships, where a sponsored program has to appear. Their cross country numbers are kept intact on their pages, because the numbers are true and the rule is what removed them. Three of the seventeen are Division 1, and two of those are the expensive ones: Mercer, at target tier, his 4th man, 196 driving miles from home, with a verified CS degree, and Tennessee Tech, also target, his 5th-to-6th man at 270 miles and the better value of the two. Jacksonville State is the third and the least painful, because it measured deep anyway. The other fourteen are D2, D3 or NAIA and so were removed twice over; they are tabled once, in the Division 1 archive on their metro pages, rather than counted against two different rules here. Ten of the seventeen are in New York, where small private and CUNY colleges routinely field cross country as the cheapest possible varsity sport and stop there. The rule, written out.

Four structural findings worth stating plainly

9 of the 44 Division 1 programs never finished seven runners in 2025 — and the Division 1 rule is why that number is not 37

A cross country team scores five, and the 7th man is the depth number this whole board is built on. At nine programs here the 7th man does not exist: they never got seven men to the finish of any championship race. Those pages compare him to the team's last finisher instead and say so. A further ten had one short race and a full seven in the other, which is usually a regional entry list rather than a roster problem — 19 of 44 in total have at least one race with fewer than seven finishers.

This is the one place where being Division 1 only is a straightforward improvement. Across the wider sweep the count was 37 programs with no seventh man, and three of those could not field the scoring five at all: Roosevelt, Benedict and York College (CUNY) finished three or four runners at their conference meet. All three are non-Division-1 and are archived. On the Division 1 board nothing is below five finishers, and the thinnest squads are Chicago State and Winthrop at five. Being the fastest man on a squad of four is the exact outcome this board exists to avoid, and the narrowed board no longer contains that case.

Six Division 1 schools on these maps have no men's program to join

The Division 1 rule reversed this board's 1500 finding outright

Every school page carries its 2026 conference-championship 1500 field with his projected 4:01 dropped into it on time, plus that program's own 1500 depth chart for the season. Across the wider all-divisions sweep the median program's fastest 1500 runner went 4:01.2 — two tenths slower than his projection — and at 57 of 110 programs he would have been their #1. On the Division 1 board the median team's fastest 1500 is 3:49.7, eleven seconds faster than he projects, and he would be their #1 at 8 of 44. That is the finding turned on its head, and it is the clearest argument for the rule: at four fifths of these programs there is somebody to chase in the metric mile.

The 1500 is still not the same question as cross country, which is the whole reason both are on the page. His 4:01 would have made the conference final at 29 of the 44 championships and finished top eight in it at 8. The fifteen meets where he would have missed the final are the level marker worth knowing by name: ETSU, UNC Greensboro, Appalachian State, Chattanooga, Coastal Carolina, Queens (Charlotte), The Citadel, West Georgia, NJIT, Western Carolina, Wofford, Clemson, Georgia Tech, Auburn and Samford. NJIT and Queens are target-tier on cross country and out of reach in the 1500. And the event is not universally contested even where men's track is sponsored: nine of the 44 entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference championship — Elon, Seton Hall, Campbell, Chattanooga, College of Charleston, The Citadel, Presbyterian, Wofford and Fairleigh Dickinson. Two of those, Seton Hall and Elon, are schools where he would be the fastest 1500 runner on the roster.

Chicago is thin on its own merits, not because of the radius — and the Division 1 rule leaves three schools

This has now been tested in both directions. An earlier version of this page blamed the 20-mile radius and recommended widening it to 30 to reach Lewis University — "a real distance program, a CS degree, and D2 scholarship money." The radius was widened, Lewis's roster data reversed that conclusion (a team best 5000 of 13:45, a 95 second gap, the same band that cut Furman and Wingate), and the extra ten miles added four other schools and not one target-tier fit. The radius has since come back to 20 miles.

Through all of that, DePaul has been the only target-tier school in Chicago, and the Division 1 rule has now reduced the metro to three schools: DePaul at target, UIC at deep, and Chicago State at caution on a five-man squad. The rule costs Chicago almost nothing it had not already lost, which is the honest way to put it: of the ten archived programs here, every one but the University of Chicago would have had him arriving as their #1. The University of Chicago is the exception worth naming — 7 driving miles out, 27 seconds outside their scoring seven, and D3, so no athletic money either way. The tighter ring had already cost North Central, a D3 national-championship program whose 7th man ran within about five seconds of his projection, and Purdue Northwest, the metro's only D2 scholarship program; Saint Xavier was the last scholarship door in range and it is NAIA. Roosevelt, one mile from the Loop, was supposed to fill that gap and then its GLIAC result came in: four finishers, where five are needed to score. Nothing fills it.

What to do next

  1. Start with the 21 target-tier schools, in order of distance. The measurement job is done: every school on the board and every school on the cut list has a 2025 championship result behind its tier, so the ◆Verify tier — unmeasured, not borderline — is now unused. The nearest target is UNC Asheville at 64 miles; the nearest anywhere before the Division 1 rule was Anderson (SC) at 29, and it is in the archive.
  2. Email the coaches, then follow the programs. Every school page carries the coach's name, title, office phone and email, read off the school's own staff directory, plus the program's Instagram — the team's own cross country / track account where one exists, and the athletics department's at the six schools that link none of their own. He can contact any coach in any division at any time; the NCAA restrictions only limit when coaches may initiate. One school on the board publishes no address (West Georgia) and eighteen publish no direct phone — use the recruit form there.
  3. Confirm the CS degree at every school marked Confirm — a BA in applied AI is not a BS in computer science. On the Division 1 board there is exactly one hard failure: Queens (Charlotte) is target-tier on running and grants no computer science degree at all.
  4. Ask the thin-squad schools how many men are coming back. A 2025 result showing four or five finishers is one season of evidence, not a verdict; the coach knows whether the roster is rebuilding or collapsing.
  5. Ask about lab scheduling. Required CS labs collide with a 3:00–5:30 practice window. Big schools run multiple sections; small ones may run one.
  6. Model net cost, not scholarship. D1 men's track shares 12.6 scholarship equivalencies across 35–45 athletes, so a partial offer is the realistic outcome and academic merit aid frequently beats it outright. This is also the axis on which the Division 1 rule costs the most: 52 of the 96 archived schools are D2 programs that can fund a distance runner and where he would frequently have been a scoring man rather than a squad member.
  7. Ask every coach about the 1500 specifically. Sponsoring men's track is not the same as racing the metric mile: nine of the 44 programs here entered nobody in the 1500 at their own conference meet. The 1500 section on each school page names the meet and links the results, so the question can be asked with the evidence in hand.
