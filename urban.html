Campus setting · 186 rows · one order

Urban

The campus setting has been on this board for a version, one column at a time: a Walk Score here, a federal locale there, the rail and bus lines on each school page. Read one row at a time, which was the only way it could be read, it never answered the question it was collected for. This page puts all 186 board rows in a single order — and then says, four times over, what that order is good for and what it is not.

It is not a ranking of the running, the program or the fit. It answers one question: how much of daily life is within walking distance — a shop, a bus, a train, a job, a lab, all reachable on foot and without waiting on somebody for a ride. It has nothing to say about where a team puts its tempo runs. The fit tier stays in its own column, and the most useful row in the table is one where the two disagree.

How the score is built

One number per campus, out of 100, the average of three equal thirds:

Each third is a different kind of evidence about the same address, which is why none of them is allowed to speak for the other two.
ThirdWhat it isThe catch
Walk Walk Score's published 0–100 for the campus's own street address, used as it stands. Third-party and not recomputed here. It describes a few blocks, not a town.
Density The federal locale: twelve codes from City: Large down to Rural: Remote, spread evenly across 100. An order only. IPEDS makes no claim that a large city is nine points denser than a midsize one, so this is a rank wearing a number's clothes.
Transit The named rail and bus lines at that address: 35 points for a rail stop and 35 for a bus stop, each fading to nothing at a mile and a half, plus two a line for how many lines there are. Computed here from the listings, not read off Walk Score's Transit Score — see below.

Equal thirds because nothing on this board justifies anything else. Any other weighting would be a claim about how much a train is worth against a shop, and the only evidence for that claim is one 17-year-old's preference, which has not been asked for. Three equal thirds is the assumption that is easiest to see and easiest to overrule. The formula and its checks in full, and what the setting columns do and do not mean.

The component that is shown here and never scored

Walk Score publishes a Transit Score, and it was the obvious third. It would have been wrong. That site prints one only where the city publishes a transit feed it has ingested, so it exists for 122 of the 243 rows and an absent one is not a zero — 48 of the rows without one have named rail or bus lines at the door. A component that read it would have scored Columbia, with the 1 train outside, as having no transit at all. The line listings cover every row, so they are the component, and the published score sits in its own column as a check.

Over the 122 rows that have both, the two agree: Spearman 0.80, a median gap of 9 points, 91 of the 122 within 15 points, and a mean bias of +2.6. Both halves of the rule earn their place: a distance-only version — 50 points a stop, no count term — agrees at only 0.70, and a count-only version at 5 points a line runs 17 points low, because twelve bus lines two miles away is not a bus route.

Four things this order shows that the main table hides

The board is bimodal, not spread

Scores run from 8 to 100 with a median of 59, but the middle is thin: 68 of the 186 schools score 70 or better and 50 score under 40. Half the board is a city campus and a third of it is a small town, and there is not much in between to compromise on. By metro the split is the whole story — the Greenville ring's median is 38, against 83 in Boston, 80 in New York, 80 in Pittsburgh and 79 in Chicago. The ring is where the depth is and the cities are where the walking is, and that is the trade this page exists to make legible.

The most urban schools are mostly the ones he should not go to

Seven of the ten most urban rows are Caution tier, which is what happens when a city campus runs a thin squad: Roosevelt, Suffolk and Thomas Jefferson all score 100 and all three are on this board as warnings — Roosevelt finished four runners at its own conference meet. Urban is not a tiebreaker to apply before the running; it is one to apply after. Sort this table by Fit and the point makes itself.

Thirteen of the 47 target-tier schools are in the most urban quarter anyway

The short list this page exists to produce — the rows where both answers are good: NYU 99, Harvard 96, Fordham 95, Penn 95, NJIT 94, MIT 93, DePaul 92, La Salle 87. Every one is a school where he would land inside a scoring seven and could get through a week on foot. At the other end, Pfeiffer is target tier and the least urban row on the board at 8 — a Walk Score of 7, a village of 1.6 square miles, no bus. That is not an argument against Pfeiffer; it is the thing to have looked at before visiting.

Walk Score and the locale disagree in both directions, and both disagreements are real

Chestnut Hill sits inside Philadelphia — City: Large, two rail lines, three bus lines — with a Walk Score of 4, because its own corner of Germantown Avenue has nothing on it. Appalachian State is a distant town in the mountains and scores 74, because Boone's downtown starts at the campus edge. One is a city address with nothing in walking distance; the other is a small town that is all walking distance. That is why the locale is a third of the score rather than the whole of it, and why both components are printed next to the total instead of behind it.

Every school, most urban first

Click any header to re-sort. # is the urban rank and does not change when you sort by something else, so a school keeps its position in this order while you read the table in another. Density is the federal locale as a number, used as an order only — the twelve codes are evenly spaced because nothing says a large city is a fixed distance denser than a midsize one. Transit is computed from the rail and bus lines beside it; the Transit Score column is Walk Score's own, shown as a check and never folded into the total, because it is published for only 122 of the 243 rows on file and an absent score is not a zero. Mi is driving miles to the row's own metro centre. Bike is Walk Score's Bike Score where it exists.

61 of the 186 schools have no rail line and no bus line listed at their address. That is what the page those scores came from named, not a survey of the town: Appalachian State has no line listed and a Walk Score of 74. Where a row says none listed, treat it as a question for the school rather than an answer about it — and where a line is listed, the school page names it and gives its distance.

What to do with this

  1. Read it after the fit, never before it. Sort by Fit, then by Urban inside the target band. Thirteen rows survive both sorts, and those are the visits worth the flight.
  2. Treat a low score in a city as a question, not a verdict. Chestnut Hill's 4 is a fact about one corner of one avenue. The locale beside it says the rest of Philadelphia is ten minutes away; what it cannot say is whether the campus shuttle goes there.
  3. Treat none listed as a question for the coach. 61 rows have no line on the page their scores came from. Some of those towns have a bus the site has never ingested, and a coach who recruits out of state will know which.
  4. Ignore the three 100s. They are the most urban rows on the board and three of the thinnest programs on it, which is the whole reason this page is a tab and not a tier.
